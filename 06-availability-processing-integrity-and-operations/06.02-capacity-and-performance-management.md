## 1. Why capacity is an availability control and not a finance exercise

Capacity management is the least dramatic chapter in this phase and it is the one that most often prevents
the dramatic ones. A1.1 asks whether the entity maintains, monitors and evaluates current processing
capacity and use of system components, and manages capacity to meet its objectives. It is a criterion about
**foresight**: not whether the platform coped last night, but whether anybody knows what it will do under
the load it can already see coming.

At CloudNimbus the load is not smooth and is not random. It is a workforce platform, so its shape is the
shape of the working week and the payroll calendar: shift boundaries at the top of the hour across six US
time zones, a weekly spike where the largest cohort of customers closes its pay period, and a hard
month-end and quarter-end peak where the calculation engine, the export service and the reporting API all
want the same clusters at the same time. **A capacity practice that measures monthly averages against a
platform with this profile is measuring the wrong thing**, which is why `CNB-C-094` is written against
committed headroom and a forecast rather than against utilisation.

**A.8.6 capacity management is the Annex A control both capacity rows cite**, and it is worth naming what
the citation reaches. A.8.6 concerns the monitoring and tuning of resource use and the projection of future
requirements. It reaches the review and the forecast. It does not reach the 99.9% commitment those things
serve, which is SC-01's and A1.1's territory, and this chapter does not stretch it.

## 2. The estate this chapter covers, and the two resources it does not

`CNB-C-094` operates across **3 EKS clusters and 5 Aurora clusters** — the ones inside the SOC 2 system
boundary. `02.03` names the five Aurora clusters: `core-us-east`, `core-us-west`, `core-eu-central`,
`analytics-us-east` and `platform-metadata`.

The wider estate is **4 EKS clusters and 6 Aurora clusters**. One of each sits outside the system boundary,
and 02.03 draws that line: the sixth Aurora cluster is `staging-core`, which holds no production customer
data, and the fourth EKS cluster carries the non-production workloads that run against it. 04.02 §3 states
the general rule this row depends on — **a control's population is the ISMS boundary unless the row names
the system boundary** — and `CNB-C-094` names it.

The distinction is worth a sentence rather than a footnote because it is the sort of thing that reads like a
discrepancy. A reader who has the infrastructure figures from Phase 01 will see 4 and 6, will read 3 and 5
here, and is entitled to be told why before they start counting. **Reporting capacity across a cluster that
holds no production tenants would be reporting on a population no service auditor is sampling and no
customer is served by**, and it would inflate the estate the control claims to cover, which is the wrong
direction for an inflation to run.

## 3. `CNB-C-094` — the monthly review

The control requires a monthly comparison of the **90-day utilisation trend against committed headroom** for
each in-boundary cluster, and it requires that **a service forecast to exceed 70% of committed headroom
opens a capacity ticket against a named engineer**. Three reviews were performed in the quarter.

| Review | Date | Clusters reviewed | Capacity tickets opened |
|---|---|---|---|
| July 2026 | **2026-07-14** | 3 EKS · 5 Aurora | `CAP-07` |
| August 2026 | **2026-08-11** | 3 EKS · 5 Aurora | None |
| September 2026 | **2026-09-15** | 3 EKS · 5 Aurora | `CAP-09` |

The threshold is the part of the control worth defending. **Seventy per cent of committed headroom is a
forecast trigger, not a utilisation alarm**, and the two are frequently confused. A control that opened a
ticket when a cluster reached 70% utilisation would fire late, because by the time a system is at 70% the
lead time on reserved capacity, node group changes and an Aurora instance class change has already been
partly consumed. `CNB-C-094` fires when the ninety-day trend **projects** a crossing, which is what gives
the named engineer somewhere between two weeks and two months to act instead of a weekend.

It also means a capacity ticket is a normal outcome of a working control rather than a sign of trouble.
**Two tickets in a quarter is the control operating**, and a quarter with none would be the more interesting
result — either the platform's growth had stopped or the forecast had.

### 3.1 `CAP-07` — `cnb-ingest`, July

The July review forecast the **`cnb-ingest` node group at 73% of committed headroom**. `cnb-ingest` takes
the clock-in and clock-out traffic from the mobile clients, which is the most sharply peaked workload on the
platform: a shift boundary in the US Central time zone delivers a substantial fraction of a day's write
traffic inside about ninety seconds. The forecast reflected customer growth in the preceding quarter rather
than any change in the profile.

`CNB-C-094` requires the ticket to be opened **against a named engineer**, and naming him is part of the
evidence rather than a courtesy: **`CAP-07` was opened on 2026-07-14 against Wes Delacroix and closed on
2026-08-04 by adding two node groups.** A capacity ticket with no name on it is a note.

### 3.2 `CAP-09` — the `core-us-east` cluster, September

The September review forecast the **`core-us-east` Aurora cluster at 78% of committed headroom**, and the
honest thing to record about that number is that **it was informed by the restart load the 8 September
incident produced**. **`CAP-09` was opened on 2026-09-15 against Devon Ashby**, is **open at this vantage**,
and carries a re-sizing scheduled for 2026-10-08 — scheduled, not performed, and this document says nothing
about how it will go.

When the eleven services on the core write path were restarted in a rolling sequence beginning at 15:04 on 8
September, the reconnection and the backlog of retried writes put a load profile on the core cluster that no
ordinary day produces. That profile is in the ninety-day trend the September review read, and it moved the
forecast.

There are two ways to treat that, and this document takes the second. The first is to exclude the incident
window as an anomaly, on the argument that a capacity forecast should model normal operation. The second is
to leave it in, on the argument that **a recovery is a thing the platform has to have capacity for**, and a
forecast that excludes every abnormal day is a forecast for a platform that never has one. The restart load
was real, it will recur the next time the write path is restarted, and a capacity plan that cannot absorb
its own recovery is a capacity plan with a gap in exactly the place availability depends on it. The
exclusion was considered at the review and refused; `CAP-09` was raised on the unadjusted forecast.

## 4. `CNB-C-095` — the quarterly re-set, and the service that was held open

`CNB-C-095` requires that, each quarter, the **autoscaling policy and reserved capacity for every production
service are re-set from the capacity forecast and committed to the infrastructure repository**, and that
**a service whose recorded headroom is below 30% at the re-set is held open until it is corrected**.

The Q3 re-set was performed on **2026-09-24**. One service failed the bar: the **reporting API**, at
**26% recorded headroom** against the 30% floor. It was **held open** under the control and **corrected on
2026-09-29**, five days later.

That is the control working, and it is worth being precise about what "held open" means, because it is the
part of the row that carries the weight. It does not mean the finding was noted and the re-set was signed
off anyway with an action attached. It means **the quarterly re-set does not close while a service is below
the floor** — the artefact `CNB-C-095` produces stays in an open state, and it is visible as an open state
to anybody looking at the evidence class. A control that recorded the exception and completed on time would
produce a clean-looking record with a problem inside it. This one produces a record that is visibly not
finished until the problem is gone.

The 26% figure and the 30% floor are also a small illustration of why the two capacity controls are separate
rows. `CNB-C-094` is detective and monthly and works on a forecast; `CNB-C-095` is preventive and quarterly
and works on a **recorded** state at a point in time. The reporting API did not trip `CNB-C-094`'s
seventy-per-cent forecast trigger in July or August. It failed `CNB-C-095`'s floor in September on measured
headroom. Two controls, two mechanisms, and one of them caught what the other did not — which is the reason
A1.1 is served by more than one row.

## 5. The peak the quarter ends on, and why it is stated as a forecast

**2026-09-30 is the hardest day in the quarter for this platform**, because month-end and quarter-end
coincide: semi-monthly and monthly pay periods close on the same date as the quarterly accrual roll-forward,
and the reporting API takes the quarter-end load at the same time.

**It is also the vantage.** Phase 06 speaks as at 2026-09-30, which makes that day a plan rather than a
result, and the only figure this chapter can honestly carry for it is the one the September review
produced: a **forecast of 4.1× median write volume**, projected from the trailing three quarter-ends and
the ninety-day trend `CNB-C-094` read on 2026-09-15. Whether the platform absorbed it — whether a threshold
was crossed, a ticket opened, an autoscaling ceiling reached — is an outcome that falls on the vantage day
itself, and **it is reported in Phase 07**. A capacity chapter that claimed the result of its own closing
date would be doing the thing this portfolio wrote ADR-0015 to stop.

What can be said at the vantage is narrower and is still worth saying. The forecast was produced, the
quarterly re-set was completed against it on 2026-09-24, and the one service that failed the floor was
corrected on 2026-09-29, before the peak arrived. **That is the capacity practice operating, and it is not
evidence about connection pools, failover behaviour or the design of a synthetic probe.** The September
commitment failure began at 14:22 UTC, which is **mid-morning in every US time zone the platform serves**
rather than a quiet hour, and it was a connection-pool condition at the application tier rather than a
capacity condition. A chapter that let a good capacity record stand next to the September availability
number without saying so would be inviting a reader to net one against the other.

The forecast also carries a caveat about what a clean quarter-end would prove for the rest of the window,
whenever Phase 07 reports it. **September 30 is the
quarter-end peak; December 31 is the year-end peak**, and year-end at a workforce platform is a larger event
than quarter-end because annual leave accrual balances roll over in the same window as the final pay period
of the calendar year. The Q4 re-set under `CNB-C-095` falls before it. That is a scheduled control
occurrence and not a prediction, and this document says nothing about how it will go.

## 6. What this chapter establishes

| Proposition | Status |
|---|---|
| `CNB-C-094` operated in each of the three months of the quarter | **Evidenced** — 2026-07-14, 2026-08-11, 2026-09-15 |
| The forecast trigger fired where the control says it should | **Evidenced** — `CAP-07` at 73%, `CAP-09` at 78% |
| `CNB-C-095` operated for Q3 and enforced its floor | **Evidenced** — performed 2026-09-24, reporting API held open at 26%, corrected 2026-09-29 |
| Capacity was a contributing condition of the 8 September incident | **Not supported** — the incident was a connection-pool condition at the application tier; 06.05 sets out the mechanism |
| The controls operated effectively across the period | **Not available** — three months is half a period, and Q4 has not run |

Three occurrences of a monthly control and one of a quarterly control is what three months buys. The
observation window will produce six and two. **A quarterly control has a population of two in a six-month
window**, which is the sampling fact 04.11 established and the reason the Q3 re-set being complete and
evidenced matters more than a monthly occurrence does: at a population of two, one missing occurrence is
half the population.
