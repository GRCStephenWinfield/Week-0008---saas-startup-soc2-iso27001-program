## 1. The exercise, and what it was for

**On 2026-08-19, between 06:00 and 08:51 UTC, CloudNimbus failed the platform from `us-east-1` to
`us-west-2` and ran it there.** This was the annual disaster recovery exercise required by `CNB-C-100` under
**CAL-10**, and it is milestone **MS-11** on the programme roadmap.

| Objective | Target under SR-10 | Achieved | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recovery time objective | **4 hours** | **2 hours 51 minutes** | 1 hour 9 minutes |
| Recovery point objective | **15 minutes** | **4 minutes 12 seconds** | 10 minutes 48 seconds |

Both objectives were met with real margin, and that is the result the exercise produced. The rest of this
chapter is about what the result means, which is narrower than it looks and is the reason this document
exists rather than a one-page certificate.

`CNB-C-100` requires the exercise to fail the platform to `us-west-2`, **measure achieved recovery time and
recovery point against the 4-hour and 15-minute objectives, and record the result and any shortfall in the
exercise report**. The exercise report is **GOV-21**, accepted on **2026-08-24** under **DEC-601**, with its
six findings entered against named owners and dates at the point of acceptance rather than afterwards. The
control feeds **EC-11**, the continuity exercise record, whose sampling unit is one exercise: scenario,
participants, timeline, achieved recovery time and recovery point, findings and actions.

**The exercise ran inside an announced maintenance window, and that has to be said in the same breath as the
August availability figure.** The window was **06:00 to 10:00 UTC — four hours — notified to every customer
on 2026-08-05**, fourteen days ahead. **A window is a notified interval, not an achieved duration**: the
exercise ran 06:00 to 08:51 and finished sixty-nine minutes inside it. The master services agreement's
availability commitment **excludes maintenance windows announced at least fourteen days in advance**, and
what the exercise actually cost customers inside that window was **23 minutes** — **14** at the failover
cutover to `us-west-2`, of which DR-F1's four are the DNS overrun, and **9** at the read-path re-point on
failback into the same window. **Those 23 minutes are excluded from the August SC-01 measurement**, which is
how August is reported at 99.97% on thirteen counted minutes of unavailability against a measured
denominator of 44,617. `02.12` published SC-01 without stating the exclusion; it has been corrected at
source and **Phase 02 re-issued**, under **DEC-613** of 2026-09-24, Marisol Vega. EC-09's minutes-excluded
field now reads **July 4 · August 23 · September 0** — July's four being the `TP-20` delivery window of
2026-07-15, notified 2026-07-01 — and 06.01 §1.1 sets out why a commitment published without its exclusion
is incomplete under DC2 rather than merely untidy, and why **an exclusion engaged twice in three months is
not an edge case**.

## 2. What was failed over, and the region that was not

The exercise moved the whole `us-east-1` service: the EKS workloads for the in-boundary clusters, the Aurora
global-database promotion in `us-west-2`, the object-store replicas, the ingress and DNS records, and the
status page posture. It was a full failover rather than a tabletop, and **the platform served real
production traffic from `us-west-2` for 148 of the exercise's 171 minutes**. The other 23 are the two
cutover intervals — fourteen minutes at the failover and nine at the read-path re-point on failback — and
they are the only minutes of the exercise a customer could not use the platform. **That is what a successful
regional failover looks like**, and it is why the exclusion removes 23 minutes from August rather than the
whole window: an exclusion covers the window announced, and the measurement removes the minutes actually
lost inside it.

**`eu-central-1` was deliberately not failed over.** Obligation **O8** and commitment **SC-04** hold the
personal data of the 41 EU-residency customers at rest in `eu-central-1`, and `eu-central-1` therefore
recovers **intra-region across availability zones only**. Failing it to Oregon would have restored a service
by breaching a residency commitment, which is not a recovery — and running it as an exercise would have
moved EU personal data across a boundary the contract forbids, in order to rehearse something the contract
forbids.

**DR-F2 exists because the runbook did not say that in terms.** The runbook described a failover of the
production estate and left the exclusion of `eu-central-1` to be understood from the architecture rather
than stated as an instruction, which is an unsafe place to leave a contractual constraint at 06:00 on an
exercise morning. It was closed on 2026-08-28 by putting the prohibition in the runbook's opening step,
where somebody executing under pressure will read it.

**The consequence for the register is stated plainly: the exercise proves the recovery of two regions and
cannot prove the recovery of the third.** That is exactly **R-20** — the loss of `eu-central-1`, where O8
forbids the cross-region recovery that would restore it — and it is why R-20 sits at **2 × 5 = 10** and
cannot move. There is no exercise CloudNimbus can run that would reduce it, because the thing that would
reduce it is the thing the commitment prohibits. A risk retained for a contractual reason is not a risk
awaiting a control; it is a residual the register carries with its eyes open, and Phase 03 named it as
immovable in advance rather than discovering it here.

## 3. The six findings

An exercise that produces no findings has usually been run as a demonstration. This one produced six, of
which one is material.

| ID | Finding | Severity | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DR-F1** | The runbook assumed a 60-second DNS TTL; the record is published at 300, adding roughly four minutes to observed customer recovery | **Material** | Closed 2026-09-02 |
| DR-F2 | The runbook did not state in terms that `eu-central-1` must not be failed over | Moderate | Closed 2026-08-28 |
| DR-F3 | Aurora global-database promotion required a manual approval step absent from the runbook's critical path timing | Moderate | Closed 2026-09-11 |
| DR-F4 | The status page notice was drafted live rather than from a template | Minor | Closed 2026-09-04 |
| DR-F5 | Two service runbook links pointed at a retired wiki | Minor | Closed 2026-08-26 |
| **DR-F6** | Failback was not timed; it took place over the two following days unmeasured | Moderate | **Open** — scheduled against the Q4 game day, due 2026-11-30 |

**All five were closed by 2026-09-11**, DR-F3 last. **DR-F6 is open at this vantage.**

### 3.1 DR-F1, and why a four-minute finding is the material one

DR-F1 looks like the smallest number on the page and it is the finding that matters, for two reasons.

**The first is arithmetic that would not have stayed small.** The runbook's critical path was timed against
a 60-second DNS time-to-live; the record is published at 300 seconds. Customers resolving the old address
continued to do so for up to five minutes after the cutover the runbook considered complete, adding roughly
four minutes to **observed customer recovery** — which is the only recovery time a customer experiences.
Those four minutes are inside the fourteen-minute failover cutover interval §2 counts, so they are four of
the twenty-three excluded minutes rather than an addition to them. At
a 2h51m achieved recovery time against a 4-hour objective the four minutes are absorbed comfortably. In an
event that ran closer to the objective, an unmeasured four minutes sitting outside the timed path is exactly
the kind of margin that turns a met objective into a missed one, and it would have done so invisibly,
because the runbook's own clock stops before it.

**The second is that it is a documentation failure of the precise kind R-30 describes** — documented
information becoming out of date and being relied on as current. The runbook was not wrong when it was
written; the TTL was changed elsewhere, for a reason unrelated to disaster recovery, and no mechanism
connected the change to the document that depended on it. That is why the September register review
**declined** the proposal to reduce R-30 from **3 × 2 = 6 to 2 × 2 = 4**: nothing in this register is closed
or removed, so the proposal was a reduction rather than a closure, and DR-F1 is the event R-30 predicts,
occurring during a live exercise, which is evidence for the risk and not against it. 06.11 records that
reasoning.

DR-F3 is the same family with a different mechanism — a manual approval step that exists in the platform and
not in the runbook's critical path timing, so the measured path and the actual path had diverged. Both were
closed by amending the runbook and re-timing the path, and both are the reason the exercise is worth running
against a document rather than against people's memory of it.

### 3.2 DR-F6, and the half of the round trip that was not measured

**Failback was not timed.** The platform ran in `us-west-2` and returned to `us-east-1` over the following
two days, and that return happened without measurement.

The exercise's scope was recovery, and against its scope it succeeded. But an organisation that can fail
over in 2h51m and cannot say how long it takes to come home has measured half of a round trip. Failback has
its own risk profile — it is planned rather than forced, which makes it easier, and it involves reconciling
whatever was written in the recovery region back into the primary, which makes it harder. Two days of
unmeasured activity is not a recovery time objective breach, because SR-10's objective is about recovery.
It is a gap in what the exercise establishes, and DR-F6 records it as one rather than closing the report
without it.

DR-F6 is **open at this vantage**, owned, and scheduled against the Q4 in-region failover game day with a
due date of **2026-11-30**. That game day is `ACT-06-04` from the September incident and is **ADR-0030**; it
has not been performed and nothing here says how it will go.

## 4. Twenty days

This is the paragraph the phase is built around.

> **Twenty days later the platform suffered an in-region failure the exercise could not have found.** A
> regional failover replaces the entire application tier, so it never exercises a *live* application tier's
> reaction to a data-layer failover beneath it. The exercise tested the recovery mechanism. The incident
> tested the application's assumption about the recovery mechanism. **Those are different tests, and only
> one of them was on the calendar.**

The mechanics of why are worth spelling out, because the sentence is easy to nod at and easy to
misunderstand as a criticism of the exercise.

When CloudNimbus fails a region, every service in the target region **starts**. Pods are scheduled,
processes initialise, connection pools are created from nothing and resolve the cluster endpoint for the
first time. There is no pool of long-lived connections held open to a previous writer, because there is no
previous writer in that region and no process that was running before the cutover. A regional failover
therefore exercises the recovery path with an application tier in the freshest possible state.

The 8 September incident was the opposite condition. The application tier was **running, warm, and holding
connections**, and the thing that changed underneath it was a single Aurora writer being demoted and a
replica promoted in its place. What was tested was not whether the platform could be started somewhere else
but whether a live process would notice that the socket it was holding no longer led anywhere useful. **The
exercise could not have surfaced that, in any variant, because the exercise's method destroys the
precondition.**

That is not a defect in CAL-10. CAL-10 tests what it is designed to test and it passed. It is a statement
about **coverage**: an annual regional failover leaves an entire class of failure — the in-region,
beneath-a-live-tier class — untested, and until September nobody had written that down. `ACT-06-04` adds a
**quarterly in-region failover game day** from Q4 precisely to close it, deliberately kept distinct from
CAL-10 rather than folded into it, because a control that tries to test two things tests the easier one.
**The argument is now carried in GOV-21 §9 as well as here**, dated to this vantage, because a coverage
limit recorded only in a chapter is a limit the sampler who pulls the exercise report never reads.

**The mechanism worked and the assumption about it did not.** The Aurora failover completed in forty-seven
seconds, inside design, on a platform whose recovery architecture had been exercised end to end three weeks
earlier. What failed was a belief the application held about what a completed failover would do to it. 06.13
draws the parallel this deliberately echoes.

## 5. What the exercise evidences against the standards

**SR-10 is evidenced.** The 4-hour recovery time objective and the 15-minute recovery point objective were
both met, measured, and recorded in an exercise report with a scenario, a timeline and named participants.
That is the strongest single piece of availability evidence this phase produces.

**A.5.29, information security during disruption, is evidenced by the exercise and by the incident
together.** No control was suspended during either. The exercise ran with the same access controls, the same
change gates and the same logging in force in `us-west-2` as in `us-east-1`; the break-glass elevation used
on the exercise morning of 2026-08-19 went through the ordinary just-in-time path and was reviewed inside 48
hours like the other three uses in the quarter. **A continuity event is the commonest occasion for a control
to be quietly turned off**, and the evidence that none was is worth as much as the recovery time.

**A.5.30, ICT readiness for business continuity, reads `Implemented` — and this exercise is the reason that
is now true, not the reason it was asserted.** `03.09` recorded A.5.30 as `Planned` at SoA v1.0, and Phase
04's RAID entry `IS-13` records that 27 of the 29 Partial or Planned entries closed at the **2026-06-15**
reissue, leaving only A.5.28 and A.8.32 Partial. A.5.30 therefore reads `Implemented` on the current SoA
issue. It was moved there on the strength of `CNB-C-100` having been given A.5.30 alongside A.5.29 —
**before `CNB-C-100` had operated.** CAL-10 was still two months away. **The status ran ahead of the
evidence**, which is the failure mode clause 7.5 exists to prevent and the same failure **R-30** describes.
At **2026-08-19** the testing limb finally acquired the evidence the status had been asserting since June.
**The status is now supportable where it was not, and nothing anybody did in September made it so.**

Two things about A.5.30 have to be stated here rather than left for a reader to reconstruct. **On the
control library as published, exactly two rows cite A.5.30**: `CNB-C-136`, which carries the **planning**
limb, and `CNB-C-100`, which carries the **testing** limb. `CNB-C-088` and `CNB-C-101` cite **A.5.29**, not
A.5.30, so a clean CAL-16 in November would not advance A.5.30's testing limb — on the library as published,
CAL-16 does not touch A.5.30 at all, and any statement that it does is an attribution to a row that does not
carry it. And **A.5.30 requires ICT readiness to be planned, implemented, maintained and tested, while
nothing in the library implements the maintenance limb.** That gap is recorded as **`IS-24`**, referred to
the clause 9.2 internal audit and carried as a candidate for the next SoA issue. Phase 04 is not amended for
it, because the referral is the point.

**And the part that does not flatter anybody.** `04.11` §4.2 re-scheduled `CNB-C-136` — the annual
re-derivation of ICT continuity requirements from the business impact analysis, and the **sole carrier of
A.5.30's planning limb** — into **2026-08**, inside this quarter. **It was not performed.** That is
**`D-06-05`**, owner Wes Delacroix, treated exactly as `D-06-01` was: recorded, **not back-dated**, with
clause 10.2 corrective action **`CA-06-04`**. A.5.30 reads `Implemented`, and the only row carrying its
planning limb did not operate in the one month the window gave it.

**R-19 was not reduced.** The proposal to reduce R-19 — disaster recovery fails to meet the four-hour
objective in a real event — on the strength of a 2h51m exercise result was put to the September register
review and **declined on two independent grounds**. The first is that **an exercise is not a real event**: it
was scheduled, it began at 06:00 on a Wednesday chosen for its low load, the participants knew it was
coming, and nothing about it was adversarial. The second is the register's own movement rule — R-19 sits at
2 × 5 = 10, the consequence has not changed, and **the next likelihood step is 1, which is reserved for the
not-reasonably-foreseeable**. A four-hour recovery failing in a real event is not that. R-19 is one of the
two entries Phase 03 named as immovable, and the best exercise result in the programme's history did not
move it.

R-19 was one of **six reduction proposals** put to that review and **none of the six was accepted**. The
only movement the register recorded in September was in the other direction: **R-08 rose from 4 × 3 = 12 to
5 × 3 = 15**, Moderate to High, on a re-rating nobody had proposed. The register left the review at **37
entries — 8 High · 17 Moderate · 12 Low**, and 06.11 and GOV-24 carry the reasoning.
