## 1. A backup is a claim about the future

Every organisation has backups. Rather fewer have evidence that a backup can be turned back into a running
database with the same number of rows in it, and the gap between those two positions is what this chapter is
about.

The claim a backup makes is a claim about a future event that has not happened: *if the primary is lost, the
data can be recovered from this copy.* A backup job that reports success has established that bytes were
written. It has not established that the bytes can be read back, that the restore path works, that the
target account has the capacity and the permissions, that the schema round-trips, or that the row counts
agree. **Only a restore establishes any of that**, which is why the library carries two controls here rather
than one: `CNB-C-097` for the job, and `CNB-C-098` for the test.

This chapter reports one of those two operating on every occurrence in the quarter and the other missing an
occurrence, and the missing occurrence is `D-06-01`.

## 2. `CNB-C-097` — the nightly cycle

The control requires that **Aurora automated snapshots and S3 cross-region replication run nightly under
the 35-day retention of RT-07**, and that **a backup job that does not report success inside its window
pages the on-call site reliability engineer**.

| Measure | Q3 2026 |
|---|---|
| Nightly snapshot and cross-region replication cycles | **92** — one per night of the quarter |
| Cycles reporting success | **92** |
| Jobs re-run after a transient failure and reporting success inside their window | **2** |
| Cycles escalated to the on-call engineer as a failure | **0** |

**Ninety-two of ninety-two is the figure, and the two re-runs are stated rather than netted off.** A job that
failed transiently, was re-run automatically, and completed inside its window is a job that reported success
inside its window; the control's condition is satisfied and no page was warranted. But a table that showed
only "92 / 92" would conceal that the mechanism was exercised twice, and the fact that the retry path worked
is a more interesting piece of evidence than the ninety clean nights. **The nights nothing happened prove
the schedule. The two nights something happened prove the control.**

The 35-day retention comes from **RT-07**, and it carries a consequence Phase 02 disclosed and did not
engineer away: RT-07's thirty-five days and RT-08's thirty-day deletion-on-termination window overlap, so
**30 + 35 = 65 days of residue** in which a record deleted from the primary stores still exists in a backup
that has not aged out. `CNB-C-128` tracks that residue and records the window on the deletion certificate
the customer receives. It is named here because a durability chapter that described a 35-day backup cycle
without acknowledging what it means for deletion would be describing half of the arrangement, and it is the
half a competent question about backups finds first.

**Cross-region replication is a durability control and not a recovery control**, and the distinction runs
through the rest of this chapter. Replication produces a second copy in `us-west-2` that is faithful to the
first. Faithful is exactly the property that makes it useful against the loss of a region and useless
against the corruption of a row: a replica replicates whatever it is given.

## 3. `CNB-C-098` — the monthly restore test, and the month it did not run

The control requires that **the monthly restore test under CAL-09 restores a production snapshot into an
isolated AWS account and compares row counts and table checksums against the source**, with the comparison
output filed as the test record. It feeds **EC-08**, whose sampling unit is one monthly test: snapshot
identifier, target account, source and restored row counts and checksums, tester, and result.

| Month | Performed | Volume | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| July 2026 | **2026-07-16** | 1.9 TB | Restored into an isolated account; **all row counts and table checksums matched** |
| August 2026 | **Not performed** | — | — |
| September 2026 | **2026-09-23** | 2.1 TB | Restored; **one checksum mismatch** on a partitioned audit table, traced to a partition created between the snapshot and the comparison. Comparison **re-run under the corrected method on 2026-09-25** and matched in full |

**Q3: two of three.**

### 3.1 September's mismatch, and why the fix was to the method

The September test produced a checksum mismatch on one table, and the disposition of it is worth setting out
because a mismatch reported and then explained away is the shape of a control that has stopped working.

The table is a partitioned audit table. The mismatch was traced to **a partition created in the source
between the moment the snapshot was taken and the moment the comparison was run** — that is, the source had
grown a partition the snapshot could not contain, and the comparison was reading a live source rather than a
source pinned to the snapshot's point in time. The restored data was correct against the snapshot it came
from. The comparison was wrong about what it was comparing to.

**The comparison method was corrected, not the data.** The comparison now pins the source read to the
snapshot's point in time so that the two sides of the checksum describe the same instant. That is the right
correction and it is also a slightly uncomfortable one, because it means the July test — which reported all
checksums matching — ran under the same method. July's result is not thereby wrong; a method that can
produce a spurious mismatch when a partition appears mid-comparison does not produce a spurious match, and a
matching checksum under the old method is at least as strong as one under the new. **The correction removes
a source of false mismatches, not of false matches.**

**The September exception did not close on the explanation.** The comparison was **re-run under the
corrected, point-in-time-pinned method on 2026-09-25** and returned a full match on every table including
the partitioned audit table. Until that re-run the September occurrence carried an open exception, and a
"two of three" that leans on a test with an uncleared exception is a weaker statement than it looks. The
re-run is what makes September's occurrence a completed test rather than an explained one.

## 4. Why August was missed

The reason is not that anybody forgot. **The team treated the disaster recovery exercise of 2026-08-19 as
satisfying August's restore test.**

That is a reasonable-sounding mistake and it is a mistake, and this chapter has to say why in its own terms
rather than by citing a calendar.

**`CAL-09` restores a snapshot into an isolated account and compares row counts and table checksums against
the source. It is a durability and integrity test.** What it asks is: *is the copy we hold a faithful and
complete copy of what was there, and can it be turned back into a database?* Its output is a comparison, and
its failure mode is a number that does not match.

**`CAL-10` fails the platform to a standing replica in another region and measures how long that takes and
how much data is lost in the process. It is an availability test.** What it asks is: *can we serve customers
from somewhere else, quickly enough, with a small enough gap?* Its output is a recovery time and a recovery
point, and its failure mode is a clock.

The two answer different questions, and the sentence that settles it is this:

> **A replica that has faithfully replicated corruption passes CAL-10 and fails CAL-09.**

A failover to a replica that contains a corrupted table will complete inside the recovery time objective,
serve traffic, and satisfy every measurement CAL-10 takes — because CAL-10 measures the transition, not the
contents. CAL-09 is the only control in the set that reads the contents and compares them against something.
Substituting one for the other does not weaken the evidence a little; it removes the only occurrence in the
month of the control that tests integrity, and it substitutes evidence about a property nobody was asking
about.

**The root cause is not individual carelessness, and the corrective action says so.** The assurance calendar
placed CAL-09 and CAL-10 in the same month, both activities involve restoring data, both are owned by the
same director, and **no one owned the question of whether one discharged the other**. That is a calendar
design condition, and an engineer who concluded that a full regional failover was at least as good as a
monthly restore was making an inference the calendar invited and nobody had answered in writing.

It is recorded as a **control deficiency** and carries clause **10.2** corrective action **CA-06-01**, whose
correction is the September test performed on 2026-09-23 and whose corrective action addresses the cause:
the two calendar items now state in terms what each tests, and the restore test record template requires the
performer to name the snapshot and the target account, which a failover exercise cannot supply.

## 5. The test was not re-performed and back-dated

**A restore test performed in September is not evidence of a control operating in August**, and ADR-0026
records the decision not to pretend otherwise.

The option existed, though it was narrower than it sounds. RT-07 holds snapshots for **35 days**, so a
snapshot taken on 2026-08-01 expired on **2026-09-05** and only **a snapshot taken in the last third of
August** was still in retention in the last week of September. Such a snapshot could have been restored in
the last week of the month and filed as the August test record. It would have produced an artefact with an
August snapshot date on it, and it would have made `CNB-C-098` look like a control with three occurrences in
three months.

It was refused, and the reasoning is short. **The control's cadence is monthly, and a monthly control's
occurrence is an event in a month, not a document with a month's name on it.** What the service auditor
samples is whether the control operated in each period of the population, and a test performed on 24
September against August's snapshot is a test that operated in September. Filing it as August's would be
misstating the date on which a control operated to a party who is going to ask for the surrounding evidence
— the ticket, the calendar entry, the engineer's time, the isolated account's CloudTrail record — all of
which say September. It would convert a recorded deficiency into a misrepresentation, and it would do so to
avoid a single missing occurrence in a control that has five more to run.

**The population for the observation window is six and one occurrence is missing.** That is the whole of the
disclosure. **This document states no window deviation rate**, because three of the six occurrences have not
happened: October, November and December are ahead, and a rate computed on three occurrences and presented
as a rate for six is a number that is wrong in whichever direction the remaining months go. **The honest
number is the one the service auditor will compute at the end of the period, from the population that
actually exists**, and Phase 06 does not compute it early to get ahead of it.

What can be said now is stated as two of three to date, in every table in this phase, with the population
named alongside it.

## 6. What the quarter says against the criteria

Two controls, one criterion and one Annex A control, and it is worth setting the mapping out rather than
leaving a reader to infer it from Phase 04.

The words "**develops or acquires**" are quoted in full because they are the ones that do the work here.
CloudNimbus does not develop the environmental protections or the recovery infrastructure underneath its
databases and object stores; it **acquires** them from AWS, and they are carved out and disclosed as
**CSOC-01**, **CSOC-05** and **CSOC-08** rather than claimed. A version of A1.2 that read only "develops"
would let an entity in this position argue the criterion did not reach it. It does reach it, on the
acquiring limb, and what CloudNimbus has to evidence is the authorising, the implementing, the operating,
the maintaining and the monitoring of what it acquired.

| | Position at 2026-09-30 |
|---|---|
| **A1.2** — the entity authorises, designs, **develops or acquires**, implements, operates, approves, maintains and monitors environmental protections, software, data back-up processes and recovery infrastructure to meet its objectives | Both `CNB-C-097` and `CNB-C-098` serve it. The back-up process is evidenced across all 92 nights; the **recovery** half is evidenced for two of the three occurrences that fell due |
| **A.8.13** information backup | The same two controls carry it in the Statement of Applicability. The status field does not change on one missed occurrence — a control that is implemented and had a deviation is still implemented — and 06.13 says so rather than letting the reader assume the SoA moved |
| **RT-07** — 35-day snapshot retention | Held on every store for the whole quarter; the retention tag is part of the `CNB-C-097` job record and part of EC-07's sampling unit |

**The distinction between the two halves of A1.2 is the whole of this chapter.** Backing data up is a
continuous control with 92 occurrences and no deviations. Proving the backup restores is a monthly control
with three occurrences and one deviation. **An entity that reports A1.2 as satisfied on the strength of the
first half has answered the easier of the two questions the criterion asks**, and the service auditor will
sample the harder one six times.

## 7. What durability rests on, and what it does not

| Proposition | Status at this vantage | What supports it |
|---|---|---|
| Snapshots and cross-region replication ran on every night of the quarter | **Evidenced** | 92 of 92 cycles, `CNB-C-097`, EC-07 |
| The backup failure path operates | **Evidenced** | Two transient failures, both re-run and successful inside their window |
| A production snapshot can be restored into an isolated account with matching row counts and checksums | **Evidenced for July outright, and for September only after the exception cleared** — September's first comparison produced a checksum mismatch on a partitioned audit table, and the match was obtained on the re-run of 2026-09-25 under the point-in-time-pinned method | `CNB-C-098`, EC-08, 1.9 TB and 2.1 TB |
| The same was true in August | **Not evidenced, and cannot now be** | The occurrence did not happen; `D-06-01` |
| The 8 September incident engaged the recovery point objective | **No** | Writes were rejected at the application and never accepted; nothing was lost to recover |
| Backups protect against a corrupted row propagating to the replica | **Only through CAL-09** | Replication is faithful by design; the integrity comparison is the only check that reads contents |

The last two rows are the ones that repay attention.

**No data was lost on 8 September and the recovery point objective was never engaged.** The Aurora failover
completed cleanly at the data layer in forty-seven seconds; the writes that failed were **rejected at the
application** rather than accepted and then lost. **A rejected write is not a lost write**, and the whole of
the durability claim in this quarter turns on that difference. A worker who could not clock in had to clock
in again; nobody's clock-in was silently swallowed. 06.05 sets out how that was established.

And the third row from the bottom is the reason the missing August test is a real deficiency rather than a
paperwork one. The only control in this set that would detect a faithfully replicated corruption is the one
that did not operate in August. Nothing in this chapter suggests such a corruption occurred — there is no
evidence of one, and the September test found none — but the absence of evidence in a month where the test
was not run is an absence produced by not looking, and that is a different kind of absence from the one July
and September produced.
