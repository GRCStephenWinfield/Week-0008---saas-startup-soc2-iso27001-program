## 1. PI1.2, and the three doors into the engine

**PI1.2 concerns the completeness, accuracy and authorisation of the inputs to processing.** It is the
criterion an engineer is most likely to think has been answered by an API schema, and the criterion a
service auditor is most likely to find has been answered by nothing at all — because input validation is
usually implemented as a scatter of guards written by different people at different times, and no single
artefact records what was refused.

Data reaches the calculation engine through exactly three doors, and Phase 04 put a control on each.

| Door | What arrives | Control | Cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| The end user's device | Clock events, expense captures, leave requests — submitted to the API by the mobile and web clients | `CNB-C-105` | Continuous |
| The employer's systems | Employment-data feeds: workers, assignments, pay codes, cost centres | `CNB-C-106` | Continuous |
| The tenant administrator | The pay-rule configuration the engine derives against | `CNB-C-104` | Continuous |

The three are not variations on one control. They differ in who supplies the data, in what a failure looks
like and in who can fix it, and cutting them apart means a deviation lands on the door it came through
rather than on "input validation" as a whole. The rest of this chapter takes them in that order, and then
says what the quarter's numbers do not establish, which is the part that matters most.

## 2. Time records — `CNB-C-105`

`CNB-C-105` validates a time record at the API on submission for overlapping shifts, duplicate clock events
and out-of-range durations, and a record that fails is **rejected into a tenant-visible exception queue
instead of being written to the ledger**.

| Measure | Q3 2026 |
|---|---|
| Time records submitted | **101,447,318** |
| Rejected at the API | **476,802** |
| Rejection rate | **0.47%** |
| — overlapping shifts | 218,394 |
| — duplicate clock events | 163,006 |
| — out-of-range durations | 95,402 |

218,394 + 163,006 + 95,402 = 476,802. Roughly 1.1 million records arrive on an average day, which is a
little over eighty records per end user across the quarter, and the shape of the rejections is the shape a
mobile workforce produces. **Duplicate clock events are overwhelmingly a person tapping twice on a phone
with an uncertain network**, not a system defect and not fraud. Overlapping shifts are the largest category
because they are what happens when a scheduled shift is edited by a manager while a worker is standing
inside it. Out-of-range durations are the smallest and the most varied — a clock-in with no clock-out that
the device eventually submits, a shift crossing a daylight-saving boundary, a device clock that is wrong.

**The rejection rate is not a quality metric and this chapter will not present it as one.** A stable 0.47%
tells you the validation is running and the population is behaving as it did last quarter. It does not tell
you whether the validation is catching the right things, and a rate that fell would be at least as
interesting as one that rose, because the most likely explanation for a sudden drop is a validator that
stopped executing rather than a workforce that got tidier. What makes the figure evidence is that the
rejections are enumerable by category and attributable to a tenant, which is what lets somebody ask the
question at all.

## 3. A rejected record is not a lost record

This is the argument the chapter exists for, and it is the one place where the word "completeness" earns
its keep.

**PI1.2 asks whether the inputs to processing are complete.** A control that discards a malformed
submission is a control that makes the processing *clean* and the input population *incomplete*, and it
would satisfy an accuracy reading of the criterion while failing a completeness reading. Silent discard is
the failure mode here, and it is attractive precisely because it produces a tidy ledger and no support
tickets.

`CNB-C-105` does not discard. **A rejected record goes into a tenant-visible exception queue, where the
worker's manager or the tenant's administrator can see it, correct it and resubmit it.** The record is not
in the ledger and it is not gone; it is in a place where the party who knows what actually happened on that
shift can adjudicate it. That is what completeness means for an input CloudNimbus did not author: not that
every submission is processed, but that no submission disappears without somebody being told.

The distinction has a consequence for how a deviation in this control would present. A rejection is not a
deviation — it is the control operating. **The deviation would be a rejected record that never reached the
queue**, or a queue nobody could see, or a queue with no route back into a period that had already closed.
Those are the conditions worth testing, and they are what the evidence class behind `CNB-C-105` retains:
the rejection record, its category, its tenant, its queue entry and its resolution state. A control that
recorded only the count of rejections would be a control whose evidence could not distinguish the healthy
case from the pathological one.

The same principle, one stage later, is why the four blocked exports in 06.09 are the control working
rather than a service failure. **A held output and a queued input are the same design instinct pointing in
opposite directions**: refuse to process what you cannot trust, refuse to deliver what you cannot
reconcile, and in both cases put the thing where a human can find it.

### 3.1 The 41,208 writes the platform refused on 8 September

The completeness argument above is about records the validators refused. On **2026-09-08** the platform
refused **41,208** writes for a reason that has nothing to do with validation — the write path in
`us-east-1` was down for seventy-one minutes and `INC-2026-031` is 06.05's subject. But **the completeness
question the refusals raise is PI1.2's**, it belongs in this chapter, and a chapter on input completeness
that did not mention the quarter's largest single loss of inputs would be answering the criterion around the
event.

A refused write is a clock event, a timesheet submission or an expense capture that did not enter the
ledger. Whether the input population is complete therefore turns on what happened next.

| Outcome | Count |
|---|---|
| Resubmitted and accepted within the following twenty-four hours | **40,930** |
| Not resubmitted | **278** |
| **Total refused during the incident window** | **41,208** |

40,930 + 278 = **41,208**. The match was established by joining the refusal log to the ledger on **worker,
tenant and clock-event timestamp**, so a resubmission is a record of the same event by the same worker
rather than a count of writes that happened to arrive afterwards.

**The 278 are the honest part.** They cannot be distinguished between a worker who tried, was refused, and
gave up, and a worker whose shift boundary had passed before they tried again — the platform sees a refusal
with no matching later submission and cannot see which of those produced it. That is a **completeness
question under PI1.2** and it is recorded as one rather than absorbed into the incident's availability
account.

**None of these writes reached a validator, so none of them entered `CNB-C-105`'s queue.** A refusal on 8
September happened **at the database**: the write path in `us-east-1` was bound to an instance that had
stopped being the writer, and the `INSERT` was refused there. The submission never got as far as the
overlap, duplicate and duration checks, was never adjudicated against them, and produced no rejection
record, no category and no queue entry. **The mechanism §3 describes is fed by validation, and this
population was refused upstream of it** — so an account that places the 278 in the tenant-visible exception
queue is describing a control path that did not operate.

**What surfaced them was a reconciliation Customer Success ran by hand from the refusal log on 2026-09-09**,
the day after the incident. It performed the join above, produced the 40,930 / 278 split, and named the
unresubmitted events to the tenants they belonged to. **No row in the control library requires that
reconciliation, names an owner for it, sets a cadence for it or retains its output as an evidence class.**
It happened because somebody decided it should. That absence is recorded as **`IS-27`** and referred, rather
than closed by writing a control in the chapter that found the gap — the same treatment `IS-25` and `IS-26`
receive.

**Forty-seven tenants, and not 318.** The 318 is the count of tenants that attempted at least one write
inside the seventy-one minutes. **The 278 are concentrated in the 47 of those tenants whose shift boundary
fell inside the window** — where a worker had one particular moment to clock in at, and no reason to try
again once it had gone. In the other 271 tenants every refused write was retried and accepted inside the
day. The 47 is a subset of the 318 and not a competing figure for it, and it is the population the
2026-09-09 notification went to.

The adjudication that follows is the employer's. The platform does not know what happened on those shifts
and the employer does, **each tenant's own records govern**, and the boundary is the one CUEC-04 draws for a
configured rule. What CloudNimbus can say is that no refusal was silently dropped, that 40,930 of 41,208
came back inside a day, and that the remaining 278 were named to the tenants who could resolve them.

**And the finding, stated plainly: no control, no deviation and no register entry owns the 278.**
`CNB-C-105` never saw them. `D-06-02` is about measurement and `D-06-03` about the reconciliation queue in
06.09; neither reaches an input population left incomplete by an availability failure, and no entry in the
risk register describes one. The 278 are a **PI1.2 completeness question the control set does not answer**,
which is why they are carried forward as `IS-27` and not reported as closed here.

## 4. Employer feeds — `CNB-C-106`

`CNB-C-106` reconciles each employment-data transfer from an employer feed on receipt against the record
count and checksum supplied with the file, and **a mismatch suspends the load** and raises a discrepancy
report to the tenant's Customer Success manager.

| Measure | Q3 2026 |
|---|---|
| Feed loads received | **2,764** |
| Suspended on a record-count or checksum mismatch | **11** |
| Resolved with the tenant and reloaded | **11** |
| Mean resolution time | **1.4 days** |

Eleven suspensions in 2,764 loads is 0.4%, and the interesting property is not the rate but the *stop*.
**A partially loaded employment feed is worse than no feed at all**, because a calculation performed
against a worker population that is missing its last two hundred rows will complete, reconcile against
those inputs, and produce a set of amounts that is internally consistent and covers the wrong people.
Nothing downstream would catch it: `CNB-C-107`'s reconciliation compares output to the inputs the run
actually used, and the inputs the run used would be the truncated ones.

That is why the control suspends rather than loads-what-it-can-and-flags-the-rest, and it is why the
counterparty is the tenant's Customer Success manager rather than an engineering queue. A count mismatch on
an employer feed is almost never something CloudNimbus can resolve alone — the file came from the
employer's HR system, and the question of whether 4,812 rows or 5,012 rows is the right number for that
employer this week is a question only the employer can answer. **The mean resolution of 1.4 days is
therefore a measure of a conversation, not of an engineering fix**, and it is the figure most sensitive to
the customer's own responsiveness. All eleven were resolved and reloaded inside the quarter; none was
carried across a period boundary.

`CNB-C-106` cites **A.5.14**, information transfer, and the citation reaches the transfer rules and the
integrity check on receipt. It does not reach the substance of the reconciliation, which is a
completeness question the annex does not ask.

## 5. Rule-set activations — `CNB-C-104`, and the authorisation limb

`CNB-C-104` validates a tenant's pay-rule configuration against the published rule schema, **refuses a rule
set that fails validation**, and records the named tenant approver, the timestamp and the rule version on
activation.

| Measure | Q3 2026 |
|---|---|
| Rule-set activations | **1,344** |
| Refused on schema validation | **96** |
| Activations recording a named tenant approver | **1,344 of 1,344** |

The second row is the accuracy limb and the third is the authorisation limb, and they are worth separating
because PI1.2 asks for both and most control libraries answer only the first.

**Ninety-six refusals in 1,344 activations is 7.1%**, which is an order of magnitude above the rejection
rate on time records and is exactly what should be expected. A rule set is authored by a human being
configuring an overtime threshold or a differential band in an admin console, not emitted by a device. The
schema check is the last automated thing between that person and a rule the engine will apply to real pay,
and a validator that never refused anything on a surface like that would be a validator worth
investigating.

The authorisation limb is the quieter one and it is the one that carries the boundary. **Every activation
recorded a named tenant approver**, which is the artefact that lets CloudNimbus say who at the employer
turned a rule on and when. It is not a claim that the approver was right, and 04.06 is explicit that
`CNB-C-104` records the tenant approver and does not perform the tenant's review. **CUEC-04 — the employer
reviews and approves its own pay rules before activating them — is the user-entity control PI1.2 depends
on**, and 02.11 §5.1 is careful that this, and not CUEC-05, is where the necessity for the wrong-rule
defect class sits. A schema-valid rule expressing a policy the employer did not intend will activate, will
be recorded against a named approver, and will be applied by the engine exactly as configured.

## 6. What the quarter's numbers do not establish

Three limits, stated on the face of the chapter rather than left for the examination to find.

**The denominator is not a control.** 101,447,318 records were submitted; the controls in this chapter
speak to what happened to the records that arrived. A clock event a worker's phone never transmitted, a
feed file the employer never sent, an activation that failed before it reached the console — none of them
appears in any figure above, and no control in this family would see them. Completeness of an input
population is bounded by what reached the boundary, and the reader who takes 101.4 million as "all the time
worked in the quarter" has read it wrong.

**The 476,802 are the records the validators were written to catch.** A time record that is internally
consistent, non-overlapping, non-duplicate and of plausible duration will be accepted, and it will be
accepted whether or not the worker was there. **`CNB-C-105` tests the shape of a submission, not its
truth**, and no automated control at this door can do otherwise. The truth of a clock event is adjudicated
by the manager approving the timesheet, which is the employer's process, and by the geolocation capture
where the tenant has enabled it — a privacy matter Phase 07 takes rather than an integrity one.

**Three months is not a period.** All three controls in this chapter operate continuously, so the auditor
samples from a system-generated population rather than from occurrences, and a large clean population in
Q3 is welcome and is not a conclusion about the window. Nothing here forecasts one.
