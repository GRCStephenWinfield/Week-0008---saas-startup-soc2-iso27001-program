## 1. A promise Phase 02 made, coming due

Phase 02 mapped the nine description criteria of **DC section 200, *2018 Description Criteria for a
Description of a Service Organization's System in a SOC 2 Report***, and answered five of them: DC1, DC2,
DC3, DC6 and DC7. Against **DC4** — disclosures about incidents — it wrote that the criterion belonged to
Phase 06 and that, as at its vantage of 2026-03-31, **no reportable incident had occurred**.

That was true when it was written and it is no longer true. **DC4 is engaged, it is mandatory, and this
chapter is where the promise comes due.**

Phase 02 also said why it declined to write DC4 in advance, and the reason has aged well enough to repeat.
DC4 and DC9 are the two criteria nobody can draft ahead of the period, because DC4 requires disclosing
incidents that have not happened yet and DC9 requires identifying changes during a period that has not
started. An organisation writing its description in January for a period that ended in December will
reconstruct DC1 and DC3 comfortably from architecture diagrams and **will find that the evidence for DC4 is
in people's memories and in a ticket queue.**

## 2. What DC4 requires

Under DC section 200, **DC4 requires the description to disclose relevant details of identified system
incidents** that either

- **resulted from controls that were not suitably designed or did not operate effectively**, or
- **resulted in a significant failure in the achievement of one or more service commitments and system
  requirements**,

during the period covered by the description.

**The 8 September incident engages the second limb, and the second limb alone.** It caused **SC-01**, the
99.9% monthly availability commitment, to be missed for September 2026 for the **599 customers served from
`us-east-1`**: 99.84% achieved in that region against an allowance of 43.2 minutes, on seventy-one minutes of
unavailability. The 41 EU-residency customers served from `core-eu-central` were unaffected and SC-01 was
met for them at 100%. A missed monthly service commitment, on a commitment that appears in every customer's
master services agreement, is a significant failure in the achievement of a principal service commitment on
any reading of those words.

### 2.1 The first limb is not engaged, and saying so costs something

It is tempting to reach the **first** limb as well, through `CNB-C-096`, and the temptation should be
resisted because the reading does not survive the words of the criterion.

Limb (a) covers incidents **that were the result of** controls that were not suitably designed or did not
operate effectively. `CNB-C-096` did not cause the outage. **It failed to measure it.** A measurement
control whose probe performs a read is a design deficiency — it is `D-06-02`, it is real, and it belongs in
**Section IV** with the control, the test, the population and the auditor's evaluation. It is not a cause,
and an incident is not the result of the instrument that failed to see it. **The disclosure rests on limb
(b).**

That correction displaces a question, and the question is the auditor's next one, so it is answered here
rather than left for somebody to ask.

> **Did the incident result from a control that was not suitably designed?** The pool health check's
> reachability-only test and the unset `max_lifetime` are **application configuration**. `CNB-C-099` governs
> the provisioning of data stores and node groups from shared Terraform modules; it does not reach an
> application's connection pool. `CNB-C-096` measures availability and does not configure it. No control in
> the library governs the pool configuration at all. So the honest answer is that **the incident did not
> result from a control that was not suitably designed; it resulted from a configuration that no control
> governed.**
>
> That is a different disclosure from the softer one, and **a worse one**. A badly designed
> control is a control that can be rewritten; an ungoverned configuration is a gap in the control set, and
> the entity did not know the gap was there until a failover happened underneath a live application tier.
> Recorded as **`IS-25`** and referred, rather than closed by writing a control in the week after an
> incident.

Detection itself was not the failure — `CNB-C-068`'s synthetic clock-in paged at 14:24 — and the distinction
between detecting an outage, measuring it, and causing it is three distinctions rather than one. DC4 asks
about the third.

## 3. Six things this chapter has to get right

### 3.1 The disclosure is not discretionary

**DC4 is a description criterion, not a control.** The description of the system is management's, and it is
evaluated against the description criteria in their own right. An incomplete description is a description
that does not meet the criteria — whatever the opinion on the controls happens to be, and however well the
controls performed elsewhere. There is no threshold of embarrassment below which an incident that meets
DC4's terms may be left out, and no discretion vested in management to weigh disclosure against commercial
comfort.

Nor is disclosure something the service auditor supplies. The auditor reads the description and evaluates
it; the auditor does not write it, and cannot repair an omission by noticing one. **An incident that meets
DC4 and is absent from the description is a defect in the description, and it is management's defect.**

### 3.2 Disclosing an incident is not the same as the opinion being modified

These are separate things done by separate parties for separate purposes, and conflating them is the reason
organisations under-disclose.

**The description discloses. The auditor evaluates.** DC4 governs what Section III must contain. Test
exceptions are disclosed in Section IV with the tests performed and the results; and an auditor modifies an
opinion only where the deviations mean the applicable criteria were not met. A clean opinion does not mean
zero exceptions, and a disclosed incident does not mean a modified opinion.

**This chapter says nothing about the opinion**, and neither does any other document in this phase. That is
not modesty; it is the same rule ADR-0015 established for the risk register — no forecast is published until
it has been proved reachable — applied to a conclusion that only one party is entitled to reach and that
party has not reached it.

### 3.3 The missed August restore test is a deviation, not a system incident

This is the distinction most likely to be got wrong, and getting it wrong would put a true fact in a place
where no reader looks for it.

**The August occurrence of `CNB-C-098` did not happen.** That is `D-06-01`. A control that did not operate
on one of its scheduled occurrences is a **deviation** — a matter for **Section IV**, where the control, the
test, the population and the result are set out and the auditor records its evaluation.

**It is not a system incident.** No system failed, no service commitment was missed, and no customer
experienced anything. There is no timeline, no impact, no restoration and no incident record, because there
was no event: an activity that should have occurred did not. **DC4 is about identified system incidents, and
a control occurrence that did not take place is not one.**

Filing it under DC4 would look like candour and would function as concealment. A reader of Section III's
incident disclosures is looking for events that affected the service; a reader of Section IV is looking for
control occurrences that fell short. Putting a control deviation in the incident disclosure removes it from
the place where a sampler goes to find it and buries it in a place where it reads as noise. **This is the
same argument 04.11 made about the re-scheduled annual controls and DC5** — a matter recorded under the
wrong criterion has been disclosed in form and hidden in effect.

### 3.4 The disclosure is written now, at the vantage

The draft in §4 was written on **2026-09-09**, the day after the incident, under **DEC-605**, and it is
**ADR-0028**.

The alternative was to write it in February 2027 during examination fieldwork, when the description is
assembled, which is when most organisations write theirs. The reason not to is not diligence for its own
sake. It is that **the material DC4 needs decays faster than any other material in the description.** The
architecture will still be documented in February. The change tickets will still be in the pipeline record.
But the sequence of who knew what at 14:24, why a rolling restart was chosen over a second failover, which
tenant reported first and from where, and what the status page actually said while it was happening are
facts that live in an incident channel, a pager history and four people's recollection — and by February
they will have been compressed into a summary of a summary.

There is a second reason and it is less comfortable. **An incident written up five months later is written
up by people who already know how the period ended.** The 8 September incident will look different once
October, November and December have happened, and a disclosure drafted with that knowledge is a disclosure
shaped by it. Writing at the vantage produces a record made before anybody knew what it would need to
support.

### 3.5 The draft belongs in this phase

Phase 09 assembles the description. What it should not have to do is reconstruct an incident from a ticket.
The block below is bounded, complete on its own terms, and written to be **lifted into the description
unchanged**.

### 3.6 The commitment the disclosure is measured against was itself incomplete

A DC4 disclosure that a commitment was missed is only as good as the statement of the commitment, and this
one was not good enough.

> `02.12` published SC-01 as "99.9% monthly availability of the platform" and **did not state the
> exclusion**. The master services agreement's availability commitment excludes **maintenance windows
> announced at least fourteen days in advance**, and the August exercise ran inside a four-hour window —
> 06:00 to 10:00 UTC, notified to all customers on 2026-08-05 — whose **23 customer-visible minutes are
> excluded from the August measurement** on that basis; the `TP-20` window of 2026-07-15, notified
> 2026-07-01, removes a further 4 from July. **A 99.9% figure
> measured with an undisclosed exclusion is not the figure the customer reads**, and a description of the
> system that carries the commitment without the exclusion is incomplete under **DC2**.
>
> `02.12` is corrected to read "**99.9% monthly availability of the platform, excluding maintenance windows
> announced at least fourteen days in advance**", and **Phase 02 is re-issued**. Recorded at **DEC-613**,
> 2026-09-24, Marisol Vega.

This belongs in the DC4 chapter and not only in 06.01, because the criterion the September figure is
disclosed against and the criterion the commitment is stated under are read together by the same reader. A
phase that discloses a missed commitment precisely and states the commitment loosely has corrected the
smaller of the two defects.

## 4. The DC4 draft disclosure

> **Disclosure of identified system incidents.** One identified system incident during the period resulted
> in a significant failure in the achievement of a principal service commitment.
>
> On 8 September 2026, between 14:22 and 15:33 UTC, the CloudNimbus Workforce Platform did not accept write
> requests in the `us-east-1` region. An automated failover of the writer instance of the `core-us-east`
> Aurora PostgreSQL cluster completed at the data layer within 47 seconds. Application connection pools
> continued to hold open connections to the demoted database instance: the pool health check tested network
> reachability rather than the ability to write, and pooled connections were not retired and so did not
> re-resolve the cluster endpoint. Read requests succeeded throughout. Write requests failed for 71 minutes;
> they were rejected at the application rather than accepted and subsequently lost, no customer data was
> lost, and the recovery point objective was not engaged. **Write requests failed for every customer served
> from `us-east-1`; 318 of the 599 customers served from that region attempted a write during the window.**
> Customers served from the `core-eu-central` cluster in `eu-central-1` were not affected. 41,208 write
> requests failed. Service was restored by a rolling restart of the eleven services on the core write path.
>
> The 99.9% monthly availability commitment was not achieved for September 2026 in `us-east-1`; achieved
> availability for the region for the month was 99.84%. Service credits were applied under the master
> services agreement **to affected tenants on request**. A post-incident review was completed on 11
> September 2026 and five corrective actions were recorded with named owners and due dates.

**A little over two hundred and fifty words**, and every figure in them appears elsewhere in this phase with
its derivation attached.

Three properties of the draft are deliberate. It **names the mechanism** rather than describing "a database
issue", because a disclosure a reader cannot evaluate is not a disclosure. It **states what did not happen**
— no data lost, recovery point objective not engaged — because those are the questions a customer asks
second and third. And it **does not characterise the incident's significance beyond the commitment it
missed**, because DC4 asks for relevant details of the incident and its effect, not for management's view of
how serious it was.

One thing Phase 09 must do with it, and it is not a change to the text: confirm that no further incident
arising after this phase's vantage of 2026-09-30 requires disclosure alongside it. This phase covers three
months of a six-month period and says nothing about the other three.

### 4.1 The draft was written before its own consequences were known

The draft was written on **2026-09-09** and the post-incident review was held on **2026-09-11**. Both say
**no customer data was lost**. On **2026-09-21** — twelve days after the draft and ten after the review —
the `CNB-C-112` quarterly reconciliation found **two calculation runs with no stored output, both inside the
incident window.** The finding is recorded against **`INC-2026-031`**.

**Both runs were re-executed and their results stored on 2026-09-22**, and **no export had been issued from
either**, so nothing derived from an incomplete run reached a payroll provider. That is why "no customer
data was lost" survives in the draft above. It survives as **a fact that was checked**, not as a claim that
happened to hold.

The uncomfortable part is the sequence. A disclosure written in the week of an incident is written before
the incident's slower consequences have surfaced, and the two runs surfaced through a **quarterly** control.
Writing at the vantage buys the freshness ADR-0028 argues for and it buys a specific exposure alongside it:
**a disclosure drafted in the week of an incident has to be re-confirmed at period end, and re-confirmed
against the incident's own consequences rather than merely checked for additional incidents.** That cost is
recorded in ADR-0028's consequences, where the decision that incurred it lives.

## 5. The incident controls in Q3

`CNB-C-071` to `CNB-C-078` are the CC7 incident family, and this quarter gave them their first live
occurrence.

The table below carries a ninth row that is not part of that family. **`CNB-C-102` is the A1 post-incident
review control**, and an availability Severity-1 engages both families at once: the CC7 rows because an
incident was declared and worked, and the A1 row because the incident was an availability failure. A table
that showed only `CNB-C-071` to `CNB-C-078` would leave the reader to discover elsewhere that the same
review satisfied a second control on a tighter window.

| Control | What it requires | Q3 |
|---|---|---|
| `CNB-C-071` | Severity-1 or Severity-2 invokes the plan, names an incident commander, opens a timestamped record | Invoked once, at **14:31 on 2026-09-08**; Wes Delacroix named incident commander |
| `CNB-C-072` | Customer Success notifies affected tenants of a **confirmed security incident** within the MSA period | **No occurrence** — the incident was not a security incident |
| `CNB-C-073` | Semi-annual verification of the authority and counsel contact list | **No occurrence in Q3.** Due **2026-11**, inside the observation window and in Q4 — the single occurrence the window will hold |
| `CNB-C-074` | Chain-of-custody collection of incident material | No occurrence requiring forensic collection |
| `CNB-C-075` | Post-incident review within **ten business days**, actions tracked monthly | Satisfied — review held 2026-09-11, three business days |
| `CNB-C-076` | Semi-annual tabletop exercise | **No occurrence in Q3.** Due **2026-10**, inside the observation window and in Q4 — the single occurrence the window will hold |
| `CNB-C-077` | Incident commander and on-call SRE both confirm health against the restoration runbook before closure | Satisfied — both confirmed, incident closed 15:41 |
| `CNB-C-078` | Malware isolation and clean rescan | No occurrence |
| `CNB-C-102` | Post-incident review within **five business days** (A1), open actions carried at the monthly operations review | Satisfied — three business days |

**The two semi-annual rows are not due in the quarter and they are due inside the window.** `CNB-C-076`
falls in **2026-10** and `CNB-C-073` in **2026-11**, one occurrence each, both in Q4 and both inside the
observation window; 06.12 §4.3 carries them as the population the examination will sample. At a population
of one, a missed occurrence is the whole population, which is why "not due in the quarter" is a statement
about Q3 and not a statement about the period.

**One Severity-1. Zero Severity-2. Zero security incidents.**

### 5.1 Zero security incidents is a fact and not a comfort

**SC-02's 48-hour notification path produced no evidence in Q3.** SC-02 commits CloudNimbus to notifying a
customer of a security incident affecting customer data within 48 hours of determination; the operational
expression of it is `CNB-C-072`, and the determination that starts the clock is `CNB-C-069`'s recorded
decision. No event in the quarter was determined to be a security incident, so no notification was made, so
**`CNB-C-072` has a population of zero for three months of the observation window.**

A control with no occurrences cannot be shown to work. That is the same argument 05.10 made about `A.5.28`
and the incident evidence store in July, and it is **why R-22 — a security incident is not notified to the
affected customer within 48 hours — cannot move.** The register's movement rule requires evidence, and the
absence of an occurrence is not evidence about the control that would have handled one. A programme that
re-rated R-22 downward on a quiet quarter would be re-rating on the absence of tests rather than on their
results, which is the same refusal the September review applied to R-04 and the supplier outage that did not
happen.

### 5.2 One incident does not mean nothing else happened

**Severity-3 and Severity-4 tickets are not incidents under POL-13** and are worked as operational tickets.
The quarter contained many of them — single-service degradations, elevated latency on a reporting path, a
failed batch that retried successfully, an integration endpoint that stopped acknowledging and resumed.
They are triaged, dispositioned and closed under `CNB-C-067` and `CNB-C-069`, they contribute to the 5,102
alerts and the 148 high-severity triages in the quarter's record, and none of them met the severity matrix's
threshold for invoking the incident response plan.

This is said explicitly so that **"one incident in ninety-two days" cannot be read as a claim that one thing
went wrong in ninety-two days.** It is a claim about a threshold in a documented severity matrix. A reader
who takes it for a claim about operational quiet has been misled by a number that is technically correct,
and a description that permits that reading has chosen a comfortable ambiguity.
