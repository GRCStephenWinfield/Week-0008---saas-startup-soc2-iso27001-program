## 1. The engagement

**Ironwood Security Labs tested the CloudNimbus Workforce Platform from 2026-05-04 to 2026-05-22.** The
engagement discharges `CNB-C-026` — penetration testing of the external perimeter, the internal cloud
estate, the web and mobile applications and the multi-tenancy isolation model, commissioned annually and
after significant change, with every finding tracked to closure with a documented retest — and it is
**CAL-11** in the assurance calendar and **MS-06** in the programme milestones.

Three facts about the engagement's shape matter before any finding is read.

**It was scoped to the system, not to the company.** The five test streams cover the platform as 02.03
defines it: the internet-facing surface across `us-east-1`, `us-west-2` and `eu-central-1`; the five
in-system AWS accounts; the customer web application and tenant admin console; the iOS and Android clients;
and every documented tenant-scoped data path. CloudNimbus's corporate back-office systems were out of
scope, consistent with the SOC 2 system boundary and inconsistent with the ISMS boundary — which is one of
the places the two boundaries visibly do not coincide, exactly as 02.01 said they would not.

**It was adversarial, not a compliance walkthrough.** Ironwood held no design documentation for the
isolation model beyond what a customer administrator can see, and TS-02 was run assumed-breach: the tester
began from a compromised workload inside the estate rather than from the outside, because an external
perimeter test that stops at the load balancer tells a cloud-native company almost nothing about the blast
radius it actually cares about.

**Testing ran against a production-equivalent pre-production environment seeded with synthetic tenants.**
That is possible because `CNB-C-083` and **SR-12** require it — production data is not copied into
non-production, and a non-production dataset that must resemble production is generated synthetically or
masked. The consequences for the Critical finding are the subject of 05.12 and are the first thing that
chapter says.

## 2. The five test streams

| ID | Stream | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| TS-01 | External network | Internet-facing surface across three regions |
| TS-02 | Internal and cloud configuration | The five in-system AWS accounts, assumed-breach from a compromised workload |
| TS-03 | Web application | Customer web application and tenant admin console, authenticated and unauthenticated |
| TS-04 | Mobile application | iOS and Android clients, including on-device storage and transport |
| TS-05 | Multi-tenancy isolation | Cross-tenant access attempts against every documented data path |

**TS-05 is the stream commissioned because of what this business is.** Customer compensation data is
confidential under the master services agreement, and 04.06 records that multi-tenant isolation is the
confidentiality control that matters. A test programme for a multi-tenant SaaS platform that treats
isolation as a sub-heading of the web application stream has mis-weighted the risk. Here it is a stream in
its own right, scoped against *every documented data path* — which is also the phrase that defines the
stream's limit, since a path nobody documented is a path nobody tested, and the isolation suite under
`CNB-C-115` covers the same 214 documented access paths and inherits the same limit.

## 4. Reading the table honestly

**Sixteen findings is not a bad result and it is not a good one.** It is a result. A test of this scope
returning three findings would raise a question about the test; a test returning sixty would raise a
question about the estate. What the distribution says is more informative than the count: **six of the
sixteen came from TS-02 and TS-01 — the cloud estate and the perimeter — and every one of those six is a
configuration property rather than a defect in CloudNimbus's own code.** An over-wide IAM prefix, a security
group scoped to the VPC range, object-level logging absent on two buckets of 168, an unused key past ninety
days: these are the findings a mature estate produces, and they are the findings the five automated
mechanisms in 05.08 are supposed to catch. **That the four TS-02 findings were found by a human and not by
the daily posture scan under `CNB-C-060` is a fact about coverage, and it is filed where a coverage fact
belongs.**

Not in the detection backlog. That backlog is `CNB-C-070`'s product — a register of gaps in the **SIEM's
detection rules**, maintained by the weekly review of alert volume, false-positive rate and unactioned
events — and it answers a different question, which is what the platform fails to notice at run time. What
failed here is what the **posture scanner evaluates**. A gap of that kind is either a finding in the
**vulnerability register**, carrying a severity and a due date like any other, or a missing check in the
**`CNB-C-060` baseline**, which is the declared standard the scanner evaluates the seven accounts against.
These four were filed as findings in the register on 2026-05-22, and the baseline checks that would have
caught them are tracked as part of their remediation. Filing a scanner coverage gap into a detection-rule
backlog would have put it in front of the people who tune alerts rather than the people who maintain the
baseline, which is how a known gap survives a quarter.

**The three mobile findings sit outside the pipeline's control.** A mobile client is deployed by an
app store on a schedule the store partly controls, and its old versions keep running on devices for months.
That is why the deferral decision in §6 is a genuine decision rather than a formality.

**The Critical is `PT-01` and it is not treated in this chapter.** It has its own document. 05.12 sets out
the defect, what the test did and did not prove, the forensic search and the gap it exposed, the
remediation, the notification judgement and the dissent recorded against it. Everything this chapter says
about `PT-01` is the row above and the timeline below.

## 5. The remediation timeline, and where it sits relative to the window

| Wave | Findings | Remediated | Retested |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical | `PT-01` | 2026-05-29 | 2026-06-11 |
| High | `PT-02`, `PT-03`, `PT-04` | 2026-06-08 to 2026-06-19 | 2026-06-26 |
| Medium and Low, first tranche | `PT-05`, `PT-07`, `PT-10`, `PT-11`, `PT-13` | 2026-06-24 to 2026-06-30 | 2026-07-24 |
| Medium and Low, second tranche | `PT-06`, `PT-08`, `PT-12`, `PT-16` | 2026-07-08 to 2026-07-15 | 2026-07-24 |
| Deferred to the mobile release | `PT-09`, `PT-14` | Release 2026-08-14 | — |
| Accepted | `PT-15` | — | — |

Every closed finding has a remediation date that precedes its retest date, and every retest is Ironwood's
rather than CloudNimbus's. A finding closed on the strength of the engineer's own assertion that the code
now behaves is not a documented retest, and `CNB-C-026` requires one.

**No date in that table straddles the observation window, and the Critical is the row worth being explicit
about.** The window opened on **2026-07-01**. `PT-01` was found on 2026-05-22, remediated on 2026-05-29 and
retested clean on 2026-06-11 — **the whole sequence sits before the window opened**, so the defect and its
fix both fall outside the period the service auditor will opine on. That is a fact of the calendar and not
an achievement, and it should not be presented as one. What it buys is narrower than it looks: it means the
isolation control operating inside the window is the remediated control, so the population the auditor
samples from July onward is the build check and the isolation suite firing on every build, rather than a
control that changed halfway through. **The finding was still disclosed proactively to the service auditor
and to the certification body under `DEC-506`**, and 05.12 says why.

**Nine findings were remediated or retested inside July, and exactly four of them are in the July change
population — and the difference matters enough to state.** `PT-06`, `PT-08`, `PT-12` and `PT-16` were
remediated in July, on 2026-07-08 and 2026-07-15, and those four are CloudNimbus changes: they passed
through the same peer review, gates and pipeline as any other change and they are four of the **47**
deployments 05.09 records for the month. `PT-05`, `PT-07`, `PT-10`, `PT-11` and `PT-13` were remediated in
**June** and only **retested** on 2026-07-24. **A retest performed by Ironwood is not a CloudNimbus
change**; it produces a retest attestation, not a merged pull request, and counting those five into the
July change population would inflate it by five deployments that never happened. A penetration test
remediation that bypasses change management to move faster has traded one control failure for another —
which is why the four are in the population rather than beside it.

## 6. The two deferred mobile findings

`PT-09` and `PT-14` are open and scheduled for the **2026-08-14** mobile release under **DEC-510**, taken by
Junia Okonkwo on 2026-07-06. The decision was to hold them for the release train rather than ship them out
of cycle, and the reasoning is recorded rather than assumed.

**Both are inside their remediation target.** A Medium identified on 2026-05-22 falls due at ninety days on
2026-08-20; a Low falls due at 180 days. The 14 August release precedes both. Deferring a finding to a date
inside its own service level is not an exception to the vulnerability management standard; deferring one
past it would be, and 05.08 records that nothing Critical or High was past target at the vantage.

**An out-of-cycle mobile release is not free.** It carries an app store review with a schedule CloudNimbus
does not control, a truncated regression pass against the client, and a forced-update prompt to roughly
1.24 million end users of 640 customers. The judgement was that for a Medium confined to on-device storage
on a device the user controls, and a Low concerning certificate pinning where transport is already TLS 1.2
minimum under `CNB-C-056`, the risk introduced by an unscheduled release exceeded the risk removed by
closing three weeks earlier.

**That judgement is falsifiable and this document states the condition.** If the 14 August release slips,
`PT-09` approaches its 20 August due date with no remaining margin, and the decision that was defensible on
6 July stops being defensible without a new one. The dependency is recorded in the finding tracker rather
than held in somebody's head.

## 7. PT-15: enumeration is not authorisation

`PT-15` is a Low finding, it is the only accepted finding of the sixteen, and it is the one this chapter
must argue rather than record.

**The finding is true.** Tenant identifiers at CloudNimbus are sequential integers. A tenant administrator
who knows their own tenant identifier knows that identifiers near it exist, can estimate how many customers
CloudNimbus has and roughly when a given tenant was onboarded, and can enumerate a target list to point
requests at. All of that is real, and none of it is disputed.

**What sequential identifiers do not do is authorise anything.** Knowing an identifier exists is not the
same as being permitted to read what sits behind it. The control that decides whether a request returns
another tenant's data is **the tenant predicate applied at the data-access layer** — `CNB-C-035`, with the
isolation suite in `CNB-C-115` and the build-time check behind it. **The isolation control is the predicate,
not the opacity of the identifier.**

The remedy `PT-15` implies is a migration to opaque identifiers across every tenant-scoped table in five
Aurora clusters, with the foreign keys, the API contracts, the export formats and the customer integrations
that hang off them. That is a large, risky change touching the exact data paths the isolation model
protects, undertaken during a period in which the platform is being examined. **And at the end of it the
predicate would be doing precisely the same work it was doing before.** An attacker who cannot guess an
identifier is stopped by ignorance; an attacker who can guess one is stopped by the predicate — and it is
the second population that matters, because the first has always been one leaked integer away from becoming
the second. A programme that responds to `PT-15` by making identifiers opaque has bought the appearance of
security in exchange for a migration touching every tenant-scoped table, while leaving the actual control
untouched.

**This is the same argument `PT-01` tested, and `PT-01`'s outcome is the reason the argument is credible
rather than the reason it is suspect.** `PT-01` is the case where a tenant identifier supplied by an
attacker in a nested filter did return another tenant's data — and it did so because **the predicate
failed**, not because the identifier was guessable. Ironwood did not need to guess: the aggregate resolver
accepted a value and the row-level policy, evaluating against an unset session variable on that code path,
did not constrain the result. Opaque identifiers would not have prevented it. A tester who has enumerated
one valid identifier has everything they need.

So the acceptance is not "we assessed this as low risk and moved on". It is a positive claim with a
condition attached:

- **The claim.** Cross-tenant access is prevented by the predicate. Identifier opacity is a property of the
  identifier and contributes nothing to the decision the predicate makes.
- **The condition.** The claim is only worth anything if the predicate actually holds on every path. On
  2026-05-22 it did not hold on one, and an organisation accepting `PT-15` on that date would have been
  relying on a control it had just been shown to be defective.
- **What changed.** The predicate moved out of the connection session variable and into the data-access
  layer as a mandatory parameter with a build-time check, deployed 2026-05-29 and retested clean on
  2026-06-11 across the isolation suite and 14 new cases derived from the finding. **The predicate is what
  failed, and the predicate is what was fixed.**

That sequence is why the acceptance was recorded on **2026-06-16** — after the retest — under **DEC-507**
and **ADR-0022**, rather than in the first week of remediation planning. The decision depended on the fix
being demonstrated, not promised.

**The acceptance is conditional and is revisited at the second test.** `TS-05` will be re-run in October
against the remediated model. If that test finds another path on which the predicate does not hold, the
premise of this acceptance has failed and `PT-15` is reopened on its own terms, because the argument
"enumeration is not authorisation" is only available to an organisation whose authorisation control is
sound. Recording the acceptance without recording what would overturn it would have been the weaker
document.

## 8. The second test, and what an annual control looks like inside a six-month window

**A second penetration test is scheduled for October 2026, inside the observation window**, under
**DEC-509** and **ADR-0025**. It discharges the obligation Phase 04 created when it re-scheduled ten annual
controls into the window under ADR-0019: `CNB-C-026` is one of the ten, and re-scheduling an annual test so
that it has an occurrence inside the period commits the programme to actually performing a second
engagement. That commitment was carried as **PR-28** in the Phase 04 risk, assumption, issue and dependency
log, and this is where it is discharged as a plan.

At the vantage the October engagement is **scheduled and not performed**. Nothing in this phase reports its
scope beyond the intent to re-run `TS-05` against the remediated isolation model and to retest `PT-09` and
`PT-14` after the August release, and nothing reports a result, because there is none.

## 9. What a penetration test is, and what it is not

**It is a sample taken by humans at a point in time.** Ironwood tested for nineteen days in May. The
statement supported by a clean retest is *these specific findings no longer reproduce, on these paths, on
this date* — not *the platform is secure*, and not *the isolation model is sound*. 05.12 makes the same
distinction about a log search and it is the same distinction: evidence of absence over a bounded scope is
not proof of absence.

**It is not a control in itself.** `CNB-C-026` is typed **Detective** and sits in **CC4**, the monitoring
family, rather than in CC6 or CC7. It is the mechanism by which CloudNimbus finds out whether its own
control design holds when somebody who is not invested in it attacks it, which is a monitoring activity in
the strict COSO sense — an evaluation of whether the components of internal control are present and
functioning. That is also why its findings feed the risk register: **R-37 was admitted on 2026-05-22 on this
engagement's evidence**, and 03.04's baseline was explicitly left open to additions on evidence for exactly
this reason.

**It is not assurance about the subservice organisations.** Nothing Ironwood tested says anything about
what AWS or Halcyon Identity do. Both are carved out, fourteen complementary subservice organisation
controls are stated instead, and a test of CloudNimbus's configuration of AWS is a test of CloudNimbus.

**What it leaves behind is EC-05**, the alert and finding record: one finding, with its raised timestamp,
assignee, triage disposition and closure timestamp, held in the evidence store. For this engagement the unit
also carries Ironwood's report reference, the remediation change identifier and the retest attestation —
because a finding tracked to closure without the retest artefact is a finding tracked to an assertion.
