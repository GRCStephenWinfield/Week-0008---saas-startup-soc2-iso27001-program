## 1. Purpose

Cryptography at CloudNimbus is asked to do three jobs, and they are not the same job. It protects customer
data at rest so that a copied volume or a mislaid snapshot is not a disclosure. It protects data in transit
so that a network position is not a listening post. And — the job that is easiest to state and hardest to
implement honestly — **it enforces a separation between the people who administer keys and the people who
can read what those keys protect**, so that "encrypted" means something to an insider and not only to an
outsider.

The controls are `CNB-C-057` and `CNB-C-116` for customer data, `CNB-C-056` for transport, `CNB-C-044` for
machine secrets and `CNB-C-050` for destruction, and all of them cite **POL-05**, the Cryptography and Key
Management Policy. The Annex A control they serve at the centre is **A.8.24 use of cryptography**, with
**A.5.14** and **A.8.20** on the transport side; the criteria are **CC6.1**, **CC6.7** and **C1.1**.

## 2. At rest

**Customer data at rest is encrypted with AES-256 under region-scoped customer-managed keys in AWS KMS, one
key per region**, with **envelope encryption for object storage**.

| Region | Serves | Key scope |
|---|---|---|
| `us-east-1` | Primary production for all non-EU-residency tenants | One region-scoped customer-managed key |
| `us-west-2` | Warm standby and disaster recovery for `us-east-1` | One region-scoped customer-managed key |
| `eu-central-1` | Production for the 41 customers with an EU data residency commitment | One EU-scoped customer-managed key |

"One key per region" describes the **root of the hierarchy**, not the key that encrypts each object. Object
storage uses envelope encryption: a data key encrypts the object, the region's customer-managed key encrypts
the data key, and the encrypted data key travels with the object. This is what makes a single region key
practical across **168 in-system S3 buckets** without any one data key protecting an unreasonable volume, and
it is what makes revocation of access to a whole region's data a key-policy operation rather than a
re-encryption project.

The reason the keys are **region-scoped** is a commitment rather than a preference, and it is the clearest
example in the estate of a control chosen so that a mistake fails safe. **SC-04** commits EU-residency
customers' personal data to `eu-central-1`; **O8** is the obligation behind it; **SR-02** requires region
pinning by tenant residency configuration and **SR-03** requires the encryption.

**The mechanism is worth stating accurately, because the loose version of it is wrong and a reader who
tests the loose version will find it so.** A snapshot encrypted under a region-scoped key cannot simply be
copied to another region and left unreadable there: a cross-region copy **requires a destination-region key
supplied at the time of the copy**. Without one the operation does not produce an unreadable artefact — **it
fails, and nothing leaves the region.** With one it succeeds, and the copy is re-encrypted under the key
that was named. **The safeguard is real and it is a different safeguard from the one the loose version
describes**: moving EU data out of `eu-central-1` cannot happen through inattention, because it requires
somebody to name a key in the destination region, which is a deliberate act. And because key policy and
infrastructure are declared as code and deployed through the pipeline, that act is a change with an author
and a reviewer, caught by the Terraform policy gate at `CNB-C-114` before it caught anything else. The
failure mode is a refused operation and a recorded attempt, not a silent copy discovered at a review.
Recovery for `eu-central-1` is intra-region across availability zones for the same commitment, and the
residual that follows from refusing cross-region recovery is disclosed rather than engineered away.

`CNB-C-057` is the control that keeps this true over time: customer data at rest encrypted with customer-
managed keys, EU-scoped keys held in `eu-central-1` for the EU-resident tenants, and **the rotation status of
every key verified monthly**. `CNB-C-116` is its confidentiality-category counterpart and carries the
lifecycle: region-scoped keys at rest, TLS 1.2 or above in transit, and **annual rotation per region with
the rotation and the re-encryption completion recorded**.

## 3. In transit

**TLS 1.2 is the minimum and 1.3 is preferred. Deprecated cipher suites are disabled.**

Every public endpoint terminates TLS 1.2 or above with HTTP Strict Transport Security enabled, and a
**weekly external scan raises a ticket for any endpoint whose configuration falls below the standard**
(`CNB-C-056`). Internal service-to-service traffic within the production VPCs is likewise encrypted, and
interface endpoints keep traffic to AWS services off the public internet.

The weekly scan is the part of that control worth defending. A transport standard is a configuration that
drifts: a load balancer policy is changed for a customer with an old client, a subdomain is stood up by
someone outside the platform team, a managed service default changes at a version upgrade. The scan gives
the standard a population and a cadence.

**It did not, however, find the one transport finding in the May test, and that is the more useful fact.**
**PT-05** — a legacy marketing subdomain permitting a deprecated cipher suite — is a penetration test
finding in the register because **Ironwood found it**, closed on 2026-06-30 and retested on 2026-07-24. Had
the weekly scan found it, it would have been a scanner ticket and not a PT number. The finding is
instructive on two counts. The first is where it sat: the weakest transport configuration in the estate
belonged to the asset nobody thought of as part of the platform, which is the usual place to find one — and
an asset outside the scan's population is invisible to a cadence however short. The second is the point
05.11 §4 makes about the estate findings generally: these are the classes of weakness the automated
mechanisms are supposed to catch, and a human found them instead.

## 4. Key custody, and the separation that gives it meaning

| Role | Count | What it can do |
|---|---|---|
| Key administrators | **3 named individuals** | Administer key policy, lifecycle and rotation |
| Key custodians | **6**, across the three regions | Hold custody responsibilities under POL-05 for the keys in their region |

**No single person can both administer a key and read the data it protects.** That sentence is the control,
and everything else in this section is how it is made true rather than asserted.

Administrative actions on a key and cryptographic use of a key are distinct grants in the key policy. The
three key administrators hold the first and are not members of the roles that hold the second; the service
principals that decrypt customer data hold the second and cannot alter policy, schedule deletion or change
rotation. An administrator can therefore make a key unusable — which is a denial-of-service risk that
belongs to availability and is treated in Phase 06 — and cannot make themselves able to read what it
protects without a policy change that is itself a change: declared as code, peer reviewed, and deployed
through the pipeline, where `CNB-C-080` denies direct console mutation of production infrastructure.

This is the cryptographic instance of the argument 05.03 makes about privileged access. The value of the
separation is not that it is impossible to defeat; it is that **defeating it requires an action that leaves
a record**. A key policy change is a change; a change has an author, a reviewer and a pipeline run.

Six custodians across three regions means two per region, which is the smallest number that survives one
person being unavailable. Naming a larger population would have made the roster comfortable and the control
weaker, because custody that everybody holds is custody nobody exercises.

## 5. Rotation, and what rotation is actually for

Keys rotate **annually**, per region, with the rotation and the re-encryption completion recorded
(`CNB-C-116`), and the rotation status of every key is verified **monthly** (`CNB-C-057`).

**The word "rotation" carries two different operations and `CNB-C-116` describes the larger one, so this
chapter says which.** Automatic key rotation as a managed service offers it generates new backing material
on a schedule and **retains the old material so that existing ciphertext remains decryptable**. It
therefore re-encrypts nothing, and it produces no completion record, because there is no completion to
record. `CNB-C-116` requires a re-encryption completion, which means what CloudNimbus performs is a
**planned re-key**: new key material for the region, followed by a bulk pass that re-encrypts every data
key under it so that nothing in the region continues to depend on the superseded material. Envelope
encryption is what makes that feasible — the pass re-wraps data keys rather than rewriting object bodies —
and it is still a scheduled bulk operation across 168 in-system S3 buckets and the in-system Aurora
clusters, with a start, an end and a recorded completion. **It is a project each year, not a configuration
toggle**, and describing it as the toggle would have claimed an artefact the toggle does not produce.

It is worth being equally exact about what the annual re-key buys, because rotation is routinely described
as though it were a compromise mitigation. It is not, or not principally. If a key were compromised, the
response would be immediate re-keying and an incident, not a wait until the anniversary. What the annual
operation buys is that **the key lifecycle is exercised**: the organisation demonstrates each year that it
can re-key a region and complete re-encryption without an outage and without losing access to old data. An
organisation that has never done it does not know whether it can.

**The occurrence inside the window is not an anniversary that happens to fall there.** `CNB-C-116` is one
of the **ten annual controls re-scheduled into the observation window** under **DEC-409** and **ADR-0019**,
and its re-scheduled occurrence is **2026-11**. The consequence is a commitment: having moved the date to
give the control a population inside the period, CloudNimbus has to perform the re-key in November. That is
the same kind of commitment 05.11 records for `CNB-C-026`, the other of the ten this phase touches, where
re-scheduling the annual test obliges the programme to commission a second engagement in October. Both are
disclosures that create work rather than remove it.

The monthly verification is the detective half and exists for a different failure: a key whose rotation
schedule was quietly changed, or a new key created outside the standard that nobody scheduled. **The window
holds six monthly verifications, one for each of July to December, and exactly one re-key, in November.**
The annual figure and the window figure are different numbers and are kept apart here for that reason.
**04.11 makes the general point about annual controls and a six-month window** — a population of one, where
a single late occurrence is a hundred per cent deviation rate — and the position here is simply that the
monthly check is what a sampler will mostly be sampling and the November re-key is the one that cannot slip.

## 6. Machine secrets

Service accounts and machine identities draw their credentials from the secrets manager, which **rotates
them every ninety days**, and no service account holds an interactive shell or a console password
(`CNB-C-044`). Workload identity on the in-system EKS clusters is federated to IAM roles for service
accounts, so a running pod holds no long-lived cloud credential at all.

The ninety-day rotation matters less than the sourcing. A credential that lives in the secrets manager can
be rotated, revoked and attributed; a credential that lives in an environment variable set two years ago in
a configuration file cannot. **PT-13** in the May test — an unused access key older than ninety days on a
service account — is what the residue of the old pattern looks like, and it was closed on 2026-06-24 and
retested on 2026-07-24.

## 7. Destruction, and what cryptography does not do

Every managed device and every decommissioned storage volume is erased through the documented destruction
workflow before disposal or re-use, and the certificate of erasure is retained against the asset record
(`CNB-C-050`), serving **A.7.14** and **A.8.10**.

Key scoping supports deletion and does not substitute for it. Making a body of ciphertext unreadable by
destroying the key that protects it is a real property — but it operates at the granularity of a key, and a
key protects a region rather than a tenant or a data subject. **Deletion at the granularity the retention schedule requires is a data operation**, performed by
the deletion orchestrator against every store, and it carries the residue arithmetic that RT-07 and RT-08
produce. That whole subject — the retention rules, the deletion certificate and the disclosed residue —
belongs to Phase 07, and this chapter deliberately does not reach into it.

## 8. What this leaves behind

| Control | Artefact | Evidence class | Sampling unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| `CNB-C-057` | Monthly key rotation status verification across every key in the three regions | EC-04 | One monthly verification: keys evaluated, rotation state, exceptions raised |
| `CNB-C-116` | Annual rotation record with re-encryption completion, per region | EC-04 | One rotation event for one region |
| `CNB-C-056` | Weekly external transport scan and the tickets it raises | EC-05 | One scan; one ticket for a non-conforming endpoint |
| `CNB-C-044` | Secrets manager rotation records | EC-04 | One credential rotation event |
| `CNB-C-050` | Certificate of erasure held against the asset record | EC-16 | One erased device or volume |
| Key policy | The peer-reviewed, pipeline-deployed policy change | EC-03 | One merged change to a key policy |

The last row is the one a tester should ask for first. It is the artefact that shows whether the separation
in §4 is a configuration or a claim, and it is produced by the change process rather than by the
cryptography.

## 9. Limits, stated

**AWS operates KMS; CloudNimbus configures it.** AWS is a subservice organisation and is **carved out**, with
eleven complementary subservice organisation controls stated. Nothing in this chapter asserts anything about
how AWS generates, stores or protects key material, and AWS's own assurance reports cover AWS's controls
rather than CloudNimbus's use of them. The assertions here are about key policy, key scope, rotation
configuration and the roles that hold each grant — all of which are CloudNimbus's.

**Encryption at rest is not a control against an authorised reader.** A service that is entitled to decrypt
will decrypt, which is why the confidentiality argument in this portfolio rests on the tenant predicate in
05.04 and not on the cipher. Encryption at rest answers a different threat: the copied volume, the
mis-replicated snapshot, the decommissioned disk.

**No claim is made about any cryptographic validation regime**, and none is needed for either deliverable.
The parameters stated here — AES-256, TLS 1.2 minimum and 1.3 preferred, deprecated suites disabled, annual
rotation — are the parameters, and a reader should test them rather than infer others.

**One month is one month.** July 2026 contains one monthly key verification and four weekly transport scans.
Nothing in this chapter asserts how these controls will behave across the observation window.
