## 1. Purpose

CC6.1, CC6.2 and CC6.3 are the criteria that ask who may hold access, how it is granted, how it is changed
and how it is removed. **Thirteen controls answer the first, seven the second and nine the third — and the
distinct population is 21, not 29.** The three counts are right and they overlap: `CNB-C-034`, for one
example, answers CC6.1 and CC6.3 and is counted in both. Adding criterion-level counts to arrive at a
control count is the same category error this portfolio names elsewhere — treating overlapping sets as a
partition, which is what ML-3 recorded about the vendor register and what 04.03 warns about mapping
generally. **A control can serve several criteria; a criterion is served by several controls; neither
direction is a division.** This document describes the identity estate those **21 rows** configure — with
privileged access held back for 05.03, because privileged access is a different problem and its separation
is the point of the design.

Two propositions run underneath everything here. The first is that **identity is the only perimeter a
remote-first company has**: 187 staff across 31 US states and 4 countries, no data centre, and a Denver
suite that is a meeting space. The second is that **an access decision is worth what its lifecycle is
worth**. A perfectly configured authentication factor attached to an account that should have been closed in
March is not a control; it is a strong lock on an open door.

## 2. Two identity planes, three populations

| Plane | Population | Authenticated by | Principal flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer plane — end users | ~1.24M employees of 640 customer organisations | **Halcyon Identity, Inc.** (SUB-02) | DF-01, DF-05, DF-07 |
| Customer plane — tenant administrators | Named administrators within each customer | Halcyon Identity, under the tenant's configuration | **DF-02** |
| Staff plane — CloudNimbus staff | 187 people, 164 of them inside the people component | The workforce identity provider | DF-08 |

**There are two planes and three rows, and the table is not a partition of the planes.** The planes are the
customer plane and the staff plane; tenant administrators are a distinct population **inside** the customer
plane, given their own row because their provisioning, their entitlements and the boundary the customer
owns are all different from an ordinary end user's, and §6 is about that difference.

**The administrative flow is DF-02, not DF-01.** DF-01 is clock-in from the mobile app, and 02.04 marks it
as crossing Halcyon Identity. DF-02 — the employer administrator configuring pay rules through the admin
console into `platform-metadata` — is one of the two flows in the register of twelve that carry no personal
data, and 02.04 marks it as **not crossing a subservice organisation**. That is a boundary about where the
flow was drawn rather than a contradiction of the column beside it: the registered flow begins at the
gateway with an already-authenticated token, and Phase 02 recorded the authentication step against the
end-user flows rather than against the configuration write. **This chapter follows the register.** Where a
customer's administrators sit in its Halcyon tenant they are authenticated there like any other end user;
nothing here claims a Halcyon control on DF-02, and none of the three CSOCs is asserted against it.

Halcyon Identity is a **subservice organisation and it is carved out**. Nothing in this document asserts
anything about how Halcyon performs its own controls; **three complementary subservice organisation
controls** are stated instead, and 02.10 carries them. What CloudNimbus is responsible for is its
configuration of the relationship — which claims it trusts, which assurance levels it requires, what happens
to a session when the assertion is stale, and what the platform does when Halcyon is unavailable, which is
**R-04**, the one risk in the register treated by sharing.

The staff plane is CloudNimbus's own and is where the rest of this chapter sits. It is worth stating that
**DF-08 carries no personal data of end users**: it is the flow by which CloudNimbus staff authenticate into
production, and it is one of only two flows in the register of twelve that carry none.

## 3. Authentication — the factor decision, and where it does not apply

**Phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication is mandatory for every path into production.** The factor
is WebAuthn, bound to hardware the person holds, and it is required at the identity provider for every
internal user and every tenant administrator by `CNB-C-032`, with the authentication standard itself —
passphrase length, screening against known-breached credentials, lockout thresholds, and phishing-resistant
factors for every privileged role — enforced by `CNB-C-045`. **DEC-501**, taken by Wes Delacroix on
**2026-04-28**, is the decision that made it mandatory rather than encouraged.

**Time-based one-time passwords are accepted for corporate SaaS and nowhere else.** That sentence is a
concession and is written as one. A time-based code is a shared secret typed into whatever page asked for
it, and a relay proxy in front of a convincing sign-in page collects the code and the session together. It
is materially better than a password alone and materially worse than a bound credential, and the honest
description of the position is that CloudNimbus tolerates the weaker factor where the blast radius is a
single SaaS tenant and refuses it wherever the path leads to customer data.

The boundary between "corporate SaaS" and "a path into production" is therefore a control boundary and not a
convenience. It is enforced because production is reachable only through the zero-trust access proxy
(`CNB-C-053`), which authenticates the person, checks the device and authorises per application, and because
there is no VPN concentrator and no administrative interface exposed to the public internet for the weaker
factor to reach. A reader who wants to test the claim should not test the MFA policy; they should test
whether any route into production bypasses the proxy.

Authentication is also **device-bound**. Conditional access refuses any sign-in from a device the mobile
device management platform does not report as compliant, which means an authentication decision and an
endpoint posture decision are made together rather than in sequence. 05.07 describes what makes a device
compliant and what happens when it stops being so; the relevant point here is that the identity plane
consumes that signal as an input, so a device that falls past its patch target loses access without anybody
revoking anything.

## 4. One identity provider for ninety-six applications

CloudNimbus runs **96 third-party SaaS applications** internally. All of them authenticate through the
corporate identity provider under `CNB-C-033`, local application accounts are prohibited, and a monthly
discovery scan reports any application authenticating outside it.

The scan is the part that matters. A single sign-on standard with no discovery is a statement about the
applications somebody remembered to enrol, and the failure mode is not defiance but procurement: a team buys
a tool with a corporate card, the tool has its own login, and eleven months later a leaver still has it. The
scan gives the standard a population it can be measured against, and it feeds `CNB-C-093` when the ninety-
seventh application appears, because a cloud service is registered and reviewed against the baseline before
customer data is placed in it.

## 5. Joiner, mover, leaver — four controls, not one

The lifecycle is driven from the **HR system of record**, and the design decision worth naming is that
**deprovisioning is triggered by the termination event rather than by a ticket**. A ticket is a request
somebody has to remember to raise; an event is emitted by the system that already knows.

| Stage | Control | Mechanism | Cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Joiner | `CNB-C-037` | Provisioning only from an approved onboarding ticket recording the hiring manager's approval and the role profile; the identity provider group assignment is generated from the role profile rather than typed | Continuous |
| Mover | `CNB-C-043` | Entitlements expressed as fourteen named roles; anything outside the role model needs a system owner's approved exception, expires automatically at ninety days, and appears on the monthly exception report | Continuous, reported monthly |
| Leaver | `CNB-C-038` | The HR termination event triggers automated session revocation and account disablement at the identity provider **within four hours**, with the completion timestamp written back to the offboarding ticket | Continuous |
| Detective check | `CNB-C-039` | A weekly reconciliation compares active identity provider accounts against active worker records and raises a ticket for every account with no matching active worker | Weekly |
| Certification | `CNB-C-040` | Each quarter every system owner certifies the entitlement extract for the production, privileged and customer-data-bearing systems they own, in the GRC platform, with revocations applied and evidenced before the review closes | Quarterly |

Cutting the lifecycle into four rows rather than one has two consequences the library wanted. A deviation
lands on a single row instead of contaminating the whole lifecycle, so a late certification does not imply a
failed deprovisioning. And the weekly reconciliation is a **genuinely independent check** on the automated
deprovisioning rather than a description of it: `CNB-C-039` compares two populations from two systems, so it
can only pass if the automation in `CNB-C-038` actually ran.

The mover case is the weakest link in every access model and it is worth saying why it is weak here too. A
person moving from a quota-carrying sales role into Customer Success crosses the boundary of the people
component, and the access tier model in 02.05 treats a move as a change of access rather than a change of
manager. The role-derived group assignment in `CNB-C-037` means the new access is generated; it does not by
itself mean the old access is removed. That removal is what `CNB-C-043`'s ninety-day exception expiry and
`CNB-C-040`'s quarterly certification exist to catch, and a mover with accumulated entitlements is precisely
what a certification is for.

## 6. Tenant administrators and the boundary the customer owns

A tenant administrator account is created only by an existing tenant administrator of that tenant, or by
Customer Success against a signed authorisation from the customer's named contact retained in the CRM
(`CNB-C-042`). The customer's side of that arrangement is a **complementary user entity control**: CloudNimbus
cannot know that a customer's departing HR director should no longer administer the tenant, and it does not
pretend to. Eleven CUECs are disclosed and each names the criteria it is necessary for, as DC6 requires.
This is the clearest case in the estate where a criterion is met by CloudNimbus's controls **and** the user
entity's together, and describing it any other way would overstate the description.

## 7. Machine identity

Service accounts and machine identities draw their credentials from the secrets manager, which rotates them
every ninety days, and **no service account holds an interactive shell or a console password** (`CNB-C-044`).
Workload identity on the three in-system EKS clusters is federated to IAM roles for service accounts, so a
pod's permissions derive from its identity rather than from the node it landed on, and no long-lived cloud
credential is issued to a running workload.

The prohibition on interactive access for service accounts is the load-bearing half. A service account that
can be logged into is a shared administrator account with a job title, and it defeats attribution in exactly
the population where attribution matters most. One of the penetration test's low findings — **PT-13**, an
unused access key older than ninety days on a service account — is the residue this control exists to
prevent, and 05.11 records that it was closed on 2026-06-24 and retested on 2026-07-24.

## 8. The first certification inside the window

The quarterly entitlement certification runs under **CAL-07** in the assurance calendar. The certification
completed on **2026-07-27** is the first performed under `CNB-C-040` in the GRC platform and **the first
access review to fall inside the observation window**. Its output is an **EC-01** access review package: for
one system, for one quarter, an entitlement extract, a per-line decision, the revocation tickets arising,
and the reviewer's sign-off with a timestamp.

That form is the direct answer to **ML-1**. The 2025 Type I management letter recorded that access review
evidence was retained as email threads and screenshots — a control that worked and left nothing a sampler
could use. The distinction between the old form and the new one is not thoroughness; it is that the new form
has a **unit**. A sampler can select one system-quarter and test it. A mailbox has no units in it.

**One certification is one unit, and the window will hold three — which is worth deriving, because the
obvious answer is two and the obvious answer is wrong.** 01.11 schedules **CAL-07 quarterly, at 2026-03,
2026-06, 2026-09 and 2026-12**, of which September and December fall inside the observation window. The
certification of **2026-07-27** is neither of them: it is an **additional occurrence**, run when the
entitlement certification moved into the GRC platform under `CNB-C-040` rather than on the calendar's
cycle. Nothing was re-based and nobody moved the cycle. **Three occurrences, not two**, and counting the
July one as though it had replaced a scheduled point would understate the population Phase 08 samples by a
third.

## 10. July, and what it does not establish

Three access provisioning requests were raised in July 2026, and each carried a recorded manager approval
**before** provisioning. One access review was completed. Those are the facts.

They are also a population of three and a population of one, and the smallness cuts both ways. It is too
small to be reassuring, and it is small enough that a single failure in a later month would be a large
proportion of the whole. The criterion the service auditor will test is **CC6.2** — registration and
authorisation of new users prior to issuing credentials, and removal when access is no longer required —
across six months of joiners and leavers, not across July's three. Nothing in this chapter is a claim about
that period.
