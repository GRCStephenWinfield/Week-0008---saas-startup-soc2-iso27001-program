## 1. Purpose

This is the reference chapter for the SOC 2 side of the programme. It states what a SOC 2
examination is, what it is performed against, what it produces, and — with as much emphasis as a
document can carry — what it is not. Later phases assume every position taken here. Where a
practitioner outside this programme would use looser language, this document uses the precise term
and explains why the precision is load-bearing rather than pedantic.

## 2. What a SOC 2 examination actually is

A SOC 2 engagement is an **attestation examination** performed by an independent **CPA firm** under
the AICPA attestation standards. Two sections govern it: **AT-C section 105**, which sets out the
concepts common to all attestation engagements, and **AT-C section 205**, which since **SSAE No.
21** is titled *Assertion-Based Examination Engagements*. The practitioner obtains reasonable
assurance and expresses an **opinion**. That is the product. There is no certificate, no certifying
body, no registrar and no logo of achievement.

The title of AT-C 205 is worth reading rather than skipping, because it is the reason Section II of
the report exists. SSAE No. 21 split the examination standards in two. **AT-C section 205** covers
engagements in which a **responsible party makes an assertion** about the subject matter and the
practitioner reports on that assertion. **AT-C section 206** covers **direct examination
engagements**, in which the practitioner measures or evaluates the subject matter itself without a
written assertion from the responsible party. **A SOC 2 examination is an AT-C 205 engagement and
not an AT-C 206 one.** Management asserts; the auditor examines the assertion. That is why
management's written assertion is a precondition of the engagement rather than a courtesy, why
obligation O12 in 01.11 records it as something CloudNimbus owes Ashcombe and Doyle, and why the
CEO rather than the compliance function signs it. Describing AT-C 205 as governing examination
engagements generally would blur a distinction the engagement's whole shape depends on.

The consequences of taking that seriously are practical, not cosmetic. **There is no such thing as
"SOC 2 certification", and nobody is "SOC 2 certified" or "SOC 2 compliant."** CloudNimbus will not
use those phrases in a sales conversation, a questionnaire response, a trust centre page or a board
paper. The correct formulations are "a SOC 2 Type II examination report" and, where the outcome is
being described, "an unmodified opinion." The reason to be strict about this is not vocabulary
hygiene. A buyer who has been told a supplier is "certified" believes a pass/fail judgement has
been made by an authority. What has actually happened is that a CPA has expressed a professional
opinion, in a restricted-use report, about whether specified controls were suitably designed and
operated effectively to meet specified criteria over a specified period. Those are different
assurances, and misdescribing the first as the second is a misrepresentation that the programme
would then have to defend.

The engagement for CloudNimbus is with **Ashcombe and Doyle LLP**, engagement partner Meredith
Vance, CPA, with senior manager Dev Ramanathan. The independence architecture around that
appointment is 01.09's subject.

## 3. The criteria and the description criteria

Two separate AICPA publications govern the content of the report, and they do different jobs.

The **trust services criteria** are set out in **TSP section 100, *2017 Trust Services Criteria for
Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, and Privacy* (with revised points of
focus — 2022)**. These are the criteria against which the suitability of design and operating
effectiveness of controls is evaluated. The 2022 revision changed the points of focus, not the
criteria themselves.

The **description criteria** are set out in **DC section 200, *2018 Description Criteria for a
Description of a Service Organization's System in a SOC 2 Report***, comprising **DC1 through
DC9**. These govern whether management's description of the system is presented in accordance with
the description criteria — a separate matter from whether the controls met the trust services
criteria, and separately opined on. A description can fail DC even where the controls are sound;
that is one of the ways a report gets modified for a reason that has nothing to do with control
failure. The AICPA Guide, *SOC 2® Reporting on an Examination of Controls at a Service
Organization*, provides the implementation guidance both parties work from.

## 4. The criteria in scope

CloudNimbus is examining all five trust services categories. The selection argument is 01.05's; the
arithmetic is here.

| Security — Common Criteria | CC1.1–CC1.5 (5) · CC2.1–CC2.3 (3) · CC3.1–CC3.4 (4) · CC4.1–CC4.2 (2) · CC5.1–CC5.3 (3) · CC6.1–CC6.8 (8) · CC7.1–CC7.5 (5) · CC8.1 (1) · CC9.1–CC9.2 (2) | 33 |
| Availability | A1.1–A1.3 | 3 |
| Processing Integrity | PI1.1–PI1.5 | 5 |
| Confidentiality | C1.1–C1.2 | 2 |

| **Total in scope** | | **61** |

The Common Criteria are the floor. They apply to every SOC 2 examination regardless of which other
categories are selected, which is why the Security category is never optional. The other four
categories are additive: each brings its own criteria and each is examined alongside, not instead
of, the Common Criteria.

## 5. The COSO relationship

**CC1 through CC5 are organised against the 17 COSO principles** from the 2013 *Internal
Control — Integrated Framework*: control environment, communication and information, risk
assessment, monitoring activities, and control activities. Appendix A of the trust services
criteria carries the mapping. CC6 through CC9 are supplementary criteria that address logical and
physical access, system operations, change management, and risk mitigation.

This is worth understanding rather than memorising, because it explains why so much of CC1 is about
things engineers do not think of as security: board oversight, organisational structure, commitment
to competence, accountability. Hannah Brill, Head of People, owns most of the CC1 population
precisely because hiring, background screening, onboarding, offboarding and training are where the
COSO control environment principles are actually operated. It also explains, in 01.06, why some
Common Criteria have no clean Annex A analogue — the COSO material has no direct counterpart in a
control set built for information security.

## 6. Points of focus are not requirements

**Points of focus are considerations that may assist management and the service auditor in
evaluating whether a criterion has been met.** An entity need not address every point of focus. An
entity may meet a criterion in a way no point of focus describes. And a service auditor may not
treat the points of focus as a checklist and conclude that a criterion is unmet because one of them
was not implemented.

This is the most consistently misunderstood feature of the trust services criteria, and the damage
it does is specific. Treating the points of focus as a control list produces control sets that are
complete against the wrong target. A worked example from this programme makes it concrete.

**CC6.1** concerns the entity implementing **logical access security software, infrastructure and
architectures** over protected information assets to protect them from security events. Read
carefully, that is a criterion about the *architecture* of protection, not merely about who is on
an access list — restricting access to authorised persons and removing it when it is no longer
appropriate are the subject matter of CC6.2 and CC6.3. CC6.1 asks whether the mechanisms by which
protected information is kept separate from those who should not reach it have been built.

Its points of focus address identifying and managing the inventory of information assets,
restricting logical access, identifying and authenticating users, considering network segmentation,
managing points of access, managing identification and authentication for infrastructure and
software, using encryption to protect data, and protecting encryption keys. Every one of those is a
sensible thing to consider. **None of them names multi-tenant data segregation.**

CloudNimbus is a multi-tenant platform holding compensation data for 640 employers, and the
architecture that keeps one employer's data away from another is a row-level security predicate
applied inside a shared database rather than a network boundary between tenants. An organisation
that wrote its CC6.1 control set by walking down the points of focus would produce an asset
inventory, an access-restriction control, an authentication control, a network segmentation
control, an encryption-in-transit control and a key-management control — a complete, tidy, entirely
defensible-looking answer to CC6.1. It would also have nothing whatsoever to say about whether one
tenant can read another tenant's data, which for this platform is the single most important
question CC6.1 asks, because tenant separation *is* the logical access architecture over the
protected information assets. A control set built from the points of focus would be complete
against the wrong target and would look complete while being so.

That is why the penetration test commissioned for May 2026 (DEC-109, CAL-11, EP-06) is scoped to
include a **multi-tenancy isolation test** alongside the external, internal and cloud, web
application and mobile application testing. The scope was written that way deliberately, precisely
because the points of focus would not have prompted it and because an assumption of this
consequence — that row-level security is uniformly enforced across every data access path, recorded
as AS-02 — is not something to carry into a six-month observation window on the strength of a
design document. What the test finds belongs to the phase that reports it. What belongs here is the
reasoning that put the test in scope: the criterion was read, the architecture was identified, and
the test was designed against the criterion rather than against the list of considerations
underneath it.

The rule this programme adopts, and which the unified control library in Phase 04 enforces, is
therefore: **design controls against the criterion, then use the points of focus as a completeness
check, never as the specification.**

## 7. The trust services criteria contain no controls

There is no published list of "SOC 2 controls," because the trust services criteria contain none.
Management designs, implements, operates and describes the controls. The service auditor evaluates
whether those controls, as described, are suitably designed and — in a Type II — operated
effectively to provide reasonable assurance that the applicable criteria were met.

The practical consequence for this programme is that any vendor product, template library or
consultant deliverable presented as "the SOC 2 controls" is somebody's opinion about how a criterion
might be met, not an authority. Solstice Trust Advisors provides control design support and policy
drafting under exactly that understanding: their output is an input to CloudNimbus's own control
design, and CloudNimbus accepts management responsibility for all of it.

## 8. Type I and Type II

| | Type I | Type II |
|---|---|---|
| Subject matter | Suitability of design of controls | Suitability of design **and** operating effectiveness of controls |
| Period | **As of a specified date** | **Throughout a specified period** |
| Tests of operating effectiveness | None | Yes, with sampling across the period |
| Test exceptions possible | **No** | Yes, disclosed in Section IV |

The distinction that matters most, and which 01.04 develops at length, is the last row. **A Type I
cannot produce test exceptions, because a Type I performs no tests of operating effectiveness.**
There is nothing to deviate from. An entity coming out of a clean Type I therefore knows that its
controls looked well designed on one day and knows nothing at all about whether they operate.

CloudNimbus's Type II observation window is **2026-07-01 to 2026-12-31**, a six-month period. A
control that is not operating on 2026-07-01 cannot be tested for the full period, which makes the
window's opening date the single hardest constraint in the programme roadmap (01.12).

## 9. The anatomy of the report

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| I | The independent service auditor's report — the opinion |
| II | Management's assertion |
| III | Management's description of the system |
| IV | Applicable trust services criteria, related controls, tests performed by the service auditor, and results of those tests |
| V | Other information provided by management |

**Section V is not covered by the service auditor's opinion.** It is where management may place
information it wishes to convey — context, responses to matters disclosed in Section IV, planned
remediation — and readers must understand that nothing in it has been examined. It is useful and it
is unaudited, and confusing the two is a reader error that a well-drafted Section V heading
prevents.

Sections II and III are management's, not the auditor's. The assertion in Section II is signed by
management, and in this programme the CEO signs it. What she is asserting is that the description
is presented in accordance with the description criteria, and that the controls stated in the
description were suitably designed and operated effectively throughout the period to provide
reasonable assurance that the applicable trust services criteria were met. That is a substantial
statement, made by management on its own knowledge, before any opinion exists.

## 10. The opinion, and what exceptions do to it

There are four forms of opinion: **unmodified, qualified, adverse, and disclaimer of opinion.**

Test exceptions identified during the examination are **disclosed in Section IV** alongside the
control and the test that produced them. **Exceptions do not automatically modify the opinion.** The
service auditor modifies the opinion only if, in professional judgement, the deviations mean an
applicable trust services criterion was not met. A small number of deviations in a large population,
mitigated by compensating detective controls, may leave the auditor able to conclude that the
criterion was met and the opinion unmodified — with the deviations still fully disclosed, because
disclosure and modification are different decisions.

This programme states that plainly and early because the alternative is corrosive. An organisation
that believes any exception is a failure will manage towards a report with no exceptions, and the
ways to achieve that are to narrow the population, to weaken the control so it cannot be deviated
from, or to argue with the auditor. All three are worse than a disclosed deviation.

## 11. Restricted use, and the bridge letter

**A SOC 2 report is restricted-use.** Its use is intended for the management of the service
organisation, for **user entities during the period covered by the report**, and for other
specified parties who have sufficient knowledge and understanding of the service organisation, the
nature of the service, internal control and its limitations, the applicable criteria, and the risks
that may threaten the achievement of the service organisation's objectives. **It is not a
general-use document and it will not be published.** CloudNimbus provides it under NDA on request
and, for the 118 enterprise-tier customers, within 30 days of issuance under obligation O2. The
trust centre says the report exists and how to request it; it does not host it.

A **bridge letter** — sometimes called a gap letter — covers the period between the end of the
report period and a user entity's own reporting date. It is issued by **management**, it is
**unaudited**, and it carries **no auditor opinion**. It typically states that management is not
aware of material changes to the system or to the controls in the intervening period. Customers ask
for it constantly, and the risk is that they read it as an extension of the report. Ana-Sofia Cruz
owns the standard language that says, in the letter itself, that it is not.

## 12. Subservice organisations, CUECs and CSOCs

Where another organisation performs controls that are necessary, in combination with the service
organisation's own controls, to meet the applicable trust services criteria, that organisation is a
**subservice organisation**. Two methods exist. Under the **carve-out method**, the subservice
organisation's controls are excluded from the description and the description states the
**complementary subservice organisation controls (CSOCs)** the service organisation assumes are in
place. Under the **inclusive method**, the subservice organisation's controls are included in the
description and tested.

CloudNimbus carves out both of its subservice organisations — Amazon Web Services and Halcyon
Identity, Inc. — under DEC-108. Separately, **complementary user entity controls (CUECs)** are
controls the description assumes the *customer* operates; a multi-tenant platform where the
employer administers its own users depends on several. The specific CUEC and CSOC counts are
determined as part of the system description and are stated in Phase 02, not here.

The classification test is easy to state and easy to get wrong in both directions: most vendors are
**not** subservice organisations. Volume of spend, criticality to the business and access to data
do not make a vendor a subservice organisation; performing controls necessary to meet the criteria
does. 01.09 sets out the register arithmetic and why the prior Type I management letter treats this
as a first-order problem.
