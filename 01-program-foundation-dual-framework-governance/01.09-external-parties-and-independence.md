## 1. Purpose

Eight external parties stand between CloudNimbus and its two assurance deliverables, and they fall into
four groups rather than three. **Two** — EP-07 and EP-08 — are suppliers whose controls form part of the
service itself. **Four** — EP-01, EP-03, EP-04 and EP-05 — are professional firms whose value depends
entirely on their being kept apart from one another. **One**, EP-06, is a testing firm whose independence
question is internal rather than inter-firm: it must report past the people whose work it tests. And
**one**, EP-02, is a predecessor with no continuing role. Two plus four plus one plus one is eight. This
document names them, states the independence boundary that applies to each, and defends the architecture
that keeps those boundaries intact — including the part of it that cost money.

It is approved by the Chair of the Audit &amp; Risk Committee rather than by management, because the
committee is where an arrangement that would compromise a boundary is meant to be refused, and a document
about independence approved solely by the people whose convenience it constrains would be worth less than
the paper it is written on. The limits of that safeguard are set out honestly in 01.07 §4.1: the ARC has
one voting member, and its practical force is that of an independent non-executive chair with a direct line
to the outsourced internal auditor rather than that of a committee a listed company would recognise. The
approval signature on this document means Lorraine Kessler has read it and will be asked about it, which is
a real thing and a smaller thing than the word "committee" suggests.

## 3. The independence architecture

Four of these parties — EP-01, EP-03, EP-04 and EP-05 — could, in a permissive reading of the market, have
been one or two firms. They are four because each of the boundaries below is real, and because collapsing
any pair of them removes the reason the resulting deliverable would be worth anything.

### 3.1 Why the readiness advisor cannot be the internal auditor

Solstice Trust Advisors is engaged to help CloudNimbus design controls, draft policies and build the
evidence architecture. That is consultancy, it is legitimate, and a company doing this for the first time
across two frameworks with 4.6 FTE-equivalents would be reckless to attempt it without help.

Clause 9.2 asks a different question: does the ISMS conform to the organisation's own requirements and to
the requirements of the standard, and is it effectively implemented and maintained? An auditor who designed
the thing being audited cannot answer that question, not because of dishonesty but because of the structure
of the judgement. The person who chose an approach evaluates it against the criteria that made it seem
right. Clause 9.2.2 requires audits to be conducted in a manner that ensures objectivity and impartiality of
the audit process, and an advisor auditing its own design specification fails that on its face. Solstice is
therefore barred from the clause 9.2 programme entirely — not merely from auditing the specific artefacts it
produced, because in a programme this integrated the artefacts it produced touch nearly everything.

Camberwell Risk Partners performs the clause 9.2 audit and has not advised on the design of what it audits.
The boundary is a term of the engagement, and Marisol Vega is accountable for holding it.

### 3.2 Why the certification body cannot consult

Northgate Certification Services is accredited by ANAB, and accreditation is what makes the certificate mean
anything to a customer who has never heard of Northgate. Accreditation is granted against ISO/IEC 17021-1,
the requirements for bodies providing audit and certification of management systems, supplemented for
information security by ISO/IEC 27006-1:2024.

ISO/IEC 17021-1's impartiality requirements prohibit a certification body from providing management system
consultancy to the organisations it certifies, and impose a cooling-off period before a body may certify a
management system on which it or a related body provided consultancy. The prohibition extends to providing
internal audits to a certified client. The rationale is the same as in 3.1 and the effect is stricter: a
certification body that advises is not merely making a poor judgement, it is putting its accreditation at
risk, and with it the value of every certificate it has ever issued.

The practical consequence for CloudNimbus is a cadence that feels unhelpfully sparse. Contact with Northgate
occurs at Stage 1 and at Stage 2 and at no other time. There is no monthly checkpoint, no pre-read of the
Statement of Applicability, no informal call to ask whether an approach would be acceptable. Any of those
would be consultancy in substance whatever it was called in the calendar invite, and the programme has
turned down the opportunity more than once. The gap is filled by Solstice, whose entire purpose is to be the
party that *can* be asked, and by Camberwell, whose findings arrive before Stage 2 rather than after it.

It is worth stating plainly, since the sentence appears in vendor marketing constantly: **ISO does not
certify anyone.** ISO publishes the standard. An accredited certification body grants the certificate. No
document in this programme will say that ISO certified CloudNimbus.

### 3.3 Why the CPA firm cannot design the controls

Ashcombe &amp; Doyle LLP will perform the SOC 2 Type II examination and issue the opinion in Section I. Its
independence is governed by the AICPA Code of Professional Conduct, and specifically by the nonattest
services provisions at ET section 1.295, which apply where a firm performs services for an attest client
beyond the attest engagement itself.

The rule that matters here is that the firm may not perform management responsibilities for an attest
client. Designing, implementing or operating the controls that will subsequently be examined is a management
responsibility in the plainest sense: it is the client's system, and if the auditor built it, the auditor is
evaluating its own work. Where a firm performs any permitted nonattest service, the client must designate an
individual with suitable skill, knowledge and experience to oversee it, must make all significant judgements
and accept responsibility for the results, and must evaluate the adequacy of what was delivered. If the
client cannot do those things, the service cannot be performed without impairing independence, however
harmless it looks.

This is the boundary that erodes fastest in practice, because it erodes conversationally. An auditor is
asked whether a control "would be acceptable"; the answer shapes the control; the control is then examined
by the person who shaped it. CloudNimbus's monthly service-auditor checkpoint from 2026-04 exists to keep
the examination on schedule and to surface scope and evidence-format questions early, and its agenda is
constrained accordingly. Ashcombe &amp; Doyle may say what evidence would be sufficient to test a control
CloudNimbus has designed. They may not say what control CloudNimbus should design. Rahul Bhargava keeps the
minutes and Karim Haddad is responsible for stopping the conversation when it crosses the line.

### 3.4 What accepting management responsibility concretely requires

"CloudNimbus accepts management responsibility" is a sentence that will appear in an engagement letter and
mean nothing unless someone can point to what it obliges. Two people carry it.

**Karim Haddad** is the designated individual with suitable skill, knowledge and experience for the purposes
of ET 1.295. That is not a formality attached to the most senior available name; it is the assertion that he
possesses the competence to evaluate the adequacy of what an external firm delivers and to take
responsibility for the result. Concretely, it requires that every control design produced with Solstice's
help is reviewed and adopted by him on CloudNimbus's own reasoning, not accepted because a consultant
recommended it; that the Statement of Applicability's justifications are ones he can defend in his own words
at Stage 2; that where Ashcombe &amp; Doyle raise a question about evidence, the answering position is
CloudNimbus's; and that he does not treat any advisor's template as a decision already made. Documents
adopted without that review are documents the company cannot defend under challenge, and an auditor asking
"why did you decide this?" is entitled to an answer that does not begin "our advisor suggested".

**Marisol Vega** owns the engagements and therefore owns the conditions attached to them. She approves the
scope of each external engagement, holds the four-way separation as a contractual term rather than a
convention, and is the person who has to say no when a firm proposes a broader remit than its boundary
allows. She is also accountable for the budget consequence of that refusal, which is the subject of the next
section.

Both accountabilities are reflected in the responsibility matrix in 01.08, and any arrangement that would
compromise a boundary is an absolute escalation threshold in the charter: it goes to the Audit &amp; Risk
Committee before it goes anywhere else, and it can be refused there.

The honest reading of that safeguard is that it rests on one person rather than on a body. The escalation
threshold routes the question to Lorraine Kessler, and what makes the routing worth anything is not a
quorum but the fact that she is outside the management line and has no stake in the answer being
convenient. If she does not press, nobody else on the committee will, because there is nobody else on the
committee with a vote. That dependency is named in 01.07 §4.1 and carried as a known gap in 01.13 rather
than papered over here.

## 4. ADR-0004: the four-way separation, and the cheaper option that was refused

The alternative was available and was specific. At least one provider in the market offered readiness
advisory, an outsourced internal audit function and certification through an affiliated body as a single
managed engagement, with one relationship manager, one document repository and one schedule. It would have
been simpler to run, faster to mobilise, and cheaper. For a 4.6-FTE programme, "simpler to run" is not a
trivial benefit.

It was refused by the Audit &amp; Risk Committee on 2026-02-11 and recorded as ADR-0004. Three reasons, in
order of weight.

The first is that it is not permissible for the certification arm. A certification body may not consult on
the management system it certifies, and an affiliated structure does not cure the conflict; the accreditation
requirements contemplate exactly this arrangement and prohibit it. Whatever the commercial packaging, the
result would have been a certificate at risk, which is worse than no certificate because it would be
discovered by someone else.

The second is that the internal audit would have been worthless as evidence. Clause 9.2's requirement for
objectivity and impartiality is not satisfied by a firm auditing a management system it helped design, and
Stage 2 would be entitled to ask who designed it. An internal audit programme that cannot survive that
question does not discharge clause 9.2; it consumes budget and produces a document.

The third is the one that would not have appeared in a compliance review at all. A single provider spanning
readiness, internal audit and certification has no structural interest in telling CloudNimbus anything
uncomfortable. Every party in that arrangement is paid by the same relationship and reports, ultimately, to
the same account. The value of Camberwell's findings comes precisely from Camberwell having no stake in
CloudNimbus's control design looking good.

The refusal cost money, and the honest place to show it is the budget. B-02 (certification, $47,500), B-03
(readiness advisory, $96,000) and B-04 (outsourced ISMS internal audit, $34,000) are three separate lines in
the charter because they are three separate firms, each with its own mobilisation, its own onboarding and
its own duplication of context. A bundled arrangement would have carried a single, lower line. These are
professional fees, freely negotiated: nobody is penalising CloudNimbus for choosing the harder structure,
and no penalty, fine or fee schedule arises anywhere in this programme. The company simply paid more for an
arrangement whose defensibility does not depend on nobody looking closely at it. The Audit &amp; Risk
Committee took that decision knowing the number, which is the only way such a decision is worth taking.

EP-06 sits slightly apart from this architecture but on the same principle. Ironwood Security Labs reports
its penetration testing findings to Karim Haddad, not to the engineering teams whose work it tests. A test
report that is delivered first to the people who would have to remediate it is a test report that gets
negotiated before it is read.

## 5. Vendors, sub-processors and subservice organisations — three tests, overlapping sets

EP-07 and EP-08 are a different kind of party from the professional firms above: their controls form part
of the service rather than assuring it. Misclassifying which vendors belong in that group, in either
direction, is one of this programme's named risks — and, as it turns out, the classification is not a
matter of sorting each vendor into one of three boxes.

A **vendor** is any third party CloudNimbus buys from. A **sub-processor** is a vendor that processes
personal data on CloudNimbus's behalf under a data processing addendum, with contractual consequences —
disclosure, thirty days' notice of a new engagement, a right to object, and deletion on termination. A
**subservice organisation** is something narrower and specific to the SOC 2 examination: a vendor that
performs controls which are *necessary, in combination with CloudNimbus's own controls, to meet the
applicable trust services criteria*. The test is not spend, not criticality in casual usage, and not whether
the vendor touches production. It is whether the criteria can be met without that party's controls.

The three classes are not three boxes. They are overlapping sets over the same population, and the single
most common way to get this wrong is to lay them out as a partition and then discover that the numbers only
add up because something was quietly assigned to one class and removed from another.

Applying the test to CloudNimbus's register produces this arithmetic, and it must reconcile everywhere it
appears in the portfolio:

| Population | Count | Relationship to the others |
|---|---|---|
| Vendors in the register | 84 | The whole population |
| Sub-processors under a DPA | 11 | A subset of the 84 |
| — of which subservice organisations | 2 | AWS and Halcyon Identity; **a subset of the 11, not a separate class** |
| Ordinary vendors — neither sub-processor nor subservice organisation | 73 | The remainder |

**11 + 73 = 84.** The two subservice organisations are counted inside the eleven, not alongside them, and
that is not a presentational choice. AWS hosts personal data belonging to the 41 EU-residency customers in
`eu-central-1`; Halcyon Identity authenticates 1.24 million end users and holds their identity records.
Both process personal data on CloudNimbus's behalf under a data processing addendum, so both are
sub-processors. Both also perform controls necessary, in combination with CloudNimbus's own controls, to
meet the applicable trust services criteria, so both are subservice organisations. They are in both sets
because both definitions are satisfied, and a register that forced them into one bucket would be wrong
about whichever bucket it chose.

That overlap is the normal case rather than an awkward edge. The infrastructure and identity providers a
SaaS business genuinely depends on are exactly the providers most likely to touch personal data, so the
population that satisfies the SOC 2 test and the population that satisfies the DPA test will tend to
intersect at their most important members. **Treating the three classes as disjoint is itself the
classification error**, and it is the error ML-3 recorded: a register with one classification column,
forced to give each vendor a single label, cannot represent a vendor that is two things at once and will
misreport it as whichever thing the person filling in the column thought more important that day. The
remediation is a register with independent flags rather than a single category field.

Taking the SOC 2 limb of each in turn. AWS is a subservice organisation because CloudNimbus runs entirely
on AWS — seven accounts, four EKS
clusters, six Aurora PostgreSQL clusters, 214 S3 buckets across three regions — and the physical,
environmental and infrastructure controls that the Security and Availability criteria depend on are AWS's
controls, not CloudNimbus's. Halcyon Identity is a subservice organisation because it authenticates end
users; the logical access criteria cannot be met on the strength of CloudNimbus's controls alone when
another party performs the authentication. Both are carved out. DEC-108 settled the **method** on 2026-02-25, at a point when AWS was the only
subservice organisation then identified; Halcyon Identity was identified as a second when the classification
test was applied to the vendor register as a documented test, and the method already decided applied to
it without being retaken. Carve-out means their controls are
excluded from the description and the complementary subservice organisation controls CloudNimbus assumes
they perform are stated instead. Whether AWS in fact performs them is a matter for AWS's own reports — which
cover AWS's controls, and emphatically not CloudNimbus's configuration of them. A misconfigured S3 bucket is
CloudNimbus's control failure in an environment AWS operates correctly.

The other 82 are not subservice organisations, and most vendors never are — including **nine of the eleven
sub-processors**, which process personal data on CloudNimbus's behalf under a DPA and yet perform no
control the trust services criteria depend on. That group is the clearest demonstration that the two tests
are independent: being a sub-processor does not make a vendor a subservice organisation, and the nine exist
in the register carrying one flag and not the other. The programme's exposure runs in
both directions. Classifying a vendor as a subservice organisation when it is not inflates the description,
introduces complementary controls the reader must assume, and invites questions the company cannot answer.
Failing to classify one that is leaves a gap in the description that the criteria cannot be met across, and
that is a description-criteria problem before it is a control problem.

This is treated as a first-order problem rather than register hygiene because CloudNimbus has already failed
at it. **ML-3** in the 2025 Type I management letter recorded that the vendor register did not distinguish
subservice organisations from ordinary vendors. Pinehurst did not report a security weakness; it reported
that the company could not answer a definitional question about its own supply chain. The remediation is not
a better questionnaire. It is a register carrying independent classification flags rather than a single
category field, a documented test applied to each entry against each flag, and an owner competent to apply
it — which is why accountability for A-14 sits with Tobias Lund and
not with the security function. The quarterly sub-processor and vendor assurance review (CAL-08) reapplies
the test to new and changed vendors, and Annex A control **A.5.22**, monitoring, review and change
management of supplier services, is the ISO-side obligation the same review discharges.

## 6. Ongoing management of the boundaries

Independence is not established once. Each of the four professional engagements is reviewed by Marisol Vega
before renewal against the boundary stated in section 2, and any proposed extension of scope — Solstice
being asked to help with an audit response, Camberwell being asked to advise on a remediation, Ashcombe
&amp; Doyle being asked a question that shades into design — is escalated to the Audit &amp; Risk Committee
rather than settled by the person who wants the help. The register of external parties is maintained by
Rahul Bhargava and reported to the Trust Committee monthly.

One boundary requires no ongoing management and should be recorded for completeness. **EP-02, Pinehurst
Assurance Group, has no continuing role.** CloudNimbus appointed a new service auditor rather than
re-engaging the predecessor (DEC-104). Unlike a financial-statement audit, where AU-C section 210 imposes a
predecessor–successor communication before acceptance, an attestation engagement of this kind carries no
such required communication. Nothing obliged Pinehurst to speak to Ashcombe &amp; Doyle and nothing obliged
Ashcombe &amp; Doyle to ask. The transition was therefore managed by CloudNimbus itself: the Type I report,
the management letter and the remediation plans for ML-1, ML-2 and ML-3 — with their owners and their
2026-06-30 targets, the matters not then being closed — were provided directly to the incoming firm by
Karim Haddad. That the disclosure was voluntary is exactly why it is documented here — a
voluntary act leaves no trail unless someone chooses to leave one.
