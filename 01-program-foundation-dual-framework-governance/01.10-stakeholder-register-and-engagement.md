## 1. Purpose

This document identifies the parties with an interest in the Trust &amp; Assurance Programme, records how
much influence each has over it, and sets out how each is engaged. It serves two masters. Internally it is a
programme-management artefact: twenty-two entries, a cadence against each, and an owner for every channel.
Externally it is the beginning of CloudNimbus's response to ISO/IEC 27001:2022 clause 4.2, which requires
the organisation to determine the interested parties relevant to the ISMS and their relevant requirements.
The full clause 4.2 analysis, including which of those requirements become ISMS requirements, belongs to
Phase 02; what is fixed here is who the parties are.

Clause 4.2 acquired a note under **ISO/IEC 27001:2022/Amd 1:2024** stating that interested parties can have
requirements relevant to climate change. CloudNimbus has recorded that consideration in its clause 4.1 and
4.2 analysis; none of the parties below has raised a climate-related information security requirement, and
the register would have to change if one did.

## 2. How the register was built

Three questions were asked of every candidate party. What does this party want from the programme? What can
this party do to the programme? And through what channel does it act? The third question is the one that
usually goes unasked, and it is the one that makes the register useful, because a party with a strong
interest and no channel is a governance problem rather than a communications task.

Interest and influence are rated High, Medium, Low or None. Influence means the capacity to change what the
programme does — by deciding, by funding, by refusing, or by withholding something the programme needs. It
does not mean importance. A party can matter enormously and have no influence whatever, and entry SH-22
exists to stop that being forgotten.

## 4. Engagement approach by quadrant

The register sorts into four groups on interest and influence, and each group is engaged differently.

**High interest, high influence — manage closely.** This is the largest group and contains the executive
team, the platform leadership, the Audit &amp; Risk Committee chair, the enterprise-tier customer population
and both external assurance firms. What "manage closely" means here is not more meetings; it means that
these parties see the programme's actual position rather than a summary of it. The Trust Committee sees the
objective forecasts including the ones that are off track. The ARC sees the internal audit plan as a
decision to take, not a paper to note. Enterprise customers see the report itself, under NDA, rather than a
description of it.

Two entries in this quadrant carry a caveat. SH-19 and SH-20 are external assurance parties, and "manage
closely" cannot mean what it means for an internal stakeholder. The service auditor is engaged monthly from
2026-04 on evidence sufficiency and scheduling, and never on control design; the certification body is
engaged at Stage 1 and Stage 2 and at no point between, because contact outside an audit would be
consultancy whatever it was called. The reasoning is in 01.09. Listing a party as a stakeholder to be
engaged does not license engaging it in ways that would damage its usefulness.

**High interest, medium influence — keep satisfied and consulted.** The Compliance Manager, the product and
data owners, Customer Success, the general customer population and the EU-residency cohort sit here. These
parties can materially affect delivery through what they do or do not supply, but they do not decide. They
are consulted before decisions in their domain (the RACI in 01.08 shows where) and informed promptly after
ones that are not.

**Medium interest — keep informed.** Hannah Brill's function, the board observer, and the engineering
population at large. The engineering population's rating deserves comment, because SH-15 carries "Medium /
Medium" and absorbs more of this programme's cost than any other group. Seventy-eight engineers will
experience the programme as branch protection they cannot bypass, access requests that need recorded
approval, change records that need to exist before deployment rather than after, and evidence that has to be
produced in a form nobody enjoys producing. Their influence is rated Medium because collectively they can
determine whether controls operate as designed or are routed around — which is precisely programme risk
PR-02. Engagement runs weekly through team leads rather than through all-hands announcements, because
control friction is negotiated at the team level or not at all.

**High interest, low influence — keep informed, and be honest about the asymmetry.** SH-21, the 34-person
sales organisation, is the clearest case. Sales is the primary beneficiary of OBJ-05 — the reduction of
questionnaire turnaround from 11.4 days to three or fewer — and has essentially no say in how the programme
is run or sequenced. It is told what will be available and when, monthly, and its input is solicited on what
customers are actually asking for, which is the one thing it knows better than anyone else in the company.

**High interest, no influence.** One entry, and it is the subject of the next section.

## 5. SH-22 — the stakeholders with no channel

Approximately **1.24 million people** use the CloudNimbus Workforce Platform. They are not customers.
CloudNimbus's customers are 640 employers; SH-22 are those employers' employees, and they use the platform
because their employer told them to. They clock in through it, and when they do, the mobile application
captures their **geolocation**. Their hours, pay rates, differentials, accrual balances, expense receipts
and the calculated output that becomes their pay all pass through this system.

They have the highest interest of any party in this register, by a margin that is not close. If the
calculation engine derives an overtime rate wrongly, an individual is underpaid. If multi-tenant isolation
fails, one employer sees another's compensation data and individuals are exposed in it. If the retention
deletion job does not run, personal information persists past the point at which it should have gone. Every
consequence this programme is designed to prevent lands, at the end of the chain, on these people.

They have **no influence at all**. Not low influence — none. They did not choose the platform, cannot leave
it while remaining employed, have no contract with CloudNimbus, no commercial leverage, no representation in
any governance body in this programme, and no channel through which to raise anything. Where an individual
exercises a data subject right, it is routed through their employer under O6 and CloudNimbus's obligation is
to assist the customer within ten business days. The individual's counterparty is the employer. CloudNimbus
sits behind it.

The asymmetry compounds when you look at what the programme actually produces. The SOC 2 Type II examination
report is **restricted-use**: it is intended for CloudNimbus's management, for user entities during the
period covered, and for other specified parties with sufficient knowledge and understanding of the matters
in it. It is not a general-use document and it will never be published. The 1.24 million people with the
highest interest in whether these controls work will never read the report about whether these controls
work.

The ISO/IEC 27001:2022 certificate, once issued by the accredited certification body, is the only
**assurance deliverable** of this programme that can be shown publicly. That is a narrower statement than
"the only artefact", and the distinction is the one section 6 turns on. The trust centre carries a good
deal of public material — the scope and dates of the assurance position, the sub-processor list, a security
overview — but those are **CloudNimbus's own statements about itself**, which is a different kind of thing
from a certificate issued by an accredited third party or an opinion expressed by a CPA firm. A reader who
cannot tell the two apart will over-read the trust centre, which is why the trust centre attributes each
statement to its source and does not present its own security overview as though something had been
examined.

So the position for SH-22 is this. Of the two things an independent party will say about CloudNimbus, one
is restricted-use and one is publishable, and the publishable one tells a reader that a management system
was found to conform to a standard — not what any specific control does about their paycheque. Everything
else they could find is CloudNimbus talking about CloudNimbus.

This is stated without sentimentality because sentimentality would be the wrong response and would also be
useless. The register does not propose to invent a direct channel to 1.24 million people; CloudNimbus is not
their service provider in any relationship they chose, and manufacturing a consultation exercise would be
theatre. What the entry does is refuse the convenient conclusion that follows from an influence rating of
none — that a party with no leverage can be handled through the party that has it.

The refusal has a concrete consequence, and it is the reason the Privacy category was kept in scope. The
orthodox position for a processor is that notice and choice flow through the customer: the employer decides
what to tell its staff, obtains whatever basis it needs, and the platform is an instrument of the employer's
processing. That position is coherent for most of what CloudNimbus does. It is not coherent for the mobile
application, because **CloudNimbus collects personal information directly from the data subject** when it
captures geolocation at clock-in. There is no employer standing between the individual and the collection at
the moment it happens; there is an application, on a person's phone, taking a location reading. A party you
collect from directly is a party you owe something to directly, and the notice criterion at P1.1 becomes
CloudNimbus's own problem rather than solely the employer's. That argument is set out in full in 01.05 and
recorded as ADR-0002. SH-22 is where the argument comes from.

Two clarifications, because this is the kind of passage that invites over-reading. First, none of this is a
determination of compliance with any law. The Privacy category is a set of trust services criteria, not a
statute, and an examination against it establishes nothing about the GDPR, the CCPA or CPRA, or any state
privacy statute. Legal obligations to these individuals, and the allocation of those obligations between
CloudNimbus and its customers, are Tobias Lund's remit and are governed by the data processing addenda.
Second, an influence rating of None is a description of the current structure, not an endorsement of it. It
is recorded so that whenever this programme makes a trade-off that affects end users — a retention period,
a default in the mobile app, a decision about what to log — someone can point at the register and observe
that the affected party is not in the room.

## 6. What customers are told, and what they are not

Customers learn about this programme through four channels, and the boundaries of each are deliberate.

The **trust centre** carries the current assurance position: which examinations and certifications exist,
their scope and dates, the sub-processor list, and the security overview that answers most questionnaire
content without a bilateral exchange. It is the artefact OBJ-05 depends on. Everything on it other than the
certificate is CloudNimbus's own statement about itself, and the page says so — the certificate is
reproduced because an accredited body issued it and it is a general-use document; the security overview is
labelled as CloudNimbus's description of its own practices, unexamined; and the SOC 2 Type II examination
report is named and described but not hosted, because it is restricted-use. The **report under NDA** is how
the SOC 2 Type II examination report itself reaches a customer: to the 118 enterprise-tier customers within
thirty days of issuance under O2, and to any of the 640 on request under O1, in both cases under
confidentiality terms because the report is restricted-use. **Sub-processor notice** runs under O5 — thirty
days before a new sub-processor is engaged, with a right to object — and is owned by Tobias Lund. **Incident
notification** under O4 runs within forty-eight hours of determination for an incident affecting a
customer's data, and is not a programme communication at all; it is an operational obligation that the
programme's controls exist to support.

What customers are not told matters as much. They are not told the contents of the risk register, which
contains CloudNimbus's own assessment of its weaknesses and is not a customer artefact. They are not told
the findings of penetration tests in raw form; an attestation of testing performed and remediation status is
provided, and the finding detail is not, because a list of the ways a multi-tenant platform was found to be
attackable is not something to distribute even under NDA. They are not given internal audit reports or
nonconformity records; those are ISMS documented information under clause 7.5 and their audience is
Northgate and the ARC. And they are not told anything about the examination's likely outcome before the
report exists — no interim assurances, no "we expect a clean opinion", because the opinion is the auditor's
and CloudNimbus does not have it to give. Section V of the report will carry other information provided by
management, and it is worth remembering when reading it that Section V is **not covered by the service
auditor's opinion**.

Nothing in any of these channels may describe CloudNimbus as "SOC 2 certified" or "SOC 2 compliant". There
is no such status. Ana-Sofia Cruz owns the questionnaire and trust-centre language and Karim Haddad reviews
it, and this is the single most common correction the programme has had to make to customer-facing copy.
