## 1. Purpose

Two things drive the rhythm of this programme. The first is what CloudNimbus has promised other people it
will do — twelve obligations arising from customer contracts, data processing addenda, an investor rights
agreement and the two assurance engagements. The second is what the programme has decided it will do on a
recurring basis in order to keep those promises and to produce the evidence that both deliverables need.
This document records both, maps them onto a calendar, and identifies the single obligation that constrains
the programme's end-game more than any other.

It is approved by the General Counsel rather than by the programme owner because the obligations in section
4 are contractual instruments before they are compliance activities. Their interpretation is a legal
question, and the register's accuracy is only as good as the reading of the contracts behind it.

## 2. What counts as an obligation here

An obligation, for the purposes of this register, is a commitment to an identified external party that
CloudNimbus is required to perform, on a stated frequency or trigger, and whose non-performance would be a
failure to that party. Three exclusions follow from that definition and are worth stating because each
excludes something a reader might expect to find.

Internal policy commitments are not obligations. The policy suite requires a great deal of CloudNimbus and
none of it is owed to an outside party; it is control, not obligation, and it lives in the control library.

Statutory duties are not in this register either. CloudNimbus has legal obligations — Tobias Lund owns them
— and they are managed under a separate legal register. The reason for the separation is not
administrative. Neither the SOC 2 Type II examination report nor the ISO/IEC 27001:2022 certificate is a
determination of compliance with any law, and a register that mixed contractual assurance obligations with
statutory duties would invite exactly the inference this programme refuses to support.

Aspirations are not obligations. The programme's objectives in 01.07 are targets it set itself. O1 to O12
are things other people can hold CloudNimbus to.

## 3. Deriving and owning the register

The register was built by reading the instruments rather than by asking the business what it thought it had
promised. The standard master services agreement was reviewed against the enterprise-tier amendment; the
data processing addendum and the EU-residency addendum were reviewed separately, because the populations
differ — 640 customers hold the MSA, 118 hold the enterprise-tier terms, and 41 hold the residency addendum;
the Fairmount Ventures investor rights agreement was reviewed for reporting duties; and the two engagement
instruments, the Northgate certification agreement and the Ashcombe &amp; Doyle engagement letter, were read
for what CloudNimbus owes the assurance providers rather than the other way round.

Ownership is assigned to an individual, never to a function. Where the obligation is contractual and runs to
a customer or a regulator's counterparty, Tobias Lund owns it. Where it is an engagement obligation, Marisol
Vega owns it as the sponsor of both engagements. Where discharge depends on an operational process, the
process owner is named alongside. The register is reviewed at the quarterly sub-processor and vendor
assurance review (CAL-08) and whenever a contract template changes.

One observation about O1 belongs here rather than in a footnote. O1 describes a steady state — a *current*
SOC 2 Type II report covering at least Security, Availability and Confidentiality, available under NDA on
request. CloudNimbus does not yet occupy that state. What exists today is a Type I report from Pinehurst
Assurance Group covering Security only, as of 2025-05-31, and a Type I is a statement about the suitability
of design of controls at a point in time, with no tests of operating effectiveness behind it. The first
report that answers O1 in the form the clause describes is the one this programme is built to produce. That
gap is the commercial reason the board funded the programme, and it is more honest to record it against the
obligation than to let the register imply a position the company has not reached.

| O12 | Engagement letter — Ashcombe &amp; Doyle | Provide management's written assertion and a representation letter at the conclusion of the examination | At each examination |

Four of these repay a second look.

**O1 sets a floor of three categories and the programme is examining five.** That is deliberate
over-delivery and it is not free: Privacy alone adds eighteen criteria to the examination. The argument for
doing it anyway is made in 01.05 on commercial and honesty grounds, and it is an argument rather than an
inevitability. Nothing in any customer contract required it.

**O3, the bridge letter, is routinely misunderstood by the parties who ask for it.** A bridge letter — or
gap letter — is a statement issued by CloudNimbus management covering the period between the end of the
report period and the customer's own reporting date. It is **unaudited** and carries **no auditor opinion**.
Ashcombe &amp; Doyle does not sign it and does not review it. It says, in substance, that management is not
aware of material changes to the control environment since the period end. A customer that treats a bridge
letter as an extension of the examination has misread it, and the covering language CloudNimbus uses says so
plainly.

**O8 is the only continuous obligation in the register.** Everything else is periodic or triggered; keeping
EU personal data at rest within `eu-central-1` is a state that must hold at every instant, which makes it a
control question rather than a calendar question and puts it into the technical control set rather than into
CAL.

**O10 is the obligation most likely to be missed quietly.** Notifying Northgate of significant changes to
the ISMS, its scope or the organisation within thirty days depends on someone recognising a change as
significant at the time it happens. A reorganisation, an acquisition, a new region or a material change to
the scope statement all qualify; none of them arrives labelled as a certification matter. Karim Haddad owns
the judgement and the Trust Committee's standing agenda carries a change-notification item so that the
question is asked monthly rather than remembered annually.

Two structural points about this calendar. First, every item on it produces an artefact that is retained in
the single shared evidence store, and the frequency stated is the frequency the examination will sample
against. A quarterly control that operates three times in a year is a deviation whatever the reason, and the
calendar is therefore a commitment rather than an intention. Second, the calendar is deliberately not
divided into "SOC 2 activities" and "ISO activities". CAL-07 produces access review evidence that serves
CC6.2 and CC6.3 and Annex A controls in the A.5 and A.8 families at once; CAL-12 serves CC1.4 and A.6.3.
Splitting the calendar by framework would have re-created the duplication that ADR-0001 exists to prevent.

The two entries that are framework-specific are CAL-14 and CAL-15, because clause 9.2 internal audit and
clause 9.3 management review are ISMS requirements with no direct SOC 2 analogue. They nonetheless produce
evidence the examination is interested in, since a functioning internal audit and management review are
monitoring activities of the kind CC4.1 and CC4.2 contemplate.

**CAL-03 and CAL-04 run from the first quarter, not from the last.** The Audit &amp; Risk Committee report

**CAL-06's first occurrence in 2026-03 is a review of the risk assessment method, not of a register**,
because no register exists to review: the baseline is not published until MS-04 on 2026-04-10. Labelling it

afterwards. From 2026-06 onwards CAL-06 is what its name says: a quarterly review of the register itself.

The first ISO surveillance audit falls due in 2027-12 under O11, within twelve months of the certification
decision. It sits outside this view and inside the programme's steady-state calendar.

## 7. CAL-14 — coverage is undefined, and that is a known gap

CAL-14 reads "Annual programme, risk-weighted." That wording was chosen deliberately and it is incomplete,
and the incompleteness is recorded here rather than discovered later.

What the wording fixes is frequency and prioritisation basis: an internal audit programme will run annually,
and what it looks at will be weighted by risk rather than allocated evenly. What it does not fix is
**coverage**. It does not say whether the programme audits the requirements of clauses 4 to 10 themselves —
context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, improvement — or only the Annex A
controls that are easier to sample. It does not say whether coverage extends to all locations and all
operations within the ISMS scope, which for CloudNimbus is the whole organisation across three AWS regions
including EU-region operations serving 41 customers. It does not say whether the whole ISMS is covered
within one annual cycle or across a multi-year cycle with a stated rotation. And it does not say who defines
the audit criteria and scope for each individual audit.

That last point is not a matter of good practice. Clause 9.2.2 requires the organisation to plan, establish,
implement and maintain an audit programme including the frequency, methods, responsibilities, planning
requirements and reporting, and requires the audit criteria and scope to be defined **for each audit**. A
calendar entry giving frequency alone does not discharge that requirement; it discharges one element of it
and leaves the rest to whoever writes the audit plan.

Defining coverage was **deferred to the internal audit charter**, which is a Phase 08 artefact. That was a
sequencing decision made for a defensible reason — the coverage of a risk-weighted programme cannot
sensibly be fixed before the risk assessment exists, and the baseline register is a Phase 03 deliverable —
and it is nonetheless a decision to leave something undefined at the point where the operating rhythm was
being fixed for the whole programme.

Two features of the surrounding structure make the gap worth naming rather than filing. The clause 9.2 audit
is outsourced to Camberwell Risk Partners (DEC-110), and where the client has not defined coverage, the
supplier will define it, by proposing an audit plan that the client approves without a specification to
approve it against. That exposure is recorded as programme risk PR-06. And Annex A control **A.5.22** —
monitoring, review and change management of supplier services — applies to the audit supplier as much as to
any other, which means that how Camberwell's work is scoped and reviewed is itself a control CloudNimbus
operates and can be examined on.

**Recorded position:** CAL-14's coverage is undefined as at the close of the foundation phase. Responsibility for
defining it sits with the internal audit charter under the accountability recorded at A-16, which is the
Audit &amp; Risk Committee. It is carried into 01.13 as a known gap and into Phase 02's entry criteria as an
open item. Leaving it undefined is the decision that has been taken; this document records the decision and
its owner, and does not predict what follows from it.

## 8. O2 and the report-issuance runway

The obligation that constrains this programme most tightly at the end is O2: the enterprise-tier
requirement to provide the report to the customer **within 30 days of issuance**, across **118 customers**.

Work backwards from the plan. The report is planned for issue on 2027-02-26. Thirty days from issuance
places the delivery deadline on or before **2027-03-28** for every one of the 118. That is not a long window
for a distribution exercise that is not, in fact, a distribution exercise. The SOC 2 Type II examination
report is restricted-use — intended for CloudNimbus's management, for user entities during the period
covered, and for other specified parties with sufficient knowledge and understanding of the matters it deals
with. It is not published, not posted to the trust centre, and not sent as an attachment to a mailing list.
Each of the 118 deliveries requires the right recipient identified, confidentiality terms in place, and a
controlled channel. At that volume, a manual process invented in March will not complete in March.

Three consequences follow, and all three are programme obligations rather than administrative details.

The distribution mechanism must be built before the report exists. Recipient contacts, NDA status and the
delivery channel are assembled during the window, not after issuance, and Ana-Sofia Cruz owns the readiness
of that mechanism as part of the customer communication plan in 01.10.

The runway cannot absorb slippage twice. The board and Audit &amp; Risk Committee report on 2027-03-11 falls
inside the same thirty days, which means the same small team is briefing the board and distributing to 118
enterprise customers in the same fortnight. If the report issues later than planned, the O2 clock moves with
it — the obligation is thirty days from issuance, not a fixed date — but the internal reporting date does
not, and the compression lands on the people executing both.

And the demand for bridge letters under O3 will arrive in the same period. Customers whose fiscal year ends
after 2026-12-31 will want the gap covered, and the letter is management's to issue, unaudited, with no
auditor opinion behind it. Preparing a standard form of bridge letter and the basis on which management can
sign it is work that belongs in the window, not in the queue behind the distribution exercise.
