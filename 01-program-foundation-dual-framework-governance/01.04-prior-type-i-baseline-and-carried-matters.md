## 1. Purpose

CloudNimbus is not starting from nothing. In 2025 it obtained a SOC 2 Type I examination report
from Pinehurst Assurance Group, covering the Security category only, with an unmodified opinion.
That report is the baseline this programme inherits, and this document sets out what it did prove,
what it could not prove, the three matters raised in the accompanying management letter, and what
CloudNimbus decided to do about the firm that issued it.

The argument this chapter makes is uncomfortable and deliberate: **a clean Type I is a weak
predictor of a Type II outcome, and the most valuable thing the 2025 engagement produced was not
the opinion but the management letter.**

## 2. The report as issued

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Issuing firm | Pinehurst Assurance Group |
| Engagement type | SOC 2 Type I examination |
| Categories examined | **Security only** — Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality and Privacy have never been examined |
| "As of" date | 2025-05-31 |
| Report date | 2025-06-30 |
| Opinion | **Unmodified** |
| Test exceptions | **None — and none were possible** |
| Accompanying management letter | Three matters: ML-1, ML-2, ML-3 |

The last two rows need reading together. A Type I opines on the **suitability of design** of
controls **as of a specified date**. It performs **no tests of operating effectiveness**. There is
therefore no population, no sample, and nothing to deviate from — **a Type I cannot produce test
exceptions.** The absence of exceptions in the 2025 report is not a result. It is a property of the
engagement type, and quoting it as evidence of control quality would be a misrepresentation.

The three management-letter matters, by contrast, were real observations. A management letter is
not part of the report, carries no opinion, and is not distributed to user entities. It is the
mechanism by which a service auditor tells management the things it noticed that were not
inconsistent with the opinion it gave. In this case, all three concerned the same underlying theme.

## 3. Why an unmodified Type I is weak evidence of Type II readiness

Four gaps separate what the 2025 report established from what the 2027 report will need to
establish, and it is worth being precise about each.

**The first is the design/operation gap.** Suitability of design asks whether a control, if it
operated as described, would provide reasonable assurance that the criterion is met. Operating
effectiveness asks whether it actually did, consistently, across every instance in a period. A
control can be immaculately designed and operated four times out of five. The Type I cannot see
that, because it does not look.

**The second is the point/period gap.** "As of 2025-05-31" is one day. The Type II window is
2026-07-01 to 2026-12-31, six months. Everything that happens to a control over six months — staff
turnover, a tooling migration, a quarter where the reviewer was on leave, a holiday freeze, an
emergency change at 2 a.m. — is invisible to a point-in-time examination and is precisely what a
period examination samples for.

**The third is the scope gap.** The 2025 report covered the Security category: 33 Common Criteria.
The 2026–27 examination covers 61 criteria across five categories. Availability, Processing
Integrity, Confidentiality and Privacy have never been examined at CloudNimbus in any form. For
those 28 criteria there is no baseline at all, favourable or otherwise, and treating the Type I as
general reassurance about control maturity would silently extend a Security-only conclusion across
four categories it never touched.

**The fourth is the evidence gap, and it is the one this programme takes most seriously.** In a
Type I, the auditor inspects the design of a control: the policy, the configuration, the
description of who does what. Evidence that a control *was performed* is not the subject matter. In
a Type II, evidence that the control was performed — for each selected item in a sample, with a
date, a performer and an outcome that can be re-examined — **is** the subject matter. An
organisation can pass a Type I with a control that operates perfectly and leaves no durable trace,
and then fail the same control in a Type II purely on evidence form. Pinehurst noticed exactly this
and said so, which is what the management letter is for.

## 4. The three carried matters

| ID | What Pinehurst observed | What it means for a Type II | Owner | Target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ML-1** | Access review evidence was retained as email threads and screenshots, in a form that would not support a Type II sample | The control may well operate; the artefact cannot be sampled. A reviewer cannot re-perform "who approved this, on what date, against what population" from a forwarded thread | Wes Delacroix (control) with Rahul Bhargava (evidence form) | 2026-06-30 |
| **ML-2** | Change-management peer review was enforced by team convention, not by branch protection — design was adequate, enforcement was not | A convention has no population. Every merge is an opportunity for the convention not to be followed, and nothing in the system prevents it or records that it was skipped | Junia Okonkwo | 2026-06-30 |
| **ML-3** | The vendor register did not distinguish subservice organisations from ordinary vendors | A classification error puts a party in the wrong place in the system description — either carving out a vendor that is not a subservice organisation, or failing to identify and disclose one that is | Tobias Lund | 2026-06-30 |

### ML-1 — access review evidence

The observation was about form, not about whether reviews happened. Quarterly access reviews over
production and privileged access were being performed and the outcomes were being acted on, but the
record consisted of email threads between the reviewer and the system owner, with screenshots
pasted in. For a Type II, a service auditor selects a sample of review cycles and, for each,
expects to establish the population that was reviewed, who performed the review, when it was
performed relative to its due date, what was decided about each entitlement, and that revocations
were actioned. An email thread supports none of that reliably: the population is whatever was
pasted in, the timing is the timestamp of a message rather than of the review, and completeness is
unverifiable.

The remediation is to move access reviews into a system that generates the population, records
reviewer decisions per entitlement, timestamps completion, and retains the record — with the review
cycle, not the correspondence about it, as the artefact. Wes Delacroix owns the control; Rahul
Bhargava owns the evidence form, because the two are different problems and this programme has
learned to name them separately.

### ML-2 — change-management peer review

Pinehurst's phrasing was careful and correct: the design was adequate, the enforcement was not.
Engineering practice required a peer review before merge, engineers overwhelmingly did it, and the
control as described would meet CC8.1 if it operated. But nothing in the toolchain prevented a
merge without review, and nothing recorded the exception when one occurred. In a 78-person
engineering organisation distributed across 31 states and 4 countries, a convention is a statement
about culture, not a control.

The remediation is to make the toolchain enforce what the convention asked for: branch protection
requiring an approving review from someone other than the author before merge to a protected
branch, with an explicit, logged and separately approved path for emergency changes, since a change
process with no emergency path produces either outages or silent bypasses. Junia Okonkwo owns it.

### ML-3 — vendor register classification

The register listed vendors. It did not identify which of them were **subservice organisations** —
that is, which performed controls that are necessary, in combination with CloudNimbus's own
controls, to meet the applicable trust services criteria. That is not a security failure; it is a
classification failure with a contractual and descriptive consequence. Getting it wrong in either
direction is damaging: carving out a vendor that is not a subservice organisation removes controls
from the description that ought to be there, and failing to identify one that is leaves the
description asserting coverage that nobody actually provides.

The remediation is to apply the definition test to the whole register and record the reasoning per
vendor against each classification separately, which produces the arithmetic 01.09 relies on:
**84 vendors in the register, of which 11 are sub-processors under a data processing addendum —
and both subservice organisations, AWS and Halcyon Identity, are among those 11 — leaving 73
ordinary vendors. 11 + 73 = 84.** The classes overlap rather than partition, which is precisely
what the old register could not express: a single category column forces a vendor that is both a
sub-processor and a subservice organisation to be recorded as one or the other. Tobias Lund owns
it, because a sub-processor obligation is contractual before it is technical.

## 5. The pattern, and the risk it creates

All three matters describe the same failure mode from different angles: **the control was
substantially sound and the trace it left was not.** ML-1 is evidence form. ML-2 is enforcement and
therefore the absence of a population. ML-3 is classification and therefore the absence of a
defensible record of a judgement. None of them is a story about CloudNimbus not doing the work.

That makes the remediation seductive and dangerous in equal measure, because each matter can be
closed on paper by fixing the control while leaving the trace it leaves substantially unchanged.

As at the close of the foundation phase, **none of the three matters is closed.** All three are
**targeted for closure by 2026-06-30** under OBJ-04, each with a named owner and a plan: ML-1 with
Wes Delacroix on the control and Rahul Bhargava on the evidence form, ML-2 with Junia Okonkwo, ML-3
with Tobias Lund. The target date is not arbitrary — it sits one day before the observation window
opens on 2026-07-01, because a control remediated inside the window has a period during which it
was not operating as described, and that period is not recoverable.

Two things need saying about what closure will and will not mean when it comes.

**Closing a control is not the same as closing the evidence trail it leaves.** Each of these three
matters can be marked closed on the strength of an artefact that proves the control now exists and
proves nothing at all about what it will leave behind. Buy an access review tool and the ML-1
ticket closes; whether the tool's export gives a service auditor a population, a reviewer, a
per-entitlement decision and a completion timestamp is a different question, and it is a question
that will not be asked by anyone closing a ticket. Turn on branch protection and the ML-2 ticket
closes; whether the emergency-change path records a retrospective approval within its stated window
is a different question again, and emergency changes are precisely the population a Type II sample
will reach for. Rewrite the vendor register and the ML-3 ticket closes; whether each entry carries a
reasoned classification against each definition, or merely a label somebody chose, is a third
different question.

**And the evidence trail cannot be verified until sampling inside the observation window.** This is
not caution; it is what a Type II is. Verification means selecting items from a population that
accumulated over time and re-performing the auditor's reading of them, and no population exists
until the controls have been running for a while. The real verification of ML-1 and ML-2 is
therefore not available to Phase 01, and it is not available to Phase 04 when the control library
is published either. It belongs to Phase 08. OBJ-04 says "closed **and verified**" for exactly this
reason, and the programme's own delivery risk log carries **PR-04** — *the Type I management-letter
matters are closed as controls but not as evidence trails* — because naming the failure mode in
advance is the only control available against it. Recording the limit of what is currently known is
more useful than declaring victory three months early.

## 6. Changing firms — DEC-104

On 2026-01-22, three days after kickoff, Marisol Vega decided to appoint a new service auditor
rather than re-engage Pinehurst. **Ashcombe and Doyle LLP** was appointed, with Meredith Vance, CPA
as engagement partner.

The honest reasons, in the order they carried weight. **Scope competence** came first: the 2026–27
engagement covers five categories including Processing Integrity over a calculation engine and
Privacy over directly-collected geolocation, and the firm needed demonstrable depth in multi-tenant
SaaS, in examining derived-output processing, and in the privacy criteria. Pinehurst's engagement
had been scoped, staffed and priced for a Security-only design examination, which is a genuinely
different piece of work. **Anchoring** came second: the same team that formed a view of these
controls' design in 2025 would carry that view into 2026, and CloudNimbus preferred a firm forming
a first opinion on the design of a materially larger control set. **Capacity** came third: the
engagement requires a monthly service-auditor checkpoint from 2026-04 and fieldwork across
2027-01-12 to 2027-02-06, and the availability of that cadence was a selection criterion.

What was **not** a reason: dissatisfaction with Pinehurst's work. The management letter was the most
useful output of the 2025 engagement, and a firm that tells you your evidence will not survive a
Type II sample is a firm doing its job well. Nor was the decision made to obtain a more comfortable
auditor; that motive was named explicitly at the Audit and Risk Committee on 2026-01-19 and
rejected as a basis for selection, and the minute records it.

The decision has a cost and it is worth stating. A successor firm has no accumulated understanding
of the system, so the description, the control set and the environment all have to be re-explained
from scratch, and the early checkpoints are more expensive in internal time than they would have
been with an incumbent.

## 7. The absence of a required predecessor communication

In a financial statement audit, AU-C section 210 requires the successor auditor to request
communication with the predecessor before accepting the engagement. **Attestation engagements carry
no equivalent requirement.** There is no obligatory predecessor/successor communication in a SOC 2
examination, and neither Pinehurst nor Ashcombe and Doyle was under any professional obligation to
speak to the other about CloudNimbus.

The gap is therefore CloudNimbus's to fill, and CloudNimbus filled it directly rather than assuming
the firms would. Management provided Ashcombe and Doyle with the complete 2025 Type I report
including the system description as issued and the control matrix; the full management letter with
all three matters; the remediation plans for ML-1, ML-2 and ML-3 with their named owners and their
2026-06-30 targets; the register of changes to the system since 2025-05-31; and a written summary
of what the 2025 engagement did **not** cover, so that no part of the Security-only conclusion could
be read across to the four categories being examined for the first time. The transition was managed
by CloudNimbus, not by the firms, and it was minuted as such.

## 8. What the baseline is worth

The 2025 Type I established that, on one day, CloudNimbus's Security controls were designed in a
way that could meet the Common Criteria. It established nothing about whether they operate, nothing
about a period, nothing about four of the five categories now in scope, and — by the auditor's own
account in the management letter — something distinctly unflattering about whether the traces those
controls leave behind could be tested.

That is not a criticism of the report. It is what a Type I is. The value of the 2025 engagement to
this programme lies almost entirely in the three sentences of its management letter, and the
sentence this portfolio hangs on is the one those three matters make possible: **a clean Type I can
tell you precisely where you are going to fail.**
