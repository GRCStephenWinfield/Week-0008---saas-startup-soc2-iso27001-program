## 1. Purpose

This document assigns the information security and assurance roles for the Trust &amp; Assurance Programme,
names the people who hold them, and sets out a responsibility matrix for twenty programme activities. It
exists to satisfy a requirement of ISO/IEC 27001:2022 clause 5.3 and to give the SOC 2 examination something
concrete to test against the CC1 series, but its practical purpose is narrower and more useful than either:
it is the document that answers "who decides this?" before the question becomes urgent.

## 2. Clause 5.3 and the absence of a CISO

Clause 5.3 requires top management to ensure that the responsibilities and authorities for roles relevant to
information security are assigned and communicated within the organisation, and specifically that
responsibility and authority are assigned for ensuring the ISMS conforms to the standard and for reporting
on its performance to top management. It does not prescribe titles. It prescribes that the assignment be
made, and that it be communicated — which means it must exist somewhere a person can be shown it.

CloudNimbus has **no CISO title**. It has a VP Security &amp; Trust with eight people reporting into a
security function, a CTO who owns engineering, and a CFO who sponsors the programme. In many organisations
the clause 5.3 assignment can be inferred from the org chart because the title carries the authority with
it. Here it cannot, and inference is not assignment. Writing "the CISO is accountable for the ISMS" in a
policy would have been a fiction; leaving it unstated would have been a nonconformity waiting to be raised
at Stage 1, where documented information is precisely what is reviewed.

The assignment is therefore made explicitly and in writing:

- **Karim Haddad, VP Security &amp; Trust**, holds the authority and responsibility for ensuring the ISMS
  conforms to the requirements of ISO/IEC 27001:2022, and for reporting on the performance of the ISMS to
  top management. This is the clause 5.3 a) and 5.3 b) assignment in full.
- **Elise Fontaine, Chief Executive Officer**, as top management, retains the leadership obligations of
  clause 5.1 that cannot be delegated, issues the information security policy under clause 5.2, and chairs
  the management review under clause 9.3.
- **Marisol Vega, Chief Financial Officer**, is the executive sponsor. She owns the budget and both external
  engagements and is the point at which resource decisions under clause 7.1 are made.

That the assignment had to be written rather than assumed is not a weakness in the programme. It is one of
the few places where a small organisation's lack of a conventional structure forces a clearer answer than a
large one would have produced. The consequence, though, is real: Karim Haddad's accountability now depends
on a document rather than on a job title, and that document has to be kept current, communicated and
evidenced. It is reissued with the policy suite annually under CAL-13.

### 4.1 A convention, stated once, that resolves three apparent contradictions

A reader comparing this matrix against the decision log in `logs/decision-log.md` will find what looks like
disagreement in at least three places. A-03 makes the VP Security &amp; Trust accountable for trust services
category selection, while DEC-102 and ADR-0002 record Marisol Vega as the decider. A-02 makes the CFO
accountable for budget approval and monitoring, while MS-01 records the board approving the budget. A-16
makes the Audit &amp; Risk Committee accountable for the internal audit programme, while CAL-14 gives the owner
as Karim Haddad.

None of these is an error, and the programme adopts a convention here rather than reconciling them one by
one. **A responsibility matrix records accountability for the conduct of an activity. A decision log records
who took a particular decision.** They are different questions and they routinely have different answers.
Karim Haddad is accountable for running a defensible category-selection analysis and for the quality of the
recommendation that comes out of it; Marisol Vega, as executive sponsor and budget holder, took the decision
that recommendation supported, and her name is on it. Marisol Vega is accountable for managing the programme
inside an envelope; the board approved the envelope itself, and anything that would exceed it returns to the
board. Karim Haddad runs the internal audit programme day to day; the Audit &amp; Risk Committee answers for
whether it was adequate.

The convention is worth stating explicitly because the alternative — forcing every table to name the same
person — would flatten a real distinction and would produce a matrix in which the CFO is accountable for
security judgements and the VP Security &amp; Trust is accountable for spending decisions. Neither is true.
Where the RACI and a decision record differ, **the RACI governs the work and the record governs the
decision**, and later phases inherit that reading.

## 5. Three allocations, defended

A responsibility matrix is only interesting where it is contestable. Three of these rows would be allocated
differently in most organisations, and the reasoning is recorded here so that a later reader can see it was
a decision rather than an oversight.

### 5.1 A-01 and A-16 are accountable to the Audit &amp; Risk Committee, not to management

The obvious objection is that a committee cannot be accountable — accountability belongs to a person, and
Lorraine Kessler is a non-executive who does not run anything. The objection is understood, and half of it
is conceded: with one voting member the committee and the person are very nearly the same thing, which
01.07 §4.1 sets out rather than disguises. The other half is rejected, for the reasons below.

Take A-16 first, because it is the more important of the two. Internal audit that reports to the person it
audits is not internal audit. Clause 9.2 requires an internal audit programme that determines whether the
ISMS conforms to the organisation's own requirements and to the requirements of the standard, and clause
9.2.2 requires that auditors be selected and audits conducted in a way that ensures objectivity and
impartiality of the audit process. If Karim Haddad were accountable for the internal audit programme, the
person responsible for ISMS conformity would also be the person deciding what gets audited, how deeply, and
what the findings say about his own work. That is not impartial in any sense the standard would recognise,
and it is the single most common structural defect in small-company ISMSs.

CloudNimbus has 187 staff and no internal audit function. It cannot hire one; a full-time internal auditor
for a company this size would be an expensive way to produce a small amount of work, and the decision to
outsource rather than hire was taken by Marisol Vega on 2026-03-11 (DEC-110). But outsourcing the *work*
does not create the *independence*. Camberwell Risk Partners is independent of CloudNimbus, but it is
engaged by CloudNimbus, paid by CloudNimbus and scoped by whoever at CloudNimbus tells it what to look at.
The independence has to come from where the engagement reports, and that is the Audit &amp; Risk Committee.
The ARC's accountability for A-16 is CloudNimbus's answer to having no internal audit function: Karim
Haddad and Rahul Bhargava are both R — they run the programme, manage the supplier and produce the schedule
— but the programme's scope, its findings and its adequacy are answered for outside the line that operates
the ISMS.

It is worth being exact about the size of that claim, because the obvious overstatement is available and
01.07 §4.1 refuses it. The claim is **not** that management cannot overrule the ARC. The committee has one
voting member, Lorraine Kessler, and a determined executive team could plainly override her; asserting
structural independence that the composition does not support would be worse than claiming nothing. The
claim is the narrower one, and it is the one that matters for clause 9.2.2: **internal audit does not
report to the person whose management system it audits.** Camberwell Risk Partners' findings reach an
independent non-executive director directly, under engagement terms that let her meet the auditor without
management present, and they reach her whether or not Karim Haddad would prefer them framed differently.
That reporting line exists even though the committee behind it is thin, and a thin committee with a real
reporting line is a materially better position than a well-populated committee that only ever sees what
management chooses to bring it.

A-01 follows the same logic in a milder form. The charter defines the programme's scope, budget and
objectives; letting management approve its own charter without ratification would make every subsequent
scope decision self-authorised. The CEO is R — she approves and issues the charter, and it is her signature
on it — and the ARC is A, ratifying it and answering for its adequacy. Two distinct acts, deliberately kept
distinct.

A note on the practical mechanics, since "the committee is accountable" is easy to write and easy to hollow
out. The ARC receives a quarterly report under CAL-03 — CAL-04 is the board security update and is a
different item to a different audience. Rahul Bhargava is its secretary, non-voting, and produces the pack.
Lorraine Kessler chairs it and, as its only voting member, is the individual through whom the committee's
accountability is exercised in substance as well as in form.
Where the ARC needs to act between meetings, the chair acts and the committee ratifies. If this mechanism is
allowed to lapse — if the ARC receives the internal audit plan as a paper to note rather than a decision to
take — the accountability recorded in this row becomes decorative. That is a live exposure and it is
recorded as one in the programme's delivery risk log at PR-06.

### 5.2 A-19 is accountable to the CEO

Management's written assertion appears in Section II of a SOC 2 report, before the description of the system
and before the auditor's tests. It is not a formality and it is not a compliance-team artefact, and the
temptation to route it through the person who assembled the evidence is exactly the temptation this
allocation refuses.

What the assertion actually says is this. Management asserts that the description of the system presented in
Section III is presented in accordance with the description criteria in DC section 200 — that is, that it is
complete and accurate in the matters DC1 through DC9 require it to cover, and that it does not omit or
distort information that would affect a user entity's understanding. Management further asserts that the
controls stated in that description were suitably designed, and operated effectively throughout the
specified period, to provide reasonable assurance that CloudNimbus's service commitments and system
requirements were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria. Where subservice organisations
are carved out, the assertion is framed on the assumption that the complementary subservice organisation
controls operated effectively; where complementary user entity controls exist, the same assumption applies
to them.

That is a set of positive statements about the company's own system, made by the company, in a document a
customer will read. It is not an auditor's statement and the auditor does not make it on management's
behalf. Signing it means asserting that the description of a multi-tenant platform serving 640 customers and
1.24 million end users across three AWS regions is accurate — including the parts describing incidents,
changes and exceptions during the period. The person who signs it should be the person with the standing to
be wrong about it in public, and at CloudNimbus that is Elise Fontaine.

The CFO is R because Marisol Vega owns the engagement and coordinates the representation letter that
accompanies it; the VP Security &amp; Trust is R because Karim Haddad assembles the factual basis; the
Compliance function is C, not R, because Rahul Bhargava's job is to make the evidence available, not to
warrant its sufficiency. Consulting the ARC (C) before the assertion is signed is a deliberate check: the
committee sees what management is about to assert.

### 5.3 A-14 is accountable to Legal/DPO, not to Security

The intuitive owner of vendor assurance is the security function, because vendor assurance is usually
performed by sending security questionnaires and reading SOC 2 reports. CloudNimbus allocates it to Tobias
Lund as General Counsel and Data Protection Officer instead, and the reason is a specific failure the
company has already experienced.

Sub-processor obligations are contractual before they are technical. The obligation to maintain a current
sub-processor list, to give thirty days' notice of a new sub-processor and to honour a customer's right to
object, arises from the data processing addendum, not from a control standard. Whether a given vendor is a
sub-processor under that addendum is a question about what the contract says and what the vendor does with
personal data — a legal characterisation. Whether a given vendor is a **subservice organisation** for SOC 2
purposes is a different question again, turning on whether its controls are necessary, in combination with
CloudNimbus's own controls, to meet the applicable trust services criteria. A vendor can be one, both or
neither, and the three populations do not coincide.

ML-3 in the 2025 Type I management letter was precisely a failure of that classification: the vendor
register did not distinguish subservice organisations from ordinary vendors. Pinehurst did not find a
security weakness. It found that the register could not answer a definitional question, which meant the
system description could not be relied on to identify the right parties. Putting the security function in
charge of fixing that would have produced better questionnaires and the same classification problem. Tobias
Lund owns it because the distinction is one his function is competent to draw and because the downstream
obligations — notice, objection rights, deletion certification — are ones he already owns. Compliance is R
because Rahul Bhargava maintains the register operationally; Security is C because the technical assessment
of a vendor still requires it. The arithmetic that the register must reconcile to, and the definition test
itself, are set out in 01.09.

## 6. Segregation of duties in a 187-person company

Annex A control A.5.3 requires conflicting duties and conflicting areas of responsibility to be segregated.
At 187 people, some of it cannot be, and the honest thing is to name what cannot rather than to describe a
separation that does not exist in practice.

Three conflicts are structural. First, the engineers who write platform code are, in a 78-person engineering
organisation, frequently the engineers best placed to review it; the reviewer pool for any given service is
small. Second, the SRE function both requests and effects production changes, because it is the function
with production access and there is no separate operations team to hand a change to. Third — and this is the
one the programme is most exposed to — Rahul Bhargava is a single Compliance Manager who designs the
evidence architecture, operates much of it, and reports on its completeness. Key-person concentration in the
evidence function is recorded as PR-08.

What compensates is not a claim of segregation but a set of specific counterweights, two of which are
planned rather than in place and are described here as such. Peer review of changes is to be enforced in
branch protection rather than by team convention, which removes the ability of a single engineer to merge
unreviewed code regardless of who reviews it — this is the ML-2 remediation, owned by Junia Okonkwo and
targeted for 2026-06-30, and until it lands the compensating position is a convention rather than a
control. Privileged production access is reviewed quarterly under CAL-07 by Wes Delacroix, and the ML-1
remediation on the same target date moves that review's evidence into a form that supports sampling rather
than into email threads. Neither is closed at the point this document is written, and OBJ-04 tracks both.
Accountability for
evidence (A-13) sits with Compliance but accountability for the internal audit that inspects it (A-16) sits
with the ARC, which is the segregation that actually matters for the evidence function. And the outsourcing
of the clause 9.2 audit to a firm that did not design the controls provides an external pair of eyes that no
internal reallocation of duties could have produced at this headcount.

Where a duty genuinely cannot be segregated, the compensating position is detective rather than preventive,
and the programme says so rather than reclassifying a detective control as a preventive one to make a
matrix look complete.

## 7. Competence

Clause 7.2 requires the organisation to determine the competence necessary for persons doing work under its
control that affects information security performance, to ensure those persons are competent on the basis of
education, training or experience, to take action where they are not, and to retain documented information
as evidence of competence. The final limb is the one that fails audits: organisations are usually competent
and usually cannot show it.

CloudNimbus evidences competence in four ways. Role competence profiles are maintained for every role in
section 3, stating what the holder needs to know and how that was established; they are held by Hannah Brill
and reviewed when a role changes hands. Recruitment and background screening records support the education
and experience limb and are part of the CC1 control set. Annual security awareness training, with completion
tracked against all 187 staff to the ≥ 95% target in OBJ-08, plus delivery within fourteen days of hire,
covers the general population under CAL-12. And role-specific training — secure coding for the engineering
population, privacy and data subject request handling for the Customer Success and Legal populations,
incident response for SRE — is recorded against the individual rather than the team, because a completion
statistic for a team does not evidence competence for a person.

The competence of the programme's own leadership is evidenced the same way and is not exempt. Where the
clause 5.3 assignment rests on a written document rather than a title, the competence of the person named in
it is a fair question for an auditor to ask, and the answer needs to be a record rather than a reputation.
