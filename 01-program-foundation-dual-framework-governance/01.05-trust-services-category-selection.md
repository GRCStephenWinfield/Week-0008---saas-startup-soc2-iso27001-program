## 1. Purpose and starting point

This is a decision chapter. It records which trust services categories CloudNimbus selected for the
Type II examination, the argument for each, and — more usefully — the two arguments that were made
*against* inclusion and lost.

The starting point is not a blank page. Obligation **O1**, which appears in every one of the 640
customer master services agreements, requires CloudNimbus to maintain a current SOC 2 Type II report
covering **at least Security, Availability and Confidentiality**, available under NDA on request.
Three categories is therefore the contractual floor, and the least-effort conformant answer to the
question "what should we examine?" is exactly those three: 33 Common Criteria plus 3 Availability
plus 2 Confidentiality, 38 criteria in total, a scope that satisfies every contract CloudNimbus has
signed.

CloudNimbus is examining **five categories and 61 criteria**. This document has to justify the
difference, and it has to do so on grounds better than ambition.

## 2. Who decides, and against what

The selection of trust services categories is **management's decision, not the auditor's**. The
service auditor examines the categories management selects and describes, and reports on the
applicable criteria for those categories. Ashcombe and Doyle did not tell CloudNimbus to take five
categories and could not have; the categories are part of the subject matter management presents.

That makes the selection a scoping act with real consequences in both directions. Selecting a
category commits CloudNimbus to meeting every applicable criterion within it — **there is no
partial category**, no "Privacy except P2", and no equivalent of the Statement of Applicability's
justified exclusion. This is the asymmetry 01.06 builds the integration model around: an Annex A
control may be excluded with justification and the certificate remains valid; an applicable trust
services criterion cannot be left unaddressed and the opinion remain unmodified.

Within CloudNimbus, accountability for the selection process sits with Karim Haddad as VP Security
and Trust (activity A-03 in the RACI at 01.08). The decision itself was taken by **Marisol Vega on
2026-01-26** as executive sponsor, recorded as **DEC-102** and argued in **ADR-0002**. The split is
deliberate: the person accountable for delivering the scope should recommend it, and the person
accountable for the budget and the external engagements should own the decision to accept it.

## 3. The three that were never in doubt

**Security** is not a choice. The Common Criteria are the floor of every SOC 2 examination
regardless of which other categories are selected, and they carry 33 of the 61 criteria in scope.
There is no version of this programme in which Security is absent.

**Availability** is contractual and operational. Customers commit their staff to published shifts
against the platform and run payroll from its outputs, and payroll has a cut-off. An hour of
unavailability at the wrong moment is not an inconvenience; it is a payroll run that misses its
window, which is a legal and human problem for the employer and 15,000 people, not an SLA credit
conversation. CloudNimbus carries a contractual **99.9% monthly** availability commitment, and the
disaster recovery objectives — RTO 4 hours, RPO 15 minutes — exist because of it. Examining
Availability puts the three A1 criteria over capacity management, environmental protections, and
backup and recovery in front of the reader who is relying on that commitment.

**Confidentiality** follows from the data. Compensation information — rates, differentials,
earnings components, reimbursement amounts — is designated confidential under the master services
agreement, and the control that actually protects it in a multi-tenant platform is tenant
isolation. C1.1 and C1.2 address the identification and protection of confidential information and
its disposal when no longer required, which for CloudNimbus means logical segregation between 640
tenants and a deletion path that actually completes on termination.

## 4. Processing Integrity — the first hard one

Most SaaS providers exclude Processing Integrity, and **most of them are right to**.

Processing integrity addresses the **completeness, validity, accuracy, timeliness and
authorisation** of system **processing** to meet the entity's objectives. The word doing the work
is *processing*. A system that accepts a document, stores it, indexes it and hands it back
unaltered does not process in that sense. Its integrity concerns are already covered by Security
and Confidentiality, and selecting Processing Integrity for such a system produces five criteria
answered with input-validation controls and a data-quality policy nobody reads — a category taken
for marketing reasons that adds evidence burden and tells the reader nothing.

CloudNimbus does not store and retrieve. It **calculates**. The calculation engine derives overtime
eligibility and premium, shift differentials, accrual balances that carry forward across periods,
and reimbursement amounts after policy evaluation. Those outputs leave the platform through the
integration tier into the customer's payroll provider, and a payroll provider does not sanity-check
an amount — it pays it. The chain runs from a clock-in event on a phone to money in a named
individual's account, and CloudNimbus owns the arithmetic in the middle of it.

Every element of the PI1 series is therefore live rather than nominal. **Completeness**: did every
clock event and every approved expense reach the calculation? **Validity**: were the rules applied
the ones the employer configured? **Accuracy**: is the derived number right, and is there a
reconciliation that would detect it if it were not? **Timeliness**: did the output reach payroll
before the cut-off? **Authorisation**: was the rule change that altered the calculation approved by
someone entitled to approve it? A rounding rule applied at the wrong boundary, or an accrual
carry-forward that drops a fractional balance, does not announce itself. It runs for months, it is
paid, and it is discovered by a state labour claim.

Grete Lindqvist, Principal Product Manager for the Calculation Engine, owns Processing Integrity.
The category is included, and the honest framing is the one that appears in ADR-0002: **this is the
rare SaaS where Processing Integrity is the product rather than a formality, and excluding it while
selling a calculation engine would be a decision about what not to tell people.**

## 5. Privacy — the argument that was made against it and lost

This is the real decision in the chapter, and the case for exclusion was strong enough that it was
written up properly before it was refused.

### The case for excluding Privacy

The privacy criteria address personal information that **the entity** collects, uses, retains,
discloses and disposes of to meet **the entity's** objectives related to privacy — objectives which
derive from the entity's own commitments and from the requirements applicable to it. CloudNimbus
processes employee personal information on the instructions of the employer. The employer is the
party with the employment relationship, the party that decides that time will be captured and that
location will be recorded at clock-in, the party that issues the workplace privacy notice, and the
party the individual can actually complain to.

Read against that reality, most of the eighteen privacy criteria appear to flow through the
customer. Notice under P1.1 is delivered in the employment context by the employer. Choice and
consent under P2.1 is not meaningfully exercisable against one's employer, and where any lawful
basis is chosen it is the employer that chooses it. Access under P5.1 and P5.2 reaches CloudNimbus
as an assisted request routed through the customer, which is exactly how obligation O6 in the data
processing addendum describes it. Correction, disclosure to third parties, and quality — P5.2, P6
and P7 — are all defined in the addendum as things CloudNimbus does *at the customer's direction*.

The orthodox answer to this situation is well established and entirely respectable: **take
Confidentiality and let the controller carry notice and choice.** Confidentiality protects the
information without asserting a relationship with the individual that the service provider does not
have. It is the answer most B2B processors give, and there is no dishonesty in it.

The delivery argument reinforced the case. Privacy is the most evidence-hungry of the four
supplementary categories — eighteen criteria, seven of them in the P6 disclosure series alone,
several requiring evidence of processes that must demonstrably *complete* rather than merely exist.
Adding it to a first Type II, in the same year as a first ISMS certification, on 4.6 FTE, was
recorded as programme risk **PR-07**: *Privacy criteria prove unworkable for a processor and the
category has to be withdrawn late.*

### Why the argument was refused

One fact defeats it: **the mobile app captures geolocation at clock-in, directly from the data
subject, through CloudNimbus's own interface.**

That is direct collection. The individual is not handing information to their employer, who then
passes it to a supplier. They are looking at a CloudNimbus screen on their own device, pressing a
CloudNimbus button, and CloudNimbus is reading the device's location in that moment. There is no
intermediary in the interaction, whatever the contract behind it says about who instructs whom.

P1.1 requires the entity to provide **notice to data subjects** about its privacy practices to meet
the entity's objectives related to privacy. A notice obligation about a collection the individual
experiences as CloudNimbus's cannot be wholly delegated to the employer, because the employer is
not present at the moment of collection and cannot describe practices — retention periods,
sub-processors, regions — that are CloudNimbus's own. The same logic runs through the rest of the
series. Retention and disposal under P4 are executed by a CloudNimbus job on a CloudNimbus schedule
against CloudNimbus storage; the employer cannot see whether it ran. Disclosure to third parties
under P6 is a question about CloudNimbus's sub-processors, chosen by CloudNimbus and notified under
obligation O5. Monitoring and enforcement under P8 is about CloudNimbus's own privacy compliance
programme.

The second reason is about what a report communicates. A report covering Confidentiality but not
Privacy tells the reader that CloudNimbus keeps compensation data secret. It says nothing about what
CloudNimbus does with location data captured from 1.24 million people who never selected it as a
supplier and have no channel to it. Those individuals are stakeholder SH-22 in the register at
01.10: highest interest, no influence whatsoever. Selecting Privacy does not give them a channel,
and this document will not pretend otherwise. What it does is put CloudNimbus's own privacy
practices in front of an independent examiner instead of behind a contractual argument about who
the controller is.

Tobias Lund, General Counsel and Data Protection Officer, owns the privacy commitments and the
category. The counter-argument was not weak, and it is preserved in ADR-0002 under "alternatives
considered and rejected" rather than paraphrased away, because a decision recorded without its
opposition is not a decision, it is an announcement.

## 6. What the decision costs

Precision matters here, so both numbers are stated.

Security, Availability, Processing Integrity and Confidentiality together comprise **33 + 3 + 5 + 2
= 43 criteria**. Adding Privacy adds **18**, for **43 + 18 = 61**. That is a **42% increase in the
number of applicable criteria for one category**, and Privacy is not a cheap eighteen: P4 requires
demonstrable completion of retention and deletion, P6 requires a defensible sub-processor and
disclosure position across seven criteria, and P8 requires evidence that privacy complaints and
compliance are actually monitored rather than nominally owned.

There is no partial adoption available to soften it. Having selected the category, CloudNimbus is
committed to all eighteen for the full six-month window.

## 8. Over-delivering against O1, deliberately

O1 requires three categories. CloudNimbus is examining five. That is over-delivery against a
contract, and over-delivery has to be justified rather than admired.

The **commercial** justification is measurable. The 118 enterprise-tier customers and the prospects
behind them do not ask "does your report cover Security, Availability and Confidentiality?" They
send questionnaires asking how pay calculations are validated, whether location data is retained
and for how long, who the sub-processors are and how they are notified. Today those questions are
answered by hand, by Sales and Customer Success staff reading from a spreadsheet, and the mean
**elapsed turnaround** is 11.4 days — eleven days during which the deal sits in security review. A five-category report answers them from an examined source, which is
what OBJ-05 is targeting when it aims to take that mean to three days or fewer.

The **honesty** justification is the one that decided it. CloudNimbus calculates money and captures
location. A report that covers neither, while covering the categories the enterprise MSA template
happened to name when it was adopted in 2024, would be a report scoped by what CloudNimbus was
asked for rather than by what CloudNimbus does. The programme took the position that the scope of assurance should follow the
shape of the risk, and that if the two disagree it is the contract that is out of date.

## 9. What could make this wrong

Two named programme risks bear directly on this decision, and both are consequences of it rather
than objections to it.

**PR-01** — the five-category scope proves larger than a 4.6-FTE programme can evidence, and a
category has to be dropped mid-window. **PR-07** — Privacy proves unworkable for a processor and
the category has to be withdrawn late.

The programme's position on both is the same, and it is uncomfortable on purpose: **a category
cannot be quietly dropped once the window is open.** Withdrawing a category mid-window means
restating the scope, re-cutting the description, telling the service auditor that the subject matter
has changed, and — for anyone who was told the report would cover five categories — explaining why
it does not. That is a governance event requiring Trust Committee escalation and Audit and Risk
Committee visibility, not a scope adjustment. Naming it that way now is the control: it removes the
easy exit before anyone is tired enough to want it, which is precisely the point at which a
category gets dropped for reasons that are later described as scope refinement.
