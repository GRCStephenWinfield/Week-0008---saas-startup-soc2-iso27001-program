## 1. Purpose

A risk assessment is not a list of bad things. It is a **process**, and ISO/IEC 27001:2022 is unusually
specific about that: clause 6.1.2 requires the organisation to *define and apply* an information security
risk assessment process, and then sets out, limb by limb, what that process must do. The register is the
output. This document is the process, and it is the document a Stage 1 auditor will ask for before looking
at a single rating, because a defensible register produced by an undefined method is a coincidence.

It records **ADR-0011** and **DEC-301** (one register serving both frameworks, decided by Karim Haddad on
2026-03-27) and **DEC-305** (the register is scoped to the ISMS boundary, not the SOC 2 system, 2026-04-02).

## 2. What clause 6.1.2 requires, limb by limb

The clause has five lettered limbs and this method answers each of them in order rather than in aggregate.

**6.1.2 a) — establish and maintain risk criteria, including risk acceptance criteria and criteria for
performing risk assessments.** Both are in 03.02. The criteria for performing an assessment are the 5 × 5
likelihood and impact scales with their defined anchors; the risk acceptance criteria are the band
thresholds together with the rule about who may accept what. Note that the clause requires the acceptance
criteria to exist *before* the assessment, not after it. Criteria written once the ratings are known are
criteria fitted to a conclusion.

**6.1.2 b) — ensure that repeated assessments produce consistent, valid and comparable results.** This is
the limb most methods fail, and §7 below sets out the four devices that make it true here.

**6.1.2 c) — identify the risks.** Limb c) 1) requires the process to be applied to identify risks
associated with the loss of **confidentiality, integrity and availability** of information within the ISMS
scope. Limb c) 2) requires the **risk owners** to be identified. Both limbs are mandatory and the second is
the one most often reduced to a departmental label; §6 explains what CloudNimbus required of an owner
instead.

**6.1.2 d) — analyse the risks:** assess the potential consequences if a risk materialises, assess the
realistic likelihood of its occurrence, and determine the levels of risk. The word *realistic* is doing
work. A likelihood assessment that treats every scenario as plausible produces a register in which nothing
is distinguishable from anything else.

**6.1.2 e) — evaluate the risks:** compare the analysis against the criteria established under a), and
prioritise the analysed risks for treatment. Evaluation is a separate step from analysis, and collapsing
the two is why so many registers have a score column and no prioritisation.

The clause closes by requiring **documented information about the process** to be retained. These fourteen
documents, the register workbook and the decision log are that documented information.

## 3. One register, not two

The obvious alternative was two registers: an ISO register organised for clause 6.1.2, and a SOC 2 risk
assessment organised for CC3.1 to CC3.4. It was rejected as **ADR-0011**, and the reasoning is not
administrative convenience.

The trust services criteria do not ask for a different assessment. **CC3.1** requires objectives specified
with sufficient clarity to enable identification and assessment of risks relating to them; CloudNimbus's
objectives at that level are the ten principal service commitments SC-01 to SC-10 and the twelve system
requirements SR-01 to SR-12 that Phase 02 fixed, which is why the impact scale in 03.02 is anchored on a
breach of a named commitment rather than on an abstraction. **CC3.2** requires risks to the achievement of
those objectives to be identified across the entity and analysed as a basis for determining how they should
be managed — which is clause 6.1.2 c) and d) in different words, with the same output. **CC3.3** requires
the potential for **fraud** to be considered. **CC3.4** requires identification and assessment of **changes**
that could significantly affect the system of internal control.

Two registers would have produced two populations, two rating conventions and, within a quarter, two
different answers to the same question — at which point the honest response to a service auditor asking
"what is your highest risk?" would have been "which register are you reading?". One register with one scale
answers once. Where the frameworks genuinely diverge, they diverge in what is done with an entry, not in
whether the entry exists: an Annex A control determination in 03.09 and 03.10, a trust services criterion
mapping in 03.12.

**Fraud, under CC3.3, is addressed inside the taxonomy rather than as an appendix.** **TH-03**, a malicious
or negligent insider, is the fraud-facing threat source, and it produces **R-07**. The fraud scenario that
matters most in this business is not theft of data but manipulation of a derived amount — the calculation
engine computes what people are paid, and a deliberate alteration of a rule or an override would reach
money. That scenario is assessed under **R-02**, which is rated for consequence rather than for motive: a
wrong amount reaching a payroll run is equally serious whether a defect or a person put it there, and the
detective control that catches it is the same reconciliation either way.

**Change, under CC3.4**, is handled by making the register a live object rather than an annual artefact.
The assessment is repeated at **CAL-06**, quarterly, and on significant change; a significant change to the
ISMS or its scope also triggers the **O10** notification obligation to the certification body within 30
days. **R-30** and **R-32** exist because documented information and a scope statement are exactly the
things that go stale between reviews.

## 4. Scope of the assessment — the ISMS boundary, not the system

**DEC-305** scoped the register to the **ISMS boundary**: the whole of CloudNimbus, Inc. That means the
assessed population is **1,046 assets, not 800**, and the 246-asset difference Phase 02 quantified is inside
the assessment rather than outside it.

This was argued. The case for scoping to the SOC 2 system was that the examination is the commercial
mandate, the system is where the customer data is, and assessing the corporate back office consumes a
4.6-FTE programme's attention on a finance ledger nobody will test. The case that won has two parts. First,
clause 6.1.2 c) 1) says *within the scope of the ISMS*, and the ISMS scope is the whole organisation under
ADR-0003 — so a register scoped to the platform would not have satisfied the clause it was written for.
Second, and more practically, the risks that reach customer data through the corporate estate are real and
would have been invisible: **R-13**, an employee phished, does not require the employee to be an engineer;
**R-36**, mishandling of a CloudNimbus employee's own personal data, sits entirely outside the system;
**R-25**, the evidence architecture depending on one person, is a programme risk rather than a platform
risk and is one of the more likely things on the list to actually happen.

The reverse test was applied and produced nothing that needed removing. No entry in the register is there
only because the ISMS is wide. Every entry attaches either to an asset class, a data flow, a commitment or
an obligation that Phase 02 recorded.

## 5. How the risks were identified

Identification ran from six sources, deliberately overlapping, because a single-source identification
produces a register shaped like its source.

**The asset inventory** AC-01 to AC-12 supplied the confidentiality, integrity and availability question
asked class by class: what does the loss of each look like for this class of asset, and is that loss already
somewhere in the register? **The twelve data flows** DF-01 to DF-12 supplied the paths — ten carrying
personal data, three crossing a subservice organisation — and are the reason **R-01** is phrased as a defect
in an authorisation or query path rather than as "a data breach". **The commitments and system requirements**
SC-01 to SC-10 and SR-01 to SR-12 supplied the consequences: a risk is interesting here when its
materialisation breaks a promise CloudNimbus has made in writing, which is what **R-18**, **R-22** and
**R-23** are.

**The obligations register** O1 to O12 supplied the deadline-shaped risks — the 48-hour incident
notification, the 10-business-day data subject request assistance, the 30-day sub-processor notice, the
residency commitment — and each of those became an entry because a deadline is a control that can be missed.
**The 2025 Type I management letter** supplied three entries directly: ML-1 became **R-10**, ML-2 became
**R-08**, and ML-3 became **R-16**. That was deliberate. A management letter matter that has been closed
without being assessed as a risk has been closed on somebody's word.

**The load-bearing claims carried out of Phases 01 and 02** supplied the last group, and this is the source
most programmes skip. Three of them produced register entries, and the provenance of the three is not the
same — which matters, because a reader is entitled to know whether what is being demoted to a risk was an
assumption or a requirement.

**AS-01** and **AS-02** are Phase 01 **assumptions**. Phase 01's assumption log carries three — AS-01,
AS-02 and AS-03 — and two of them are load-bearing here. AS-01, that scheduled-job failures are alerted,
and AS-02, that row-level security is uniformly enforced across every data access path, were both recorded
as assumptions rather than as facts. Phase 02 then did the useful thing with them: it converted each into a
**testable system requirement**, AS-01 into **SR-08** — retention enforced by scheduled deletion jobs whose
failure is alerted — and AS-02 into **SR-01** — multi-tenant isolation enforced at the data layer on every
access path. Phase 03 takes the third step and converts each into a rated register entry: AS-01 and SR-08
became **R-06**, AS-02 and SR-01 became **R-01**.

**SR-12 is not an assumption and never was.** It is a **Phase 02 system requirement**, created under
**ADR-0007** — non-production environments hold no production customer data — and its significance is that
four scope exclusions, **EX-01 to EX-04**, rest on it. It is a claim about a control rather than a fact
about the architecture, which is what ADR-0007 said in terms, and a control-dependent boundary is a boundary
that can fail. So SR-12 became **R-07**.

The rhetorical point survives the correction and is stronger for being made accurately: **an assumption is a
risk promoted to a fact without evidence, and a control-dependent boundary is a scope decision resting on
one.** In both cases the correct treatment is to demote the claim to something rated, owned and testable,
and all three — AS-01, AS-02 and SR-12 — became register entries for that reason.

## 6. Risk owners

Clause 6.1.2 c) 2) requires risk owners to be identified, and clause 6.1.3 f) requires those owners to
approve the treatment plan and accept the residual risks. Those two clauses together mean an owner cannot
be a department.

CloudNimbus applied one test: **the owner is the person who can authorise the treatment and would be asked
to explain the failure.** That produced ten named owners across thirty-six entries, all of them from
the roles Phase 01 fixed, and it produced some assignments that look uncomfortable and are correct. **R-25**
— the evidence architecture depending on one person — is owned by **Marisol Vega**, the executive sponsor,
because the person it depends on cannot own the risk of depending on him. **R-14** — a customer failing to
perform CUEC-05 — is owned by **Ana-Sofia Cruz**, even though the control in question is the customer's,
because the only lever CloudNimbus has is communication and she owns it. **R-13** is owned by **Karim
Haddad** rather than by Hannah Brill, because phishing resistance is an authentication decision before it is
a training decision.

Where a risk plainly spanned two owners, one was named and the other recorded in the treatment item. A
register with two owners on a row has none.

## 7. Consistent, valid and comparable results

Clause 6.1.2 b) is a promise about *repetition*: the same risk, assessed twice, should come out the same
way, and two different risks assessed once should be comparable. Four devices make that true here.

**Defined anchors rather than adjectives.** Likelihood 4 means "expected at least once a year" and
likelihood 3 means "expected within the certification cycle — once in three years". Those are testable
claims about frequency. "Likely" and "possible" are not.

**An impact scale tied to this business's failure modes** rather than to a generic severity ladder. Impact 5
is cross-tenant exposure of compensation data or a wrong amount reaching pay at scale. A rater does not have
to decide what "major" means.

**A rating is set in a session with more than one rater present.** The baseline was rated across three
working sessions in March and early April 2026 with the risk owner, the Compliance Manager and at least one
person from outside the owning function present, and the rationale recorded on the row. A single rater is
consistent with himself and with nobody else.

**Movement is governed by rules, not by judgement in the moment.** The three rules in 03.02 — ratings move
on likelihood, likelihood 1 is reserved, eight is a floor — are what make the second assessment comparable
to the first. Without them, the most reliable predictor of a risk's rating is how recently somebody
presented it to a committee.

## 8. Analysis, evaluation and what happens next

Analysis under 6.1.2 d) produced a level of risk for each entry: consequence and likelihood assessed
separately, multiplied, banded. Evaluation under 6.1.2 e) compared each level against the acceptance
criteria in 03.02 and prioritised for treatment, which is why the seven High entries are analysed
individually in 03.05 and the Low entries are not.

Treatment then runs under **clause 6.1.3**, whose six limbs 03.06 walks in order: **a)** select treatment
options, **b)** determine all controls necessary to implement them, **c)** compare those controls against
Annex A to verify none has been omitted, **d)** produce the Statement of Applicability, **e)** formulate the
treatment plan, and **f)** obtain risk owners' approval of the plan and acceptance of the residual risks.
Limb c) is a verification step and not a starting point — 03.08 makes that argument in full, because it is
the difference between a management system and a checklist.

**Clause 8.2** requires the assessment to be performed **at planned intervals** and when significant changes
are proposed or occur. The planned interval is **CAL-06**, quarterly, with the first review of this baseline
scheduled for June 2026. **Clause 8.3** requires the treatment plan to be implemented, which is what the
2026-07-01 constraint in 03.06 is about.

## 9. What this method does not do

It does not produce a probability. The likelihood scale is ordinal and the multiplication is a ranking
device, not a calculation of expected loss; a score of 20 is not twice as bad as a score of 10 and this
document does not pretend otherwise. It does not quantify anything in money — no annualised loss expectancy
is computed, and no fine, penalty or fee appears anywhere in the register, because the register does not
reach legal conclusions and a compliance document that priced one would be doing so on no basis.

And it does not assert completeness. The register is what a defined method applied to a recorded population
found on 2026-04-10. **R-34** exists precisely because an asset missing from the inventory is outside every
control applied to its class, and by construction outside this assessment too.

## 10. Decision record

**ADR-0011 — one risk register serving clause 6.1.2 and CC3, scoped to the ISMS boundary.** Decided by
Karim Haddad on 2026-03-27; logged as **DEC-301**, with the scoping limb logged separately as **DEC-305** on
2026-04-02. Alternatives considered and rejected: two registers organised by framework, rejected because it
guarantees divergent answers to the same question; and a single register scoped to the SOC 2 system,
rejected because clause 6.1.2 c) 1) is explicit that the population is the ISMS scope, and because three of
the entries that most deserve attention sit outside the platform.
