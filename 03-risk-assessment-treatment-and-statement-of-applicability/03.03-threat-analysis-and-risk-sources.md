## 1. Purpose

Clause 6.1.2 c) 1) requires the risk assessment process to identify risks associated with the loss of
confidentiality, integrity and availability of information within the ISMS scope. It does not prescribe how,
and the how matters, because identification is the step where a register acquires its shape and its blind
spots at the same time.

CloudNimbus identified risks by crossing a population — the 1,046 assets, twelve data flows, twelve personal
data categories, ten commitments and twelve obligations Phase 02 and Phase 01 recorded — against a fixed
list of **risk sources**: the things that make a loss happen. This document is that list, TH-01 to TH-14, the
count of what it produced, and the finding the count produced, which is the most useful thing in this phase
for anyone running a services business.

## 2. Why a taxonomy, and what kind

A threat taxonomy does two jobs. It makes identification systematic, so that the register is the output of a
sweep rather than of a brainstorm. And it makes the register **countable by source**, which turns a list of
worries into evidence about where the organisation actually fails.

The taxonomy here is deliberately short and deliberately mixed. Fourteen entries is few enough that every
one of them was applied to every asset class and every data flow in a working session without the exercise
collapsing under its own weight. And it mixes categories that a purist would separate: **TH-01** and
**TH-03** are actors, **TH-05** and **TH-06** are defects, **TH-07** is a configuration state, **TH-08** is a
failure mode, **TH-10** is an infrastructure event, **TH-13** is somebody else's omission, and **TH-14** is
an absence. Mixing them is defensible for one reason: the purpose is coverage of the ways loss arrives, not
elegance of classification. A taxonomy that admits only adversaries is elegant and misses most of what goes
wrong.

Guidance in ISO/IEC 27002:2022 and the risk-management guidance in **ISO/IEC 27005:2022** — a single
standard, not a series — informed the shape of the list; neither is a requirement, and neither prescribes a
taxonomy. This one is CloudNimbus's.

## 3. The taxonomy

| ID | Threat or risk source |
|---|---|
| TH-01 | An external actor seeking customer data at scale |
| TH-02 | Compromise of a privileged internal account |
| TH-03 | A malicious or negligent insider |
| TH-04 | Compromise or failure reaching CloudNimbus through a dependency or a vendor |
| TH-05 | A software defect in tenant isolation or authorisation logic |
| TH-06 | A software defect in the calculation engine |
| TH-07 | Misconfiguration of a cloud resource |
| TH-08 | Silent failure of an automated process |
| TH-09 | Loss of a subservice organisation's service |
| TH-10 | Regional or availability-zone infrastructure failure |
| TH-11 | Loss or theft of an endpoint |
| TH-12 | Social engineering and phishing |
| TH-13 | Non-performance by a user entity of a control disclosed to it |
| TH-14 | An activity not performed, performed late, or performed and not evidenced |

Four of these are specific to what CloudNimbus is, and they are the four that would be missing from a
generic list. **TH-06** exists because the calculation engine is the product rather than a feature: a defect
there does not corrupt a record, it changes what somebody is paid. **TH-09** exists because two subservice
organisations sit inside the system and outside the ISMS, and their unavailability is not a CloudNimbus
control failure but is very much a CloudNimbus risk. **TH-13** exists because eleven complementary user
entity controls were disclosed, and a disclosed control is a control CloudNimbus has stated it depends on
and cannot operate. **TH-14** exists because the programme read its own management letter.

## 5. The finding: TH-14 generates fifteen of the thirty-six

**TH-14 — an activity not performed, performed late, or performed and not evidenced — produces fifteen of
the thirty-six register entries.** No other source produces more than three. The four sources that involve a
human adversary — TH-01 an external actor, TH-02 a compromised privileged account, TH-03 an insider, TH-12
social engineering — produce **four entries between them**.

That ratio is the most useful thing this phase learned about CloudNimbus, and it is not flattering to the
way security programmes are usually described. **The modal risk in a services business is not an attacker.
It is a control that was supposed to run and did not, or ran and left nothing behind.** An access review that
slips two weeks past quarter end. A change that goes out because the reviewer was on leave and the
convention did not survive their absence. A sub-processor engaged in a busy month before the thirty-day
notice went out. A data subject request that sat in a shared mailbox. A deletion job that returned a zero
and nobody read the zero. None of those requires anybody to want to harm CloudNimbus or its customers. All
of them require only that an ordinary organisation be ordinarily busy.

Three qualifications keep this from being a slogan.

**The count is a count of entries, not of severity.** TH-14 produces **no High ratings at all** — its fifteen
entries split eight Moderate and seven Low. The High band is driven by consequence, and consequence is driven
by defects, compromises and dependencies rather than by omissions. So TH-14 dominates *volume* while other
sources dominate *severity*, and the two facts are complementary rather than contradictory. A programme that
spent all its attention on TH-14 would leave the seven worst entries untreated.

**The seven High ratings arise from seven different sources**, no two alike: R-01 from TH-05, R-02 from
TH-06, R-03 from TH-02, R-04 from TH-09, R-05 from TH-07, R-06 from TH-08, R-07 from TH-03. There is no
single adversary or single failure mode concentrating the top of this register. That is worth stating,
because it means the treatment plan cannot buy down the High band with one investment.

**The taxonomy's shape partly explains the count.** TH-14 is broad — it covers any activity, in any
function, that should have happened. It could have been decomposed into six narrower sources and would then
have produced six counts of two or three. It was not decomposed, deliberately, because the useful property
of the entry is precisely that it is one thing: **the failure of an organisation to do what its own
procedures say it does**, whatever the procedure. Splitting it would have made the taxonomy tidier and
concealed the finding.

## 6. Why this is also the modal SOC 2 exception

The connection worth drawing is between TH-14 and what a Type II examination actually reports.

A SOC 2 Type II tests operating effectiveness across a period. The service auditor samples occurrences of a
control and asks whether each one happened, happened on time, and can be shown to have happened. A **test
exception** — the SOC 2 term; a nonconformity is the ISO term and the two are not interchangeable — is
recorded when a sampled occurrence fails one of those three tests. That is TH-14's definition, almost word
for word: *not performed, performed late, or performed and not evidenced*.

CloudNimbus does not have to speculate about this. It has a management letter. The **2025 Type I** from
Pinehurst Assurance Group carried an unmodified opinion — a Type I opines on suitability of design as of a
date, has no tests of operating effectiveness, and therefore cannot produce a test exception at all — and
attached three matters. **ML-1**: access review evidence was retained as email threads and screenshots, in a
form that would not support a Type II sample. That is *performed and not evidenced*. **ML-2**:
change-management peer review was enforced by team convention rather than by branch protection — design
adequate, enforcement not. That is *may not be performed, and cannot be shown to have been*. **ML-3**: the
vendor register did not distinguish subservice organisations from ordinary vendors, so assurance obligations
were not applied to the parties that needed them. That is an activity not performed against the right
population.

Two of those three are TH-14 in its purest form, and they are in the register as **R-10** and **R-08**, both
rated 4 × 3 = 12 Moderate, both carrying likelihood 4 — *expected at least once a year* — because the
organisation has already been told they occur. Rating them lower than the evidence supports would have been
the register's first dishonesty. **ML-3 became R-16**, which sits at 3 × 3 = 9 because vendor
classification is performed a few times a quarter rather than continuously. Those three — R-08, R-10 and
R-16 — are the register's entire inheritance from the management letter, and **R-09 is not among them**:
its likelihood of 4 is inferred from the design of the provisioning control rather than from a finding, as
03.04 §5 sets out.

The lesson the programme carries from this, and states here rather than in hindsight, is that **remediating
a control is not the same as remediating the evidence trail it leaves behind**. A control that has been
fixed but still produces its evidence as a screenshot in an inbox has moved from one TH-14 failure mode to
another. The treatment items in 03.06 for R-08, R-09 and R-10 are therefore evidence-architecture items as
much as control items, and that is why.

## 7. What the taxonomy does not claim

It does not claim to be intelligence. CloudNimbus has eight people in Security and no threat intelligence
function; the list was compiled from the environment, the prior management letter, the data flows and the
obligations, not from a feed. **A.5.7 threat intelligence** is determined necessary in the Statement of
Applicability and stands at **Planned** at v1.0, which is the honest status of a control that does not yet
exist, and **A.5.6 contact with special interest groups** is the control that will make A.5.7 work at a
company this size — a point 03.11 develops, because A.5.6 is the control most often dismissed as a
formality.

It does not claim stability. TH-01 is a single-entry source today because CloudNimbus's externally exposed
surface is small and the data an external actor would want is behind authentication that a subservice
organisation performs. That is a statement about the current architecture, and it is exactly the sort of
statement that a penetration test can contradict. Testing is scheduled for May 2026 and this document does
not know its outcome; if it produces a finding that no threat in this list anticipated, the taxonomy is
wrong and gets amended, and **DEC-306** left the register open to additions on evidence for that reason.

It does not claim that a single-entry source is unimportant. **TH-02** produces one entry, **R-03**, and that
entry is rated 15 High. **TH-06** produces one entry, **R-02**, also 15 High. Frequency in this table is a
property of how many distinct ways a source can express itself in this environment, not of how much it
matters.

## 8. What the analysis fed forward

The taxonomy feeds three later documents. 03.04 carries the threat identifier on every register row, which
is what makes the counting in §4 possible at all. 03.05 uses the source as the frame for each High analysis,
because "what drives the likelihood" is a question about the source and "what drives the impact" is a
question about the asset. And 03.06 groups treatment items by the source they act on, which is how the plan
avoids buying the same control twice — the four TH-14 entries R-08, R-09, R-10 and R-21 are all treated in
part by the same evidence architecture, and pretending they were four unrelated problems would have produced
four unrelated tools.
