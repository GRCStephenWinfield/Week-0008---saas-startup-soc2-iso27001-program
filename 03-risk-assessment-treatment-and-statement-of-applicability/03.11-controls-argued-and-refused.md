## 1. Why this document exists

A Statement of Applicability that lists 91 necessary controls and two exclusions tells a reader what was
decided. It does not tell them what was argued, and the arguments are where the document's credibility
sits.

Six controls were seriously proposed for exclusion during the determination. **Two were excluded** —
**A.7.11 supporting utilities** and **A.7.12 cabling security** — under **DEC-309** on 2026-04-20. **Four
were refused** — **A.7.4 physical security monitoring**, **A.7.8 equipment siting and protection**,
**A.7.10 storage media** and **A.7.13 equipment maintenance** — under **DEC-310** on the same day, by the
same person, in the same session. Both decisions are recorded in **ADR-0013**.

All six proposals came from the same theme and the same fact: **CloudNimbus is remote-first and operates no
data centre.** The 4,100 square foot Denver suite is a meeting space. All processing runs on infrastructure
operated by a subservice organisation. Where a physical control set assumes a building full of equipment,
CloudNimbus has 231 laptops distributed across 31 states and 4 countries and one room with a coffee
machine. That fact is true, and it justifies exactly two exclusions and not six. **Distinguishing the two
it justifies from the four it does not is the entire content of this document**, and the distinction is
finer than it first appears.

## 2. The two exclusions, and the distinction they rest on

| Control | Justification recorded in the Statement of Applicability |
|---|---|
| **A.7.11** Supporting utilities | CloudNimbus operates no facility containing information processing equipment. The Denver suite has no server, no network closet and no comms room, and its power and internet service are the landlord's building services. There is no supporting utility of CloudNimbus's serving information processing |
| **A.7.12** Cabling security | CloudNimbus operates no facility containing information processing equipment and owns no power or telecommunications cabling carrying information or supporting information services. The suite's cabling is the landlord's building infrastructure serving a meeting space |

A.7.11 concerns the protection of information processing facilities from **power failure and other
disruptions caused by failures in supporting utilities** — power, telecommunications, water supply,
ventilation, air conditioning. A.7.12 concerns the protection from interception, interference or damage of
**cabling carrying power, data or supporting information services**. Both controls presuppose that the
organisation has a facility housing information processing equipment. CloudNimbus does not.

That is the whole justification, and its form is what matters.

### 2.1 The exclusion is justified by the absence of the thing the control protects

**Not by the presence of a supplier.** This is the distinction the document exists to draw, and it is
routinely collapsed.

AWS operates supporting utilities and cabling at a scale CloudNimbus could not approach: redundant power,
cooling, and network at every facility supporting `us-east-1`, `us-west-2` and `eu-central-1`. It would
have been easy — and it is very common — to write the justification as *"managed by our cloud provider"*
and move on. **CloudNimbus did not, because that is not an exclusion justification. It is a carve-out.**

The two are different instruments answering different questions.

| | Exclusion (ISO/IEC 27001 clause 6.1.3 d) | Carve-out (SOC 2, DC7) |
|---|---|---|
| The claim being made | This control is **not necessary** for our ISMS | This control **is necessary** and is performed by another organisation |
| Where it appears | The Statement of Applicability, with a justification | The description of the system, as a complementary subservice organisation control |
| What the reader should conclude | There is nothing here for this control to protect | There is something here, and the evidence about it is not in this report |
| CloudNimbus's instance | A.7.11, A.7.12 | **CSOC-01** and **CSOC-02**, disclosed under DC7 |

**CSOC-01** states the assumption that AWS performs physical and environmental protections at the
facilities supporting the three regions in use; **CSOC-02** states the assumption that AWS restricts and
authorises physical access to the infrastructure hosting customer workloads. Those entries assert that the
protections are necessary and are performed elsewhere. They are the opposite claim to an exclusion.

The practical consequence of confusing the two is severe and asymmetric. An organisation that excludes a
control on the ground that a supplier performs it has, in the same sentence, admitted the control is
necessary and declined to state who performs it or how it knows. A Stage 1 auditor reading *"managed by our
cloud provider"* in an exclusion justification has been handed the next question for free: *if the supplier
performs it, on what basis is it not necessary — and what does your supplier assurance process do about it?*
That question has no good answer, because the premise of the justification contradicts its conclusion.
CloudNimbus's justification does not have that defect: it says there is nothing here, which is a claim
about CloudNimbus's own estate, verifiable by walking through the Denver suite in about ninety seconds.

**The claim is also falsifiable, which is what makes it a justification rather than an assertion.** If
CloudNimbus places a network appliance, a NAS or a rack in the Denver suite, or takes a lease on space
containing information processing equipment, A.7.11 and A.7.12 become necessary immediately. That is a
change to the ISMS notifiable to Northgate within 30 days under obligation **O10**, and the Statement of
Applicability is reissued. The exclusion has a condition attached to it, and the condition is written down.

## 3. The four refusals

Four controls were proposed for exclusion by the same reasoning that carried A.7.11 and A.7.12, and all
four were refused.

| Control | The case made for exclusion | Why it was refused |
|---|---|---|
| **A.7.4** Physical security monitoring | The Denver suite is a meeting space used a few days a week. There is nothing to monitor and no security operations function that would watch it | The suite has **controlled entry and a visitor log**, and both are monitoring in the sense the control means. The control asks whether premises are continuously monitored for unauthorised access; it does not specify cameras or a guard. **Excluding a control because it is implemented lightly is a category error** — the answer "we monitor by access badge records and a signed visitor log, reviewed periodically" is an implementation, and the honest status is `Partial` because the review cadence is not yet defined |
| **A.7.8** Equipment siting and protection | Nothing is sited anywhere. There is no data centre, no server room and no equipment floor. Siting is a concept from a world CloudNimbus does not operate in | **231 laptops are equipment**, and 187 of them are sited in homes across 31 US states and 4 countries — on kitchen tables, in shared flats, in co-working spaces, beside windows facing streets. **Home-working siting is precisely what the control is for** in a remote-first company, and it covers protection from environmental threats, unauthorised viewing and opportunistic theft. **A.6.7 remote working does not replace it**: A.6.7 governs the working arrangement, A.7.8 governs the equipment used in it, and mapping one onto the other loses the physical protection of the device |
| **A.7.10** Storage media | Removable media is prohibited by policy. USB mass storage is blocked. There is no media to manage | **A prohibition is an implementation of the control, not a reason to exclude it.** The control asks how storage media is managed through its lifecycle — acquisition, use, transfer, disposal. "We do not permit removable media" is an answer to that question, and it is an answer that has to be **enforced on 231 endpoints and evidenced**. Status is `Partial` for exactly that reason: the policy exists and the technical enforcement is not complete on every device |
| **A.7.13** Equipment maintenance | Laptops are replaced on a refresh cycle, not maintained. There is no maintenance contract and no equipment to service | Endpoints are **patched, repaired and re-imaged under the mobile device management platform**, and that is maintenance. The control concerns equipment being correctly maintained to ensure continued availability and integrity, and an unpatched laptop is the ordinary failure it describes. The refusal also avoided an **internal inconsistency**: excluding A.7.13 while retaining **A.7.14 secure disposal or re-use of equipment** would have covered the end of the equipment lifecycle and not the middle, which is a shape no auditor would let pass without asking about it |

**A refusal has to be carried through into the Statement of Applicability's own citation column, or it is an
argument made in one document and abandoned in another.** A.7.13 is the instance where that nearly failed.
The refusal rests on MDM patching of endpoints; the row in 03.10 originally cited **R-12**, a vulnerability
in a third-party software dependency, which is a platform risk and not an endpoint one. The citation was
re-made to **R-13 and R-34** — an unpatched device is where a phished session lands, and an unenrolled
device is an asset outside every control applied to its class. The point generalises: an argued control
whose citation does not match the argument gives the auditor the same follow-up question the exclusion would
have.

### 3.1 A.7.10 is the sharpest of the four

The other three refusals turn on a factual finding — there is monitoring, there is siting, there is
maintenance. **A.7.10 turns on a conceptual error**, and it is the one most worth naming because it is the
one most often made.

The proposal ran: the control governs storage media; CloudNimbus permits no storage media; therefore the
control has no subject matter; therefore exclude. The first two premises are true and the conclusion does
not follow, because the control is not *"have removable media and protect it"*. It is *"manage storage
media through its lifecycle in accordance with the classification and handling scheme"*. **A prohibition is
a management decision about the lifecycle**, and it is one of the strongest available. An organisation that
prohibits removable media has answered the control's question more completely than one that permits
encrypted media under a register.

What follows from that is the part the exclusion would have escaped. A prohibition creates obligations of
its own: it must be **stated** in the acceptable use policy, **enforced** technically on every managed
device, **monitored** for exceptions, and **evidenced** — and there must be a defined route for the case
that needs an exception. Excluding A.7.10 would have removed all four obligations from the Statement of
Applicability while the prohibition itself remained on the books, unenforced on some fraction of 231
endpoints and evidenced nowhere. The exclusion would have been an administrative saving that made the
company less safe and less able to demonstrate what it already claimed to do.

The status recorded is `Partial`, and R-07 and R-26 are cited: production customer data reaching a
non-production environment, and an endpoint lost or stolen with customer data cached on it. Both are
scenarios in which removable media is the mechanism the prohibition removes.

## 4. An exclusion is cheap in the moment and expensive at Stage 1

The four refusals cost CloudNimbus four control implementations it could have argued its way out of. That
is the honest accounting, and it is worth being explicit about why the cost was accepted.

**The Statement of Applicability is the document a Stage 1 auditor reads first.** Stage 1 is a documented
information and readiness review; the auditor is establishing whether the ISMS is ready to be audited for
implementation and effectiveness, and the Statement of Applicability is the fastest available read on how
the organisation thinks. A reader who wants to know whether a management system is real does not start with
the policies — every organisation has policies. They start with the exclusions, because an exclusion is the
only place in the document where the organisation had to reason in public against a published reference
set.

Four exclusions in the physical theme at a company with no data centre would have looked, on first
reading, entirely reasonable. That is the trap. **The examination of an exclusion is not "is this
plausible?" but "does the justification survive one question?"** — and each of the four had a question
waiting:

- *A.7.4:* "Do you have a badge system and a visitor book?" Yes. Then you monitor.
- *A.7.8:* "Where are the laptops?" In 187 homes. Then equipment is sited.
- *A.7.10:* "Is removable media permitted?" No. Then you have managed the media lifecycle by prohibiting
  it, and I would like to see the enforcement.
- *A.7.13:* "How do laptops get patched?" Through MDM. Then equipment is maintained.

An exclusion that fails a single question is worse than no exclusion at all, because it is evidence about
the determination process rather than about the control. A justification that collapses on the first
follow-up invites the auditor to ask how the other ninety were determined, and the answer to that question
is the difference between an opportunity for improvement and a nonconformity against clause 6.1.3.

**The saving was never real anyway.** Excluding A.7.13 would not have stopped CloudNimbus patching
laptops — MDM patching was already running. It would only have removed the patching from the Statement of
Applicability, so the company would have performed the control and declined to claim it. That is the
recurring shape of a bad exclusion: it does not reduce work, it reduces the credit for work already done,
and it does so in the document least able to absorb the doubt.

## 5. Two controls that were easy to exclude and were kept without argument

Six controls reached a formal proposal. Two others were the obvious candidates and never reached one, and
they are worth naming because the reasons they survived are more interesting than the reasons the four
were refused.

### 5.1 A.8.23 web filtering

**The routine argument for excluding it, in SaaS engineering organisations, is that they trust their
engineers.** It is stated as a cultural position — filtering the browsing of senior engineers is
paternalistic, breaks legitimate research, and signals distrust to exactly the population whose retention
matters most — and it is almost always accompanied by the observation that a remote-first company has no
network chokepoint to filter at anyway.

Both halves of that are true and neither is a determination of non-necessity.

The control is about managing access to external websites to reduce exposure to malicious content. **R-13 —
an employee is successfully phished and their credentials or session are used — sits at 4 × 3 = 12
Moderate** with 187 remote staff, and the register's likelihood of 4 means "expected at least once a year".
Trust is not a control against a credential-harvesting page that renders correctly. And the absence of a
network chokepoint is an argument about *implementation* — which is why the design routes filtering through
the endpoint agent and DNS layer rather than a corporate egress point, and why the status is `Planned`
rather than `Implemented` at v1.0.

**Two features make an exclusion here conspicuous rather than routine.** First, A.8.23 is **one of the
eleven controls new in :2022**. An organisation certifying for the first time against :2022, excluding a
control the revision deliberately added, is drawing attention to the one part of the reference set the
auditor is newest to and most alert about. Second, the exclusion argument is about the organisation's
opinion of its people, and **an opinion about a population is not a determination about a threat.** The
Statement of Applicability has no column for culture, and it should not.

### 5.2 A.5.6 contact with special interest groups

**Usually dismissed as a formality** — a line item satisfied by an ISACA membership nobody uses — and it is
the control that makes **A.5.7 threat intelligence** work at CloudNimbus.

The reasoning is arithmetic before it is anything else. CloudNimbus has **8 people in its security
function** and **no threat intelligence capability**. A.5.7 requires that information relating to
information security threats be collected and analysed to produce threat intelligence. A company of this
size cannot produce strategic threat intelligence internally, and buying a commercial feed produces
tactical indicators without the context that makes them actionable — which is how organisations end up with
a subscription and no intelligence.

What it can do is **participate**: sector and cloud-security special interest groups, vendor and provider
advisory channels, coordinated disclosure and CERT contacts, and peer networks where a company of this size
hears about an attack pattern from someone who has already seen it. **A.5.6 is the input path and A.5.7 is
the process that consumes it.** Excluding A.5.6 as a formality would have left A.5.7 — one of the eleven
new controls, and one of the eight `Planned` at v1.0 — with no described source of the thing it is supposed
to analyse. It is recorded as `Business requirement` rather than `Risk` for that reason: it is necessary
because of how the ISMS has to operate, not because a register entry demands it.

That is the general lesson the two examples share. **The controls easiest to exclude are the ones whose
value is indirect** — a filtering control whose benefit is an incident that did not happen, and a
membership control whose benefit accrues to a different control entirely. An exclusion process that
measures each control on its own produces exactly these two exclusions and does not notice that it has
broken something else.

## 6. A.8.30 outsourced development — kept on a stated reading rather than on a described estate

A.8.23 and A.5.6 were kept because the arguments for excluding them were weaker than they looked. **A.8.30
outsourced development is a third case and a different one**: nobody argued for excluding it, and on the
plainest reading of its title there was nothing for it to govern. **CloudNimbus outsources no development.**
Phase 02 described an in-house engineering organisation of 78, and there is no development contract, no
offshore team and no agency anywhere behind the platform.

A control determined `Necessary` and asserted `Implemented` with no described subject matter is precisely
the shape §4 says invites the single question, and this one had it waiting: *who develops software for you?*
The answer "nobody" would have ended the conversation badly, because the row would then be an implemented
control over an activity that does not occur — which is not a strong Statement of Applicability entry, it is
an unexamined one.

**So the reading the row rests on is written down.** A.8.30's subject at CloudNimbus is **code the platform
runs that was not produced under CloudNimbus's own secure development life cycle**: the open-source
dependency estate, base container images, infrastructure modules and vendor client libraries across 63
microservices. That population is large, it executes inside the trust boundary, and the control's
requirements — that CloudNimbus's own development security requirements are applied to code produced
elsewhere, that acquisition arrangements address them, and that provenance and testing evidence is obtained
— apply to it without strain.

**A.5.21 does not cover it**, and that is the load-bearing part of the argument rather than a detail.
Managing information security in the ICT supply chain is a control about the supplier relationship: what is
required, agreed and monitored. A.8.30 is a control about the development practices that produced the code.
An open-source dependency has no supplier relationship in A.5.21's sense at all, so leaning on A.5.21 would
have left the practices unaddressed while the paperwork read complete. 03.10 §4.2 records the reading in the
Statement of Applicability itself, and the row cites **R-08 and R-12** accordingly — code that has not
passed CloudNimbus's review gate, and a dependency vulnerability exploited before it is remediated.

The general point is the one this document has been making throughout. **An exclusion has to survive a
question, and so does an inclusion.** A control kept for a reason nobody wrote down is not safer than a
control excluded for a reason that does not hold; it is the same defect with the opposite sign, and it is
harder to spot because 91 of 93 looks diligent.

**ADR-0013** records the determination as a whole: ninety-one of ninety-three necessary, two excluded, four
argued and refused. Recording the refusals in the architectural decision record rather than only in the
minutes is deliberate. A future maintainer looking at A.7.4 and wondering why a company with one meeting
room monitors it will find the argument, the date and the name, and will not have to reconstruct the
reasoning from a control that looks disproportionate to its estate.

The refusals are also the standing answer to a question the Statement of Applicability will attract for as
long as it exists: **why does 91 of 93 not read as an organisation that failed to think?** It reads that
way only if the two exclusions were the easy ones. They were the hard ones — the two claims that survive a
question — and the four easy ones are recorded here, refused, with the arguments that were made for them
set out in full rather than paraphrased into weakness.
