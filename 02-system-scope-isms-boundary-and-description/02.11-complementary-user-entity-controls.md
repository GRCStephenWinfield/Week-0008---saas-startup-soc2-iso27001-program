## 1. What a complementary user entity control is, and what it is not

**DC6** of DC section 200 requires the description of the system to disclose the **complementary user
entity controls** — the controls that management assumes, in designing the service, will be implemented by
user entities — **and the applicable trust services criteria that would not be met if those controls were
not suitably designed and operating effectively**.

That second limb is a requirement, not a nicety, and it is the limb most descriptions omit. A list of things
customers ought to do with no criterion named against any of them discharges half of DC6 and leaves the
reader unable to tell which part of the auditor's opinion is conditional on somebody the auditor never
tested. The table in section 2 therefore carries a **Criteria** column, and every entry in it asserts that
CloudNimbus's own controls do not reach the criterion named without the customer's control alongside them.

Three further things follow from the definition, and the third is the one this chapter exists for.

First, a CUEC is a **disclosure**, not a control CloudNimbus operates. Nothing on the list below is
performed by CloudNimbus, evidenced by CloudNimbus, or tested by the service auditor. The auditor tests
CloudNimbus's controls; the CUECs tell the reader what CloudNimbus is assuming about somebody else.

Second, a CUEC is not a contractual term. Some of these responsibilities appear in the master services
agreement and some do not. The disclosure is made because the criteria depend on the responsibility being
discharged, not because a contract allocates it.

Third — and this is the argument the chapter turns on — **a service organisation that inflates its CUEC
list is not being thorough.** It is tempting, and wrong, to describe inflation as transferring
CloudNimbus's obligations onto its customers. A disclosure that creates no contractual obligation cannot
transfer one; the customer who never reads it is under no more and no less obligation than before. What
inflation does is worse than a transfer, because a transfer at least lands somewhere.

**Inflation narrows the assurance the report provides while appearing to broaden the description.** Every
item added is a statement that a criterion is met only if a party the service auditor has not tested, and
in most cases has never heard from, performs something. The description grows; the set of criteria the
report can speak to on the strength of tested controls shrinks. The reader is left with an opinion carrying
a condition, and the condition rests on a party who may not know they are being relied upon, has agreed to
nothing, and will never be sampled. A long CUEC list therefore reads as rigour and functions as a reduction
in what has been examined — drafted by people who will never watch a customer fail to do the thing, and
landing in a restricted-use report read by a procurement analyst rather than by the tenant administrator
whose job it purports to describe.

CloudNimbus discloses eleven. It proposed fifteen.

## 2. The eleven complementary user entity controls

| ID | Complementary user entity control | Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| CUEC-01 | Provision and deprovision tenant administrator accounts promptly, including on termination | CC6.1, CC6.2, CC6.3 |
| CUEC-02 | Configure and periodically review role assignments within their tenant | CC6.1, CC6.3 |
| CUEC-03 | Enforce multi-factor authentication on their own identity provider where single sign-on is federated | CC6.1, CC6.6 |
| CUEC-04 | Review and approve pay rules and calculation configuration before activating them | PI1.2 |
| CUEC-05 | Validate the payroll export against their own records before submitting it to their payroll provider | PI1.4, PI1.5 |
| CUEC-06 | Notify CloudNimbus of employee terminations so that end-user access is revoked | CC6.2, CC6.3 |
| CUEC-07 | Provide their employees with a privacy notice covering the employer's own processing | P1.1, P2.1 |
| CUEC-08 | Respond to their employees' data subject requests, with CloudNimbus's assistance under O6 | P5.1, P5.2 |
| CUEC-09 | Maintain the accuracy of the employment data they supply | PI1.2 |
| CUEC-10 | Monitor and act on the alerts and reports CloudNimbus makes available | CC7.3, CC7.4 |
| CUEC-11 | Configure and test their own integration endpoints and credentials | CC6.1, A1.2 |

Each one satisfies a single qualifying question: **is there a criterion that cannot be met unless the
customer does this?** The third column is the answer written down. CUEC-06 is the clearest example.
CloudNimbus cannot know that an employee has left an employer. Nothing in the platform observes an
employment relationship ending; the employer observes it, and either tells the platform or does not.
Revocation of a departed end user's access is what **CC6.2** and **CC6.3** require, and the trigger for it
sits in the other organisation. The control straddles two entities, and the half CloudNimbus does not hold
has to be disclosed rather than assumed silently.

Most of the column is unsurprising and needs no defence: CUEC-01 to CUEC-03 and CUEC-06 are logical access,
which is the CC6 series; CUEC-04 and CUEC-09 concern the completeness and accuracy of the inputs a
calculation is performed on, which is **PI1.2**; CUEC-05 concerns the accuracy and the delivery of the
output, which is **PI1.4** and **PI1.5**; CUEC-08 concerns access to and correction of personal
information, which is **P5.1** and **P5.2**. Three rows are less obvious and are defended where they arise:
CUEC-07 in section 5.3, CUEC-10 in section 5.2, and CUEC-11 here.

**CUEC-11 carries two criteria for two different reasons.** **CC6.1** is the straightforward one: an
integration credential authenticates a principal that reaches customer data through the public REST API, at
scale, from outside the platform, and the customer creates, scopes, stores and rotates it. The second,
**A1.2**, is the one worth stating rather than assuming. The payroll export in DF-04 is delivered to an
endpoint the customer configures and CloudNimbus does not operate; the redelivery, retry and recovery
arrangements that A1.2 contemplates run against that endpoint, and none of them compensates for an endpoint
that was never correctly configured or never tested. Where the customer has not tested it, the availability
of the delivery path is not something CloudNimbus's controls can establish.

## 3. Fifteen proposed, four refused, eleven disclosed

**15 − 4 = 11.** The four that were refused, and the reason each was refused, are the more useful half of
the record, because each represents a control CloudNimbus would otherwise have quietly declined to operate.

| Proposed | Refused because |
|---|---|
| "Customers must monitor their tenant for unauthorised access" | Detection of unauthorised access to the platform is CloudNimbus's obligation under the CC7 series. Disclosing it as a CUEC would move a control CloudNimbus must operate onto a party that cannot see the telemetry |
| "Customers must verify that CloudNimbus has deleted their data" | Obligation O7 commits CloudNimbus to certify deletion. A commitment cannot be discharged by asking the counterparty to check it |
| "Customers must confirm that encryption is enabled on their tenant" | Encryption is not tenant-configurable. The control is CloudNimbus's and the CUEC would have described a setting the customer cannot reach |
| "Customers must ensure their employees do not share credentials" | Partly a genuine user-entity responsibility, but as drafted it shifted the substance of CC6.1. The residue is covered by CUEC-01 and CUEC-03 |

The four failures are of three distinct kinds, and naming them makes the test reusable.

The first proposal and the fourth failed because they **relocated a criterion**. CC7 concerns the detection
and response to security events; CC6.1 concerns logical access. Those criteria are addressed by
CloudNimbus's controls, and a CUEC that describes the same substance in the customer's voice does not
create a shared control — it creates a gap with a sentence over it.

The second failed because it **contradicted a commitment already made**. CloudNimbus has committed under
O7 and SC-03 to delete customer data within 30 days of termination and to certify completion. Asking the
customer to verify that would not be complementary; it would be an admission that the certificate is not
relied upon, issued alongside the certificate.

The third failed on **factual grounds**, and it is the most instructive of the four because it would have
survived a careless review. Encryption at rest and in transit is a platform property under SR-03, uniform
across every tenant, with no configuration surface a customer can reach. The proposed CUEC described an
action that does not exist. A reader who saw it would have looked for the setting, failed to find it, and
formed a reasonable view about how carefully the rest of the description had been written.

This is recorded as **DEC-208** (2026-03-13) and argued as **ADR-0010**.

## 4. Why the inflation instinct has to be resisted deliberately

Nothing in the drafting process pushes towards a shorter CUEC list, and everything pushes towards a longer
one. A CUEC costs CloudNimbus nothing to write. It reduces the apparent reach of CloudNimbus's own control
obligations without anyone having to argue that the reach should be reduced. It can be justified in every
individual case with the sentence "well, the customer *should* be doing that." And it never generates an
argument in the room, because the party it names is not in the room when it is drafted and will encounter
it, if at all, in a document their security team reads once.

The cost has to be named precisely rather than as a moral point about fairness. Adding an item does not
shift a duty onto the customer, who has undertaken nothing by being described. It **subtracts a criterion
from the set the report can speak to on the strength of tested controls.** A description with thirty CUECs
looks more complete than one with eleven and tells the reader less, because more of what it describes sits
outside what was examined.

The counterweight is a question that has to be asked out loud about every entry: **if the customer does not
do this, what happens?** There are only two possible answers, and they lead in opposite directions. If the
answer is "a criterion is not met", the item is a genuine CUEC and belongs on the list, with that criterion
named in the third column, because the reader needs to know that CloudNimbus's controls alone do not get
there. If the answer is "CloudNimbus's own control catches it", the item is not a CUEC at all — it is a
suggestion, and disclosing a suggestion as a CUEC misrepresents where the boundary of the system sits.

The four refusals in section 3 all returned the second answer, or returned no coherent answer at all. So,
as section 5.2 records, did one of the eleven in an earlier draft.

## 5. Three entries that needed more than a sentence

### 5.1 CUEC-04 and CUEC-05 — where necessity sits, and where consequence sits

**CUEC-05 — validate the payroll export against your own records before submitting it to your payroll
provider.**

An earlier draft of this chapter called CUEC-05 the most important control on the list. That was the wrong
claim to make in a chapter whose stated gate is necessity, and it is withdrawn. Importance is not the test;
if it were, the list would be a ranking of things CloudNimbus wishes customers did. **CUEC-05 is the most
consequential entry when it is not performed**, which is a different statement and a true one, and the two
have to be kept apart or the gate in section 4 means nothing.

Keeping them apart requires saying where the necessity actually lives, and for the defect people have in
mind when they reach for CUEC-05 — a calculation faithfully applying a rule the employer configured wrongly
— it lives in **CUEC-04**, not CUEC-05. **PI1.2** concerns the completeness, accuracy and authorisation of
the inputs to processing, and a pay rule is an input. The employer approving the rule before it is
activated is the user-entity control without which PI1.2 cannot be met for a configuration CloudNimbus is
not entitled to second-guess. CUEC-05 does not prevent that defect. It notices it late, after the engine
has already applied the rule correctly.

CUEC-05 is nonetheless on the list on its own necessity, and on a different limb. **PI1.4** concerns output
that is complete, accurate and **distributed** to meet the entity's objectives, and **PI1.5** concerns
output stored completely and accurately. The distribution chain does not end when CloudNimbus delivers the
export file. It ends when the customer submits that file to their payroll provider, and the submission step
belongs to the customer: CloudNimbus does not perform it, does not observe it, and cannot establish that
what reached the payroll provider is the file that left the platform, covering the period intended, in
full, unamended, and not a re-submission of a prior period. That is the part of PI1.4 no CloudNimbus
control reaches, and it is why the entry survives the gate.

The chain is worth stating once end to end, because it is what gives the consequence its size. It runs from
a clock-in on a phone, through the calculation engine, into an export file (DF-04), into the customer's
payroll provider, and out as a payment to a named individual's bank account. A payroll provider does not
sanity-check an amount; it pays it. CUEC-05 is the last point in that chain where a person who knows what
the numbers ought to look like sees them before they become somebody's pay.

It is also, candidly, the control least likely to be performed properly, and the description has to say so
rather than assume compliance and hope. Three forces work against it. The export lands against a **payroll
cut-off**, which is a deadline with no slack; a customer that finds a discrepancy at that moment faces a
choice between a late payroll and an approximate one. The **volume** makes line-by-line validation
impossible for an employer with 15,000 employees, so validation in practice means exception review — period
totals, variance against prior period, outliers — which catches gross errors and does not catch a
systematically wrong rounding boundary. And a platform that has produced correct numbers for eleven
consecutive periods **trains its user** to approve the twelfth.

#### What happens when a customer does not perform it

The description states the consequence plainly.

CloudNimbus's own processing-integrity controls are the primary defence and are **not conditional on
CUEC-05**. **SR-06** requires that calculation runs be reproducible from their inputs and rule version with
a reconciliation record retained, and the completeness, validity, accuracy, timeliness and authorisation of
the calculation itself are addressed by CloudNimbus's controls. Those controls exist whether or not any
customer ever opens an export file, and Phase 06 designs them. **CUEC-05 is not the control that makes the
calculation right**, and no part of this chapter should be read as suggesting otherwise.

What CUEC-05 provides is the **detective control at the point of application**. It catches a specific class
of defect that CloudNimbus's controls structurally cannot: cases where the calculation is faithful to the
configured rules and **the configured rules are wrong**. If an employer has configured an overtime
threshold incorrectly, or activated a shift differential against the wrong pay code, the engine will apply
that configuration exactly and reconcile against it perfectly. Every CloudNimbus control will pass. Only
somebody who knows what the employer intended can detect it, and that somebody works for the employer.

The allocation between the two entries is therefore the one section 5.1 states and is worth repeating in
the language of the gate. **CUEC-04 is the necessary control** — the employer's approval of the rule before
activation is what PI1.2 requires of an input CloudNimbus does not author. **CUEC-05 is the detective
control disclosed because it is the last check before an output becomes somebody's pay**, and because the
submission step it precedes is the customer's own, which is where its PI1.4 and PI1.5 necessity sits. A
programme that put the whole weight of the wrong-rule problem on CUEC-05 would be relying on a late check
to cure a defect an earlier control was designed to prevent, which is a poor design wherever it appears and
a worse one when the earlier control belongs to somebody else too.

Where the customer does not perform CUEC-05, that class of defect reaches individuals as pay, and it
persists — a configuration error does not announce itself, it recurs every period until somebody notices,
and the person who eventually notices is often an employee reading a payslip. The employer is the party
with the employment relationship and the obligation to the employee; CloudNimbus is the party that
calculated the number from the employer's rules.

This document is not going to convert that into a statement about where legal responsibility lies, which is
a matter for the contract and for counsel. What it states is the operational fact: **the disclosure of
CUEC-05 does not make CloudNimbus's controls any weaker or any stronger, and it does not make the outcome
acceptable.** It tells the reader that a defect of this class is outside the reach of the controls the
report covers.

Two things follow for the programme. CloudNimbus makes performance of CUEC-05 as easy as it can — variance
reporting against prior period, exception surfacing, and a hold on submission until the export is
acknowledged — because a control the customer cannot practically perform is a control that will not be
performed. And CUEC-05 is the first item in the annual CUEC reconfirmation described below, because it is
the one most worth a conversation.

### 5.2 CUEC-10 — making the distinction from the first refusal a real one

The first proposal refused in section 3 was "customers must monitor their tenant for unauthorised access."
CUEC-10 as originally drafted read "monitor and act on the alerts and reports CloudNimbus makes available",
and a reader who set the two side by side would have been entitled to ask what the difference was other
than the wording. Refusing a proposal and then disclosing its substance under a politer verb is not a
distinction; it is the same relocation of a criterion, performed twice.

The distinction is made real by narrowing what CUEC-10 is about. **CUEC-10 concerns tenant-level events
that only the customer can adjudicate.** Detection remains CloudNimbus's: the CC7 series requires
CloudNimbus to monitor the platform, identify anomalies and surface them, and nothing in CUEC-10 moves any
part of that. What CloudNimbus's detection controls surface and cannot judge is whether an event was
*authorised inside the customer's organisation* — whether a particular administrator action was one that
person was meant to take, whether a role a named individual still holds is one they should still hold,
whether a bulk export at an unusual hour was a legitimate month-end task. CloudNimbus sees the action, the
actor, the time and the scope. It cannot see the employer's intent, its internal authority matrix, or the
fact that the person taking the action resigned on Friday.

That is why the criteria named against CUEC-10 are **CC7.3** and **CC7.4**. CC7.3 requires security events
to be evaluated to determine whether they represent an incident, and for this class the evaluation cannot
be completed by CloudNimbus alone: an administrator action indistinguishable from a legitimate one on every
signal available to the platform is adjudicated only by the party who knows whether it was authorised.
CC7.4 is the response limb, which for tenant-internal misuse runs inside the customer's organisation.

The refused proposal failed because it asked the customer to *detect* unauthorised access to the platform
using telemetry they cannot see. CUEC-10 asks the customer to *adjudicate* events CloudNimbus has already
detected and delivered to them, using knowledge only they hold. The first would have replaced a CloudNimbus
control with a customer one. The second completes an evaluation CloudNimbus's control cannot finish.

### 5.3 CUEC-07 — the entry closest to the line

**CUEC-07 — provide their employees with a privacy notice covering the employer's own processing.**

The obvious objection is a good one and is met head on. **P1.1** concerns notice about *CloudNimbus's*
privacy practices — the commitments and system requirements relating to the collection, use, retention,
disclosure and disposal of personal information, communicated to data subjects. On its face that is a
criterion about CloudNimbus, and a criterion about CloudNimbus cannot be met by an employer writing a
document about themselves.

The basis for the entry is narrower than that objection assumes, and it rests on a fact about the reader
rather than about the drafting. **The individual receives one notice experience, not two.** An employee
clocking in on a CloudNimbus screen inside their employer's onboarding process does not encounter a
CloudNimbus notice and an employer notice as separable artefacts. CloudNimbus's notice can describe
CloudNimbus's practices accurately and still fail to be effective as to the processing the individual is
actually subject to, because the part that determines *why* their location is being captured, *who* inside
their employer sees it and *what* the employer does with it afterwards is the employer's part, and
CloudNimbus does not know it. Where the employer supplies nothing, CloudNimbus's notice is accurate and
incomplete about the combined processing, and an incomplete notice is a weak discharge of P1.1 however
carefully the CloudNimbus half is drafted. **P2.1**, the communication of choices about collection, has the
same shape: for the 387 customers who have enabled geolocation the decision to collect was the employer's,
so the communication of it has to be the employer's too.

This is nevertheless the entry on the list closest to the line, and the chapter says so rather than
pretending the argument is as clean as CUEC-06's. The contrary case is real and is set out here so that a
reader can weigh it: P1.1 is CloudNimbus's criterion, CloudNimbus discharges it by publishing its own
notice, and what the employer tells its employees is a matter between employer and employee. The case that
was preferred is that a notice criterion evaluated from the position of the person receiving the notice —
which is the position P1.1 is written from — does not divide as neatly as the contract does. A reader who
finds that unpersuasive has been given enough to disagree with it, which is more useful than a one-line
assertion they cannot test, and Phase 07 revisits the notice architecture with the whole privacy design in
front of it.

## 6. Communicating the eleven

A CUEC disclosed only inside a restricted-use report has been disclosed to the wrong person. The report
reaches management of user entities, and CUEC-01, CUEC-05 and CUEC-06 are performed by a tenant
administrator, a payroll analyst and an HR coordinator respectively, none of whom will read Section III of
a SOC 2 report.

Accountability for CUEC communication sits with Ana-Sofia Cruz as Head of Customer Success, and the
mechanism is four-part: the eleven are set out in the onboarding pack and walked through at implementation;
they appear in the administrator documentation at the point in the product where each is performed;
they are reconfirmed annually with each customer's named administrative contact; and they are restated in
the description itself under DC6. The first three exist because the fourth does not reach anybody who acts.

## 7. CUECs and CSOCs are not the same instrument

Both are complementary controls, both are disclosures, and they are routinely conflated. **CUECs (DC6) are
assumed to be performed by user entities**, of which there are 640 with 640 different levels of maturity,
and each performs them for itself. **CSOCs (DC7) are assumed to be performed by subservice organisations**
— fourteen of them at 02.10, across AWS and Halcyon Identity — and each is performed once, by a large
supplier, for everybody.

The practical difference is where the risk of non-performance sits. If AWS does not perform CSOC-01, the
description is wrong for every customer at once and CloudNimbus will hear about it. If a customer does not
perform CUEC-05, the description is not wrong at all — it disclosed exactly this possibility — and nobody
hears about it until a payslip is queried. That asymmetry is the reason this chapter argues for a short,
honest CUEC list rather than a comprehensive one.
