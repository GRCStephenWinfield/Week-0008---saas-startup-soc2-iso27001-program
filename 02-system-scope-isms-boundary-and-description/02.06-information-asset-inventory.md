## 1. Purpose

An asset inventory is the least glamorous document in a certification programme and the one that most
reliably determines whether the rest of it is honest. Phase 03 will assess risk, and a risk assessment can
only reach the assets somebody wrote down. Phase 04 will assign controls, and a control cannot be applied
to a population nobody has counted. The examination will test controls over stores, accounts and endpoints,
and the service auditor will sample from a population management asserts is complete. Every one of those
activities inherits whatever this document gets wrong.

Annex A of ISO/IEC 27001:2022 includes **A.5.9, inventory of information and other associated assets**, and
clause 6.1.3 c) requires comparison against Annex A to verify that no necessary control has been omitted.
Whether A.5.9 and the classification and labelling controls beside it are determined necessary, and how
they are justified, is a **Statement of Applicability** matter and belongs to Phase 03; Phase 02 does not
pre-empt that determination. What Phase 02 does is build the inventory the determination will be made
about, and baseline it — milestone **MS-03**, met on 2026-03-20.

This inventory carries **two columns, not one**. Every asset class is counted twice: once for the ISMS,
whose boundary is the whole organisation, and once for the SOC 2 system, whose boundary is the platform.
That is the two-boundary model from 02.01 expressed as arithmetic, and it is the reason a reader can check
the argument rather than take it on trust.

Three of the rows repay a second look before the totals are discussed.

**AC-03 and AC-06 are equal in both columns.** All 63 microservices and all 41 customer-data Kafka topics
are inside the SOC 2 system, because CloudNimbus operates exactly one product and there is no second
service for a microservice to belong to. A company with a second product line would see these two rows
diverge, and the fact that they do not is a description of the business rather than an accounting
convenience.

**AC-08 collapses hardest**, from 96 internal SaaS applications to 22. The company runs a large SaaS estate
for a 187-person business — a CRM, a finance suite, a marketing stack, recruiting tooling, e-signature,
expense management and the rest — and almost none of it touches the platform. The 22 that do are the tools
the service is actually operated through: source hosting and CI, the observability platform, the alerting
and on-call tooling, the ticketing system through which changes and incidents are recorded, the identity
provider, the MDM platform, and the security tooling whose findings feed monitoring. Those 22 are inside
the system because a failure or compromise of any of them reaches a system requirement.

**AC-09 and AC-10 stay high** — 119 of 148 repositories and 71 of 87 pipelines — even though the
non-production AWS accounts they deploy into are outside the system. That is the deliberate consequence of
ADR-0007 explained in 02.03 §9: excluding the environments does not exclude the mechanism that promotes
code through them into production, because SR-11 depends on that mechanism.

## 3. The 246 — what it measures

**1,046 − 800 = 246.** Two hundred and forty-six assets sit **inside the ISMS and outside the SOC 2 system**.

That number is the measure of how far the management system reaches beyond the platform. It is the finance
suite and the CRM, the marketing stack and the recruiting tooling; it is the endpoints of the 23 people in
02.05 who sell the service rather than operate it; it is the 52 corporate procedures; it is the
non-production accounts, the fourth EKS cluster, `staging-core`, and the 46 S3 buckets that serve
non-production and corporate purposes. None of it appears in the system description, none of it serves a
trust services criterion, and every piece of it has an owner, a classification and a place in the ISMS.

The 246 decomposes cleanly across the twelve classes, and the decomposition is worth stating because it is
checkable: 2 AWS accounts, 1 EKS cluster, 1 Aurora cluster, 46 S3 buckets, 23 managed endpoints, 74 internal
SaaS applications, 29 code repositories, 16 CI/CD pipelines, 2 non-Aurora data stores and 52 documented
procedures. Microservices and Kafka topics contribute nothing, for the reason given in §2.

**This is the reason the certificate answers a question the examination report does not.** A prospective
customer who asks "is the product secure?" is asking about the 800. A prospective customer who asks "is the
company secure?" — which is what most enterprise security questionnaires are actually asking, whatever
their wording — is asking about the 1,046. The examination report will address the first question, over a
defined period, against defined criteria, in a restricted-use document. The certificate addresses the
second, by attesting conformity of a management system whose boundary is the whole organisation. Neither
answers the other's question, and the 246 is the size of the gap between them.

## 4. The other direction, and why it has no number

The reverse statement is equally true and cannot be quantified.

**Components operated by AWS and by Halcyon Identity are inside the SOC 2 system and outside the ISMS.**
They are inside the system because DC3 defines the system by what delivers the service, and the service
demonstrably runs on AWS infrastructure and authenticates through Halcyon's platform. They are outside the
ISMS because CloudNimbus does not operate them: it cannot assign an owner to a rack, cannot subject a
Halcyon control to its own clause 9.2 internal audit, and cannot execute a risk treatment decision about a
hypervisor it has no access to.

**They are not counted in the 1,046, and this document will not give a number for them.** CloudNimbus does
not own, control or inventory a supplier's components. It does not know how many hosts a managed database
service runs on, how many facilities are involved in a region, or how many services sit behind a CIAM API,
and an asset register that recorded such figures would be transcribing a marketing page into a control
document. **An asset register that counted a supplier's racks would be a fiction**, and a fiction in an
inventory is worse than an omission, because an omission is visible and a fiction is not.

What CloudNimbus can and does inventory is its own side of the interface: the accounts, the configurations,
the keys, the network definitions, the integration and the contract. Those are all in the 1,046 already.
**The carve-out method exists precisely for this.** Carving out a subservice organisation means the
description states the services the subservice organisation performs and the complementary subservice
organisation controls assumed to be in place there, while excluding the subservice organisation's own
controls from the description and from the scope of the service auditor's testing. It is the mechanism by
which a description can be complete about a system that includes components the service organisation
neither owns nor can count. 02.10 develops this, including the point that AWS's own assurance reports cover
AWS's controls and say nothing whatever about whether CloudNimbus configured a bucket policy correctly.

## 5. Classification

Every asset carries a classification, applied to the information the asset holds or processes rather than
to the asset's technical type.

| Classification | Definition | Typical assets |
|---|---|---|
| Restricted | Customer personal data, calculation output that becomes pay, authentication secrets and keys | The core and analytics Aurora clusters, receipt image buckets, export files, the log archive |
| Confidential | Non-personal customer information, commercial terms, security documentation, internal design material | Tenant configuration, pay rules, the CRM, architecture documentation |
| Internal | Information intended for staff generally with no external sensitivity | Most procedures, internal wikis, non-sensitive telemetry |
| Public | Information released or intended for release | Marketing content, published API documentation, the trust centre |

Two rules keep the scheme usable. **A container inherits the highest classification of what it holds** — a
bucket holding one restricted object is a restricted asset — which prevents the common failure of a
"general" store quietly accumulating personal data under an Internal label. And **classification is not the
same as boundary**: an asset can be Restricted and outside the SOC 2 system, as the corporate finance
suite's customer billing records are, or Internal and inside it, as most in-scope procedures are. Confusing
the two produces a register in which sensitivity is used as a proxy for scope, which is how corporate
systems end up wrongly described as part of a service.

Bhargava** owns AC-12 and the register as a record. **Karim Haddad** approves it.

An asset class owner is accountable for the population; individual assets carry an owning team. The
distinction matters at review time, because "who owns the S3 buckets" and "who owns *this* S3 bucket" are
different questions, and a register that answers only the first cannot support a remediation instruction.

## 7. How the inventory is maintained, and what that proves

The inventory is maintained by **automated discovery reconciled against owner attestation**, quarterly, and
on any change that crosses a threshold defined in the change procedure.

Discovery enumerates what exists: cloud resources from the AWS Organization and the configuration recorder,
clusters and workloads from the EKS control planes, repositories and pipelines from the source and CI
platform, endpoints from MDM, SaaS applications from the identity provider's application catalogue and the
expense feed. Attestation asks each owner to confirm the population, correct it, and state what should be
there but is not.

The honest statement about what this proves is that **the two methods fail differently, which is why both
are used.** Discovery finds what is running and cannot tell you that something *should* exist, that a bucket
is unused, or that an application procured on a corporate card outside the identity provider exists at all.
Attestation catches those and is subject to every weakness of self-assessment: an owner confirms what they
remember. Reconciling the two produces a register whose errors are at least of a known shape — the
disagreements between the two methods are the interesting rows, and they are where review effort goes.

Two further reconciliations run against this register. The data flow register in 02.04 is reconciled against
it quarterly: a store in the inventory that appears in no flow is either unused or undocumented, and both
answers are worth having. And the personal information inventory in 02.07 is reconciled against the stores
recorded here, so that a category of personal data cannot be described as retained for a period in a store
nobody has listed.

One note for readers who like to derive one figure from another, because here the derivation works and it
is worth showing. **AC-07 records 208 managed endpoints inside the system: the 164 laptops issued to the
164 personnel in the people component, plus the 44 test and lab devices used to build and validate the
four client applications. 164 + 44 = 208.** The 23 endpoints outside the system are the laptops of the 23
quota-carrying sales and marketing personnel in 02.05 who satisfy neither limb of the rule at 02.05 §3.
**208 + 23 = 231.**

The two halves of the 208 qualify under **different limbs of the endpoint rule at 02.01 §5**, and saying
which prevents an obvious objection. The 164 laptops qualify under the first limb: the person operating the
device can reach production or customer data, or operates a procedure the system depends on from it. **The
44 test and lab devices qualify under the second limb** — used to build, test or validate software that
forms part of the system — and they qualify under that limb alone, because they are non-production
equipment holding no customer data and reaching no production environment. Without the second limb these 44
would be outside the system while the software they validate is inside it, which is not a boundary anybody
could defend: the iOS and Android binaries that reach 1.24 million people are validated on this estate, and
a device capable of altering a build or signing an artefact is inside the system by virtue of what it can
do to the software, not by virtue of what data it holds.

That the endpoint figure decomposes into the people figure is deliberate. An asset register whose totals
cannot be reconciled against the population they are drawn from is a register nobody will trust the second
time they check it, and the second check is the one an auditor performs.

## 8. What this inventory is not

It is not a configuration management database, and it does not attempt to record every attribute of every
resource. It is a population register with an owner, a classification and two boundary flags per asset
class, designed to be complete at the level the two frameworks need rather than exhaustive at the level an
operations team needs.

It is not a risk assessment. No asset here carries a rating, a likelihood or an impact. Those are Phase 03's
to determine from this baseline, and a phase that scored its own inventory while building it would have
prejudged the assessment it exists to feed.

And it is not static. Every figure in §2 is a baseline as at 2026-03-20. Movement is expected, is recorded
in the scope change log in `logs/`, and material change to the ISMS or its scope carries an external
consequence: obligation **O10** requires CloudNimbus to notify Northgate Certification Services of
significant changes to the ISMS, its scope or the organisation within 30 days.
