## 1. What the P6 series asks, and which half of it this chapter answers

The disclosure criteria are the largest cluster in the Privacy category — **P6.1 to P6.7, seven of the
eighteen** — and the first thing to do with them is to set them out in the terms TSP section 100 uses rather
than in the terms a control library would find convenient.

| Criterion | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **P6.1** | The entity **discloses personal information to third parties with the explicit consent of the data subject**, and such consent is **obtained prior to disclosure**, to meet the entity's objectives related to privacy |
| **P6.2** | A complete, accurate and timely record of **authorised** disclosures is created and retained |
| **P6.3** | A complete, accurate and timely record of **detected or reported unauthorised** disclosures, **including breaches**, is created and retained |
| **P6.4** | Privacy commitments are obtained from vendors and other third parties with access to personal information, **and their compliance is assessed periodically** |
| **P6.5** | Commitments are obtained from those parties **to notify the entity of actual or suspected unauthorised disclosures**, and such notifications are reported to appropriate personnel and acted on under the incident-response procedures |
| **P6.6** | **Notification of breaches and incidents is provided to affected data subjects, regulators and others** |
| **P6.7** | Data subjects are provided with an accounting of the personal information held and the disclosures made **upon the data subjects' request** |

Three of the seven are not what a first reading suggests. **P6.3 is not a requirement to prevent
unauthorised disclosure; it is a requirement to keep a record of the unauthorised disclosures that are
detected or reported.** **P6.5 is not the entity's own assessment of its third parties** — that is P6.4's
second limb — **it is about a commitment the third party gives to tell the entity when something has gone
wrong, and about what the entity does with the call when it comes.** And **P6.6 is the notification
criterion**, which is the one this chapter came closest to leaving unanswered; §6 answers it.

**P6.7** is answered in [07.06](07.06-access-correction-and-data-subject-requests.md) alongside the data
subject request path, because it is a question about what an individual is told rather than about what a
third party receives.

This chapter therefore carries P6.1 to P6.6, two controls, and one event that tested both. The two controls
are **`CNB-C-131`**, which requires a data processing addendum to be recorded against a sub-processor before
it can be enabled in production configuration, reviews the sub-processor register quarterly, and commits to
thirty days' notice before a new sub-processor is engaged; and **`CNB-C-132`**, which requires a disclosure
outside the standard processing path to be entered in the disclosure register with requester, scope, stated
basis and date, and requires a suspected unauthorised disclosure to open a privacy incident running the
48-hour customer notification path.

The event is the replacement of the outbound email delivery provider, notified on **2026-10-02** and
effective **2026-11-05**, which is the first sub-processor change the programme has made since the register
was fixed at eleven in Phase 02. It is a small change with a large amount of machinery behind it, and §4
sets it out.

## 2. The disclosure register — six entries and no view on any of them

**Zero unauthorised disclosures were identified in the period.** The register carries **six** entries between
2026-10-01 and 2026-11-27.

| Class | Entries | What the register records |
|---|---|---|
| Customer-directed exports to a named third party at the tenant's instruction | **4** | Requester (the tenant's named administrator), scope (the categories and the period exported), stated basis (the tenant's written instruction) and date |
| Responses to requests made by a customer's own auditors | **2** | Requester (the customer, on its auditor's behalf), scope, stated basis (the customer's instruction under its own examination) and date |
| **Total** | **6** | |

**The register records four fields and reaches no conclusion, and that restraint is the design rather than
an omission.** `CNB-C-132` asks for the requester, the scope, the stated basis and the date. It does not ask
whether the stated basis was a good one, and this chapter does not supply that assessment either. The basis
recorded is **the basis the requester stated**, in the requester's words, and the employer is the party that
determines the purposes of its own processing. Whether a particular instruction was one the employer was
entitled to give is a question for the employer and its own advisers, and a register that answered it would
be a register asserting a conclusion it has no standing to reach and no evidence to reach it on.

What the register does that matters is narrower and testable: it makes a disclosure **countable**. Six is a
number a sampler can select from, each entry has a date, and each has a named requester who can be asked.
The commonest defect here is not a wrongful disclosure — it is a disclosure nobody wrote down, which is
indistinguishable from no disclosure at all until somebody asks the receiving party.

**`CNB-C-132`'s second limb has a population of zero, and the record says so rather than letting the first
limb speak for both.** The control requires a suspected unauthorised disclosure to open a privacy incident
running the 48-hour customer notification path under SC-02. No such suspicion arose in the period, so that
limb had no occasion to operate. This is the same shape 06.12 §3 recorded for `CNB-C-067`'s automatic
escalation limb and 06.06 recorded for SC-02 itself: **a detective limb that has never had an exception to
find has not been shown to find one.** Six clean entries in the register are evidence about the first limb
and evidence about nothing else.

One clarification belongs here so that a reader tracing the RT-02 matter does not go looking for it in the
wrong place. **None of the six entries relates to the RT-02 over-retention.** Nothing left `eu-central-1`,
no access outside the normal processing path occurred, and
[07.03 §5](07.03-the-rt-02-retention-failure.md) sets out in full why the disclosure register holds no entry
for it and why SC-02's clock was not engaged. An over-retention is not a disclosure, and filing it as one
would put a retention failure where a reader looks for a confidentiality failure.

## 3. The two controls and the boundary between them

`CNB-C-131` is **Preventive** with a **Quarterly** cadence; `CNB-C-132` is **Corrective** and
**Continuous**. Phase 04 published the split as `CNB-C-131` carrying **P6.1, P6.4 and P6.5** and
`CNB-C-132` carrying **P6.2, P6.3 and P6.6**, with `CNB-C-133` citing P6.1 to P6.6 together. Read against
the criteria as §1 states them rather than as the library summarises them, that split holds in two places,
strains in two, and is answered by a different control in one — and the chapter states which rather than
inheriting the mapping in silence.

| Criterion | Phase 04's citation | Read against the criterion's own words |
|---|---|---|
| **P6.1** | `CNB-C-131` | **Strains hardest of the six, and harder than a mapping inherited in silence would admit.** Read in TSP section 100's own words the criterion is about **the data subject's explicit consent, obtained before the disclosure**. `CNB-C-131` governs whether a sub-processor may be enabled, and the instrument it turns on is a **data processing addendum between CloudNimbus and the employer** — an agreement no data subject is party to and no data subject consented to. What records a purpose at all is `CNB-C-132`'s **stated basis** field, and §2 says plainly that no control tests the stated basis against the notice, still less against a consent. **No control in the library evidences a data subject's explicit consent obtained prior to disclosure**, and the chapter says so rather than letting the citation stand for it |
| **P6.2** | `CNB-C-132` | **Holds, exactly.** The disclosure register is a record of **authorised** disclosures with requester, scope, stated basis and date. Population **6** |
| **P6.3** | `CNB-C-132` | **Holds.** The privacy-incident limb is the record of a **detected or reported unauthorised** disclosure. Population **zero** |
| **P6.4** | `CNB-C-131` | **Holds on both limbs, across two rows.** The addendum is the commitment; the periodic assessment is `CNB-C-131`'s quarterly register review together with `CNB-C-092`'s Tier 1 assurance refresh |
| **P6.5** | `CNB-C-131` | **Strains.** The commitment to notify sits in the addendum `CNB-C-131` and `CNB-C-091` require; the second limb — the notification reported to appropriate personnel and acted on under the incident-response procedures — is `CNB-C-132`'s. **Neither row carries the criterion whole** |
| **P6.6** | `CNB-C-132` | **Answered only in part, and §6 is about which part** |

**Phase 07 amends nothing published in an earlier phase**, so the citations stand as the library published
them and this reading is carried on the face of the chapter to the next library issue, alongside the other
candidates **Karim Haddad** holds. What the reading changes now is what the chapter claims. **P6.5** is
answered by two rows between them rather than by the one that cites it, and a criterion answered by two rows
is a criterion no single test can reach. **P6.1 is answered in full by neither** — a sub-processor addendum
is a commitment between CloudNimbus and its customer, and the criterion asks about the data subject's own
explicit consent obtained before disclosure. That is the same architectural fact §6 records against P6.6:
**the person the criterion is about is a person this system reaches only through an employer.**

**A sub-processor cannot be enabled in production configuration until the addendum exists.** That is the
enforcement point, and it is worth naming precisely because the alternative wording — "we require a data
processing addendum with every sub-processor" — describes a policy rather than a control. `CNB-C-091` holds
the same line from the CC9 side, requiring each of the eleven to hold an addendum setting security
requirements, breach notification timing, sub-processor disclosure and audit rights, with no processing
beginning before it is executed. The two rows are not duplicates: `CNB-C-091` is about the population of
eleven and its instruments, and `CNB-C-131` is about the configuration switch that would otherwise let a
twelfth begin quietly.

`CNB-C-133`, the quarterly Data Protection Officer review, sits above both and cites **P6.1 to P6.6
together**. It read the disclosure register for this period at its **2026-10-28** occurrence, and
[07.08](07.08-monitoring-privacy-and-the-quarterly-review.md) carries that review.

## 4. The sub-processor change — thirty-four days against a thirty-day commitment

CloudNimbus replaced its outbound email delivery provider. The outgoing provider is the one that appears
again in [07.09 §3](07.09-the-vendor-register-tiering-and-assurance.md) as the single Tier 1 vendor holding
no current assurance artefact, and the two facts are the same fact seen from two registers.

| Step | Date | Against what |
|---|---|---|
| Notice issued to all 640 customers | **2026-10-02** | **O5** and **SC-08** require thirty days' notice of a new sub-processor with a right to object |
| Objections received | 2 of 640 | — |
| Second objection withdrawn after the objector's own security review | **2026-10-22** | — |
| First objection resolved by suppressing platform-generated email for that tenant | **2026-10-30** | **DEC-709**, Ana-Sofia Cruz |
| Change effective — new provider engaged, outgoing provider removed | **2026-11-05** | **Thirty-four days after notice** |

**Thirty-four days against a thirty-day commitment is the commitment met with four days to spare, and the
four days were the point.** The notice period is not a waiting period for its own sake; it is the window in
which a customer can object and the parties can do something about it. A notice issued at exactly thirty
days leaves no room between the last day an objection can arrive and the day the change takes effect, which
means an objection arriving on day twenty-nine is answered after the thing it objects to has happened.
**DEC-701** records the choice to issue at thirty-four days for that reason. It cost a diary entry.

**The register stood at eleven sub-processors throughout.** One added and one removed on the same day, and
the population Phase 02 published — 84 vendors, 11 sub-processors under a data processing addendum, both
subservice organisations among them, 73 ordinary vendors, 11 + 73 = 84 — is unchanged at this vantage.
07.09 §2 owns that arithmetic and this chapter does not re-derive it.

### 4.1 A right to object is not a right to veto

Two of 640 customers objected. Neither objection stopped the change, and the chapter has to be exact about
why that is not a failure of the commitment.

**SC-08 commits to thirty days' notice with a right to object. It does not commit to abandoning a change
that draws one.** What the master services agreement says happens where an objection is not resolved is that
**the customer may terminate for convenience**. That is the whole of the stated consequence, and no amount
appears anywhere in this programme.

**A right to object with no stated consequence is a courtesy**; one that terminates in a stated exit
prices the objection, and neither party has to guess at the other's next move. The
[notice template](templates/sub-processor-notice-template.md) carries that argument and the drafting rule
that follows from it.

In this instance neither objection reached that point. **One customer withdrew on 2026-10-22 after
conducting its own security review of the incoming provider** — the notice did what a notice period is for.
**The other was resolved on 2026-10-30 by suppressing platform-generated email for that
tenant altogether**, which is an accommodation and a worse product for that customer: their
users no longer receive the notifications the platform generates, and their administrators have said that
is the trade they prefer. **DEC-709** records it in those terms rather than as a resolution, because a
customer who has accepted a degraded service has been accommodated rather than persuaded — and the
distinction matters to whoever reads this register next year and finds one tenant configured differently
from the other 639.

### 4.2 What the change did not do

The incoming provider entered the register through `CNB-C-090` — no vendor receives production data or a
system integration until a security assessment proportionate to its tier is complete and the outcome **and
the tier** are recorded — and through `CNB-C-093`, the cloud service baseline review that A.5.23 sits
behind. **It did not enter through the sub-processor change notice**, and a reader should not infer that
the notice performed the assessment. The notice is a communication to customers under SC-08. The assessment
is a control, it happened first, and the two are recorded in different registers by different people.

## 5. `IS-34` — fourteen days of a list that named the wrong provider, and no control that failed

The change took effect on **2026-11-05**. **The published sub-processor list still named the outgoing email
delivery provider on 2026-11-19**, when the annual notice reconciliation under `CNB-C-120` was performed.
Fourteen days. Corrected **2026-11-20**.

**This was drafted as a deviation against `CNB-C-120` and it is not one.** `CNB-C-120`'s occurrence was
performed on time, against the three sources its statement names, and it is the control that **found** the
divergence. [07.10 §4](07.10-reading-the-other-sides-complementary-controls.md) states the doctrine this
phase holds itself to — a finding with no failing control behind it is a referred issue and not a deviation,
because attaching a failure to a control that never made the promise makes the whole log unreadable in the
other direction. The doctrine was applied to `CA-07-01` and was not applied here at first, and the
inconsistency is corrected in this section.

> **`IS-34` — No control required the published sub-processor list to be updated at the change.** The
> list named the outgoing provider for fourteen days after the change took effect on 2026-11-05, and the
> currency of the published list was the subject of an annual reconciliation and of nothing else. **O5**
> requires the list to be current, and no control in the library carried that requirement to the event that
> makes it stale. Referred; owner **Tobias Lund**; clause 10.2 corrective action **`CA-07-04`** attached.

Three things about it are worth stating rather than leaving to the log.

**`CNB-C-131` did not fail either, and the alternative attribution was considered and refused.**
`CNB-C-131`'s commitments were met: the addendum existed, the register was reviewed, the notice ran
thirty-four days. Attaching a deviation to the sub-processor control because the subject matter is
sub-processors would have put it on a second control that did what it said.

**It was found by an annual control.** A list whose currency is tested once a year can be wrong for up to a
year. Fourteen days is the interval between the change and
the next scheduled occurrence of a control whose cadence is Annual, and the only reason it is fourteen days
rather than months is that the reconciliation was brought forward into November — **DEC-714**, and
[GOV-28 §3](governance/GOV-28-annual-notice-reconciliation.md) records who brought it forward and why.

**And a corrective action is attached to it even though no deviation is.** `CA-07-04` does not ask for a
better reconciliation. It asks for the currency check to be **moved to the change itself** — the published
list is updated as a step in the sub-processor change record, and the annual reconciliation becomes a check
on that step rather than the mechanism — and that is **DEC-712**, taken by Tobias Lund on 2026-11-20.
**Clause 10.2 is engaged by the absence of a requirement as much as by the breach of one**, which is the
same reasoning `CA-07-01` rests on and the reason both sit in the log without a deviation above them.

## 6. P6.6, and the criterion this system is not built to answer directly

**P6.6 asks about notification of breaches and incidents to affected data subjects, regulators and others.**
Its only population in this period is the RT-02 notification, and that notification is
[07.03](07.03-the-rt-02-retention-failure.md)'s subject rather than this chapter's. What belongs here is
what it does and does not do to the criterion.

The notification of **2026-10-26 went to forty-one customers**. It did not go to a data subject, and it
could not: `CNB-C-119` reaches an individual who opens the application, and **CUEC-07** puts the employer's
own notice in the employer's hands. **The criterion asks a question this system is not built to answer
directly**, and "others" is the only word in it CloudNimbus's notification satisfies. That is a statement
about the architecture, not a conclusion about anybody's obligations, and Phase 09's description will carry
it as such.

Two consequences follow for what this chapter claims about P6.6.

**`CNB-C-132` cites P6.6 and its notification path is a customer path.** The 48-hour path under SC-02
notifies the tenant — the party that holds the relationship with the individual, and the party the data
processing addendum allocates the determination of purposes to. That is the right design for this
architecture and it is not the same thing as the criterion. A citation that reads across from "notify" to
"notify the data subject" is the kind of mapping
[04.03](../04-unified-control-framework-and-policy-architecture/04.03-mapping-methodology-and-its-limits.md)
warns about, and it is recorded here rather than repaired quietly.

**And the population is one.** P6.6 would have had none at all in this period had the RT-02 matter not
occurred and had management not decided to notify where no commitment required it. **A criterion whose only
exercise in six months arrives from a voluntary act is a criterion the examination has almost nothing to
test.**

## 7. What this chapter does not say

**It reaches no conclusion about any of the six disclosures**, beyond recording that each carries the four
fields `CNB-C-132` requires. Whether a customer's instruction to export was one that customer was entitled
to give is not a question this programme answers, and the fact that this chapter is the one place in the
portfolio where the temptation to answer it is strongest is the reason for saying so here.

**It states no rate over the observation window.** Six disclosure register entries in fifty-eight days is
not a run-rate for six months, and one sub-processor change is a population of one — the only one the
programme has had, on a control whose stated cadence is Quarterly and whose notice commitment fires only
when a change occurs. `CNB-C-131`'s notice limb has been exercised **once**, and one occurrence shows that
the mechanism works and cannot show that it works consistently.

**And zero unauthorised disclosures is the weakest number in the chapter**, for the reason 06.12 §7 gave
about zero security incidents: it is either a period in which nothing happened or a period in which
something happened and was not detected, and no control described here can tell a reader which from the
inside.
