## 1. The register the 2025 management letter was about

**CC9.2** asks whether the entity assesses and manages risks associated with vendors and business partners.
**Six controls answer CC9, and four of them answer CC9.2** — `CNB-C-088` and `CNB-C-089`, the continuity
plan and the errors-and-omissions cover, cite **CC9.1 only**, and a chapter that counted all six against
CC9.2 would have credited the criterion with two rows that do not cite it. The four are the vendor estate:
`CNB-C-090` records the outcome **and the
tier** of a proportionate security assessment before any vendor receives production data or a system
integration; `CNB-C-091` requires each of the eleven sub-processors to hold a data processing addendum
setting security requirements, breach notification timing, sub-processor disclosure and audit rights, with
no processing beginning before it is executed; `CNB-C-092` refreshes the current assurance artefact for
every Tier 1 vendor each quarter, together with a reading of what that document does and does not cover;
and `CNB-C-093` registers a cloud service new to CloudNimbus and reviews it against the cloud service
security baseline before customer data is placed in it.

This is the register **ML-3** was about. The 2025 Type I management letter recorded that the vendor register
did not distinguish subservice organisations from ordinary vendors, and 02.10 §4 records the same defect
still present six weeks after the programme was chartered, on the entry that authenticates 1.24 million
people. **The remediation was structural rather than editorial** — independent flags instead of a single
classification column, one documented test per flag, and an owner competent to apply each test — and this
chapter is the first operating record of the register in that shape.

The quarterly review that produces that record is **CAL-08**, and its Q4 occurrence was held on
**2026-10-07**, in the first fortnight of the quarter. That is the scheduling discipline `04.11` §3 argues
for and **DEC-602** corrected `04.11`'s cadence line to state precisely: CAL-03 and CAL-07 to CAL-09 are the
items properly scheduled early in their quarter, with CAL-06 deliberately late because a register review
needs a quarter's accumulated evidence to review. `01.11` §6's month-by-month view places the quarterly
items in the closing month of each quarter, which is how the calendar was drawn in January; the October date
is the corrected discipline being applied rather than a departure from it. The review record is **GOV-25**.

One handover is settled here rather than left open. **Phase 06 deferred CAL-08's Q3 occurrence to this
phase**, which owns third-party assurance, and recorded no fact about it. Declining it in turn on the
ground that this phase's period begins on 2026-10-01 would have left an occurrence inside the observation
window reported by nobody, so it is reported here.

**CAL-08's Q3 occurrence was held on 2026-07-30** — early in the quarter, and two months before Phase 06's
vantage. **DEC-602, which made early-in-quarter the stated discipline, was taken on 2026-09-02, five weeks
after this occurrence**, so the July date is reported as a fact rather than as the discipline being applied;
the Q4 review of 2026-10-07 is the first held under it. `CNB-C-092`'s Tier 1 refresh at the Q3 review
produced **the same twelve readings and the same missing artefact**: the outgoing outbound email delivery
provider's service auditor's report had lapsed on **2026-06-30**, thirty days earlier, and **the Q3 review
recorded the absence, with a replacement then in procurement and no effective date yet** — 2026-11-05 exists
only because DEC-701 chose a thirty-four-day notice on 2026-10-02. **The same vendor was absent at both
quarterly refreshes**, and §3 states what that does to the population.

**The register population has not moved since
Phase 02 published it** — 84 vendors, 11 sub-processors, 73 ordinary vendors — and §4's sub-processor
change added one and removed one on the same day.

## 2. The arithmetic, and why the tier is not one of the flags

**Eighty-four vendors are in the register. Eleven are sub-processors under a data processing addendum, and
both subservice organisations — Amazon Web Services and Halcyon Identity — are among those eleven.
Seventy-three are ordinary vendors. 11 + 73 = 84.**

This is Phase 02's arithmetic, restated rather than re-derived, and the reason it is restated in exactly
those words is that every alternative phrasing of it is wrong. The three classifications are **overlapping
sets over one population, not a partition of it**. A vendor may satisfy neither test, either, or both. AWS
satisfies both because it hosts personal data for the 41 EU-residency customers under the residency
addendum **and** performs controls the applicable trust services criteria depend on. Nine of the eleven
sub-processors are not subservice organisations, which is the clearest available demonstration that the two
tests are independent. **Any presentation of 84 as a three-way partition is wrong in the specific way ML-3
recorded.**

On top of that population sits a **tier**, and the tier is a separate axis.

| Tier | Vendors |
|---|---|
| Tier 1 | **12** |
| Tier 2 | **27** |
| Tier 3 | **45** |
| **Total** | **84** |

12 + 27 + 45 = 84. The tier is an assessment of consequence — what happens to CloudNimbus and to its
customers if this vendor fails, is breached, or stops — and it drives the depth of the assessment
`CNB-C-090` requires and the assurance refresh `CNB-C-092` performs. It does not describe what the vendor
is.

The relationship between the tier and the sub-processor flag is where a register goes wrong, and the numbers
make it visible:

| Statement | Count |
|---|---|
| Sub-processors that are Tier 1 | **9** |
| Sub-processors that are Tier 2 | **2** |
| Tier 1 vendors that are **not** sub-processors | **3** |
| | 9 + 2 = **11** · 9 + 3 = **12** |

**The three Tier 1 vendors that are not sub-processors receive no personal data at all.** They hold a system
integration — a credential, an API path, a place in the platform's operation — and the consequence of one of
them failing is a Tier 1 consequence whether or not a single personal data field ever crosses the boundary.
Conversely, **two sub-processors are Tier 2**: they process personal data under an addendum, and the
consequence of their failure does not reach the top band.

> **Tier is an axis of the register. It is not the register's classification.** A vendor's row carries three
> independent flags — is it a sub-processor, is it a subservice organisation, what is its tier — and reading
> any one of them off another is the error the single classification column made possible.

`CNB-C-090` records **the outcome and the tier** for exactly this reason, and the conjunction in the control
statement is doing structural work rather than adding detail. An assessment outcome without a tier cannot be
proportionate to anything; a tier without an outcome is a label. **DEC-702**, taken by Tobias Lund at the
CAL-08 review on 2026-10-07, records the position that the tier is an axis and not a classification, and
**ADR-0035** carries the reasoning and the alternative that was rejected — a single "criticality
classification" field that would have collapsed the three flags back into the one column ML-3 was about.

The practical test of whether a register has understood this is a question nobody can answer from a
partition: **how many Tier 1 vendors receive no personal data?** The answer here is three, it is a fact
about the estate rather than an oddity, and a register that sorted 84 into three exclusive classes could not
produce it.

## 3. The Tier 1 assurance refresh, and one vendor holding nothing

`CNB-C-092` requires the current assurance artefact for every Tier 1 vendor — its service auditor's report
or its certificate, together with **a reading of what that document does and does not cover** — to be
refreshed each quarter and the conclusion recorded in the vendor register. Twelve vendors, twelve readings,
performed at the CAL-08 review of 2026-10-07 — and twelve more performed at the CAL-08 review of
2026-07-30, which is the other occurrence the observation window holds.

| What the Tier 1 vendor holds | Count |
|---|---|
| A current SOC 2 Type II report | **8** |
| An ISO/IEC 27001 certificate only | **2** |
| Both | **1** |
| **Neither** | **1** |
| **Total** | **12** |

8 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 12. **That is the position at the Q4 refresh, and the Q3 refresh of 2026-07-30 produced the
same four counts against the same twelve vendors.**

**The one holding neither is the outgoing email delivery provider**, and it is the same one at both
refreshes. Its service auditor's report period
ended and the report lapsed on **2026-06-30**; no successor report was produced, and no certificate exists.
It is the same vendor that [07.07 §4](07.07-disclosure-and-the-sub-processor-notice.md) records being
replaced, with notice issued 2026-10-02 and the change effective 2026-11-05.

> **`D-07-02` · `CNB-C-092` · One Tier 1 vendor held no current assurance artefact at two consecutive
> quarterly refreshes · **2 of 24** — two refreshes, twelve readings each, the same vendor absent at both ·
> Clause 10.2 corrective action `CA-07-03`.**

**The population is 2 of 24 and not 1 of 12, and the difference is the whole finding.** The Q3 refresh of
2026-07-30 read the same twelve artefacts, found the same one missing thirty days after it lapsed, and
recorded it against a replacement targeted for 2026-11-05. The Q4 refresh of 2026-10-07 read the same
twelve, found the same one missing, and recorded the same answer. **The control that exists to notice
noticed twice, and noticing twice and not closing it is worse than noticing once** — the first occurrence is
a lapse detected, the second is a lapse detected and left standing for a further quarter with a remediation
date as the answer.

The replacement is real, it was decided before either refresh, and it completed on 2026-11-05. None of that
changes what the register said on 2026-07-30, on 2026-10-07, or on any day between 2026-06-30 and the change
taking effect: **a Tier 1 vendor with a live integration was carrying no current assurance artefact for four
months.** A remediation already in flight is an answer to "what are you doing about it"
and no answer at all to "how long was this true, and what told you". `CA-07-03` addresses the cause rather
than the instance: the
refresh reads artefacts at a quarterly cadence, and an artefact's **expiry date** is knowable the day it is
first read. A control that discovers a lapse up to three months after it happens is a control whose cadence
is doing the work its data could do — and a control that re-discovers the same lapse a quarter later has
demonstrated that the cadence is not the only thing missing.

The other eleven readings at each refresh produced no finding, and that is a weaker statement than it looks.
`CNB-C-092`'s
requirement is not that an artefact exists; it is that the artefact is **read for what it does and does not
cover**. Twenty-two artefact readings across the two refreshes are twenty-two conclusions in the register,
and
[07.10](07.10-reading-the-other-sides-complementary-controls.md) is what happens when that reading is taken
to its logical end on the two artefacts that matter most.

## 4. A certificate and a report are not substitutes

Three of the twelve Tier 1 vendors hold an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate — two of them hold nothing else — and
the register has to be explicit about what that is worth, because the commonest way a vendor register stops
being useful is that it starts treating the two artefacts as interchangeable evidence of the same thing.

**They answer different questions.**

An **ISO/IEC 27001:2022 certificate** is granted by an accredited certification body and attests that an
information security management system conforms to the requirements of clauses 4 to 10, assessed at a point
in time within a three-year cycle with annual surveillance audits between. It tells the reader that a
management system exists, that its scope is stated on the certificate, that an independent body has audited
it against the requirements of the standard, and that Annex A was used as the reference set clause 6.1.3 c)
requires. It does not tell the reader which controls were tested, on what population, over what period, or
what was found.

A **SOC 2 Type II report** is a CPA firm's attestation examination, carrying a practitioner's opinion on the
suitability of design **and operating effectiveness** of specified controls **throughout a specified
period**, with the controls, the tests performed and the results set out in Section IV. It tells the reader
exactly what was tested and what the tests found, including any deviations, on a population and a period the
reader can see. It says nothing about whether the entity operates a management system that conforms to
anything.

> **One tells you a system exists and is audited; the other tells you what was tested, on what population,
> and what was found. Neither is better. They answer different questions, and a vendor register that treats
> them as interchangeable is a register that has stopped reading.**

Two consequences follow for how the twelve rows are recorded. **The scope statement on a certificate is
read, not assumed** — a certificate whose scope covers a corporate function and not the service CloudNimbus
consumes is a certificate about something else, and the reading `CNB-C-092` requires is where that is
caught. And **a report's period is read against CloudNimbus's own**, which is the argument
[07.11](07.11-subservice-organisations-and-the-uncovered-months.md) makes at length about the two
subservice organisations and which applies in a smaller way to every Tier 1 vendor holding a Type II report.

This is also the point at which the register has to police its own language, and the programme's standing
framing is the reason. **There is no such thing as being "SOC 2 certified".** A SOC 2 examination produces a
CPA's opinion, not a certificate; ISO does not certify anybody, accredited certification bodies do; and a
register row reading "certified" against a vendor holding a Type II report has recorded the wrong instrument
and will be relied on by somebody who needed to know the difference.

## 5. What the register still does not tell anybody

**Seventy-two vendors are not Tier 1 and their assurance artefacts are not refreshed quarterly.** Tier 2 and
Tier 3 carry proportionate assessment under `CNB-C-090` at onboarding and no quarterly refresh, which is a
deliberate allocation of a 4.6-FTE programme's attention and is stated here rather than left for a reader to
discover from the cadence column. The exposure is that a Tier 2 vendor's circumstances can change without
anything in the calendar asking.

**An assurance artefact is evidence about the vendor's controls and about nothing else.** A clean report
from a vendor says nothing about whether CloudNimbus configured its use of that vendor correctly, which is
02.10 §9's point about AWS applied to the other eighty-three rows.

**And the assessment `CNB-C-090` requires happens before the vendor is engaged.** For most of the
seventy-two, the security assessment in the register is the assessment made on the day they were onboarded,
and for some of them that day was several years ago. The register records the outcome and the tier, both
accurate as at the date they were recorded, and it does not claim either is a current statement.
