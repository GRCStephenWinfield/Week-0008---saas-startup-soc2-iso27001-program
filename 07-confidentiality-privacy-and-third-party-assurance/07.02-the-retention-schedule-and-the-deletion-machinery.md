## 1. A retention schedule is a set of promises about absence

Most of a control library is about presence: this data is encrypted, this account is authorised, this
calculation reconciles. A retention schedule is the opposite. It promises that on a stated day a record
**stops existing**, and the evidence for a thing that no longer exists is harder to produce than the
evidence for a thing that does.

That asymmetry sets the shape of this chapter. Three controls carry the whole of retention and deletion at
CloudNimbus. `CNB-C-126` generates and runs the jobs. `CNB-C-127` records what each job did and alerts when
one does not report. `CNB-C-128` tracks the residue that survives in backups after a deletion in the primary
stores. **All three produce records of absence**, and the credibility of the schedule rests entirely on
whether those records are read.

**This chapter describes the machinery. 07.03 describes what it did**, which in one region on one rule was
nothing at all for sixty-eight nights.

## 2. Eight rules over twelve categories, and the mapping is not tidy

Phase 02 published the rules and, to its credit, published the untidiness with them rather than presenting
eight rules and twelve categories as a covering set. That position is restated here because the privacy
control design has to be built against the real mapping and not the flattering one.

| ID | Data | Retention | Category governed |
|---|---|---|---|
| RT-01 | Time and attendance records | Contract term plus 90 days | PD-04 |
| RT-02 | **Geolocation at clock-in** | **13 months, then deleted, irrespective of contract term** | PD-05 |
| RT-03 | Expense records and receipt images | Contract term plus 90 days | PD-08 |
| RT-04 | Absence and leave records | Contract term plus 90 days | PD-07 |
| RT-05 | Authentication and audit logs | 13 months queryable, then 7 years pseudonymised in the `cnb-security` archive | PD-12 |
| RT-06 | Support interaction records | 24 months | PD-11 |
| RT-07 | Backups and snapshots | 35 days, rolling | The estate |
| RT-08 | All customer data after termination | Deleted within 30 days of termination, with a certificate (O7, SC-03) | The estate |

**Six rules govern a named category and two govern the estate.** PD-10, manager approval and comment
records, is held with the records it approves and expires with them under RT-01 and RT-04. **Five categories
have no in-life rule at all — PD-01, PD-02, PD-03, PD-06 and PD-09** — and are governed by the contract-term
default and RT-08 alone.

RT-02 is the only rule in the table whose clock is shorter than the commercial relationship that generates
the data, and it is short by design. Geolocation was argued for removal from the platform in Phase 02 and
refused on the strength of the 387 customers who had then enabled it; **what the refusal bought was paid for
in retention**, at thirteen months rather than contract term plus ninety days. It is therefore the rule most
likely to be tested by a reader, the rule with the largest nightly delete volume, and the rule that failed.

### 2.1 PD-03, and the gap Phase 02 handed to this phase

Phase 02 closed §6.1 by recording one gap explicitly and carrying it here: **PD-03 — role, department, FTE,
cost centre and pay rate — is the category among the five uncovered that most deserves a rule of its own,
and it does not have one.** It is what somebody earns, for every employee of every customer, held for the
life of a commercial relationship that may run for years and covering individuals who left those employers
long ago. A retention position that treats an individual's pay rate the way it treats their work telephone
number has inherited a default rather than made a decision.

**This phase does not close it either, and says so rather than letting a promise quietly lapse.** The two
designable candidates are unchanged: expiry of historical rate records a stated period after they cease to
be current, and expiry of the whole record a stated period after an individual ceases to be active in a
tenant. Both remain designable and neither has been designed, because retention shortened below the contract
term has to be reconciled against **SR-06** — calculation runs reproducible from their inputs and rule
version — and against the customers' own record-keeping arrangements, which are the customers' and not
CloudNimbus's to shorten. A rule that expired a pay rate the calculation engine still needs to reproduce a
prior period would break a commitment in SC-07 to fix a gap in P4.2.

What has changed since Phase 02 is that the work now has a place to go. The **`CNB-C-133` quarterly privacy
review** is the forum, it met on 2026-10-28, and the PD-03 rule is on its standing agenda. What has not
changed is that no rule exists, and the honest statement of the position at this vantage is that **five of
twelve categories are still governed by the contract term and RT-08 alone**, one of them being the most
sensitive of the five.

## 3. The three controls

| Control | What it requires, as published | Criteria | Annex A |
|---|---|---|---|
| `CNB-C-126` | Scheduled deletion jobs run nightly against each store to enforce RT-01 to RT-08, including deletion of geolocation at 13 months irrespective of contract term, **and the job set is generated from the retention schedule rather than maintained by hand** | P4.2 | A.5.33, A.8.10 |
| `CNB-C-127` | Each scheduled deletion job writes a completion record stating **rows deleted per store**, and **a job that does not report inside its window** alerts the data platform on-call engineer, who runs the catch-up deletion before the next cycle | P4.2, P4.3 | A.8.10, A.8.16 |
| `CNB-C-128` | Deletions executed in primary stores are tracked against the 35-day backup cycle, and the data platform owner confirms residue expiry each month and records the residue window on the deletion certificate | P4.3 | A.8.10, A.8.13 |

**`CNB-C-126`'s generation clause carries the weight of the three, and it is also the one that carried the
defect.** A job set maintained by hand drifts: somebody adds a store and forgets the job, somebody changes a
rule and edits six of the seven places it appears. Generating the jobs from the schedule makes the schedule
the single source and removes an entire class of omission. What it does not do — and what nothing in the
statement claims it does — is guarantee that the **physical object** a generated job resolves to is still
the object the rule means. The generator records that object **by identifier, at generation time**, and an
object identifier survives a rename. **Generation binds the job to the rule. It does not bind the job to the
object the rule is currently about**, and nothing in the library compares the two.

**The generation cadence is the other half of that, and the whole failure depends on it. The job set is
generated on a change to the retention schedule and not on a timer**, so a generated job's recorded target
persists until the schedule changes. Had the RT-02 job set regenerated on any interval during the
sixty-eight nights it would have re-resolved the rule against the live relation and healed itself without
anybody noticing; it did not, because nothing was due to make it.

**`CNB-C-127` is where the evidence lives.** Its completion record is one unit of **EC-17**: rule applied,
rows deleted, start and finish, success flag, and any catch-up run linked to it. Every night, across every
store, across three regions, that record is written. It is the most voluminous evidence class in the privacy
family and, until October, the least read.

**`CNB-C-128` is the control that admits something.** It exists because the schedule contradicts itself, and
§4 is about that.

Together the three answer **P4.2** — the retention of personal information — and **P4.3** — its disposal.
They do not answer **P4.1**, which concerns limiting use to the purposes identified, and that is not an
omission: P4.1 is served by `CNB-C-123`, which records a stated purpose against each optional collection at
the tenant, and by `CNB-C-124`, which refuses a pull request introducing a personal-data field the inventory
does not know about. 07.05 owns that half. **A retention rule expires data; it does not constrain what the
data is used for while it is alive**, and a library that answered P4.1 with a deletion job would have
answered the wrong question with the right mechanism.

## 4. The residue, which is disclosed rather than engineered away

**RT-07 and RT-08 conflict, and the number is 65.** RT-08 commits CloudNimbus to delete all customer data
within 30 days of termination and to certify it. RT-07 holds backups on a 35-day rolling cycle. The last
backup that can contain a record is the one taken on the day the deletion ran, and that backup does not
expire for 35 days. **30 + 35 = 65 days** in which a certified-deleted record still exists inside an
encrypted backup that has not aged out.

This is not a defect better engineering removes. A backup that could be surgically edited to remove one
tenant's rows would be a mutable copy, and SR-10's fifteen-minute recovery point objective depends on it
being neither. Every service organisation offering both a deletion commitment and a recovery objective has
this window; most do not mention it.

CloudNimbus mentions it in four places, and the fourth is the one that requires discipline.

The **deletion certificate states what it covers and what it does not** — deletion from the live production
stores the orchestrator enumerates, the residue, the maximum period, and the date the residue expires. The
**residue is protected as production data is protected**, encrypted at rest under region-scoped
customer-managed keys and held in the residency region of the source data under SR-02 and SR-03. The
**residue expires without intervention**, which matters because the failure mode of a manual deletion
follow-up is that it is forgotten once the customer has gone. And **restoration from a snapshot predating a
deletion triggers a documented re-deletion**, which is the only one of the four that depends on a person
doing something.

That fourth part is **R-33** — *a restoration from backup reintroduces data that had been deleted*, 2 × 3 =
6, Low — and it is the one the register has evidence about. **TP-31**, the post-restoration deletion
re-execution, was **proven at the CAL-10 disaster recovery exercise of 2026-08-19**: the restored estate was
re-deleted and the re-execution recorded. Phase 06 owns that exercise and this chapter does no more than
name what it established, which is that the procedure exists and has run once under observation.

**R-15** — *backup residue means data survives past the 30-day deletion commitment* — is the register's
entry for the window itself. **It stands at 4 × 2 = 8 at this vantage: likelihood 4, impact 2.** It is not
at a floor and it is not fixed. The eight floor at `03.02` §5.3 is the rule for entries sitting at **2 × 4**,
where likelihood has already reached 2 and 1 is reserved; the twelve entries `03.02` §5.3 pins are the
twelve carrying impact 4 or 5, and **R-15 is not among them**. Its likelihood limb has three steps left in
it. **`03.07` forecasts R-15 at 2 × 2 = 4, Low, at close.** Nothing in this period moved it, because **no
CAL-06 review falls in this period** and the Q4 review is 2026-12-29.

The residue is the reason **`CNB-C-128` also governs the RT-02 correction**. A catch-up deletion in the
primary stores does not end the matter; the residue window runs from the catch-up, and 07.03 §3 records that
it expires on **2026-11-29**, two days after this vantage. **DEC-705** and **ADR-0033** carry the position
that a deletion is not certified complete until the residue expires, which is the same discipline applied to
a correction that Phase 02 applied to a certificate.

## 5. `CNB-C-149` — the library grows for the first time

**The unified control library has been 148 controls since Phase 04 published it. On 2026-10-28 it became
149**, and every count in this phase says 149 and says when it changed.

`CNB-C-149` was admitted at the `CNB-C-133` quarterly privacy review of 2026-10-28 (**GOV-27**) under
**DEC-706**, Karim Haddad. The statement as admitted:

> **`CNB-C-149`** — A retention job that reports success having deleted zero rows on a rule whose eligible
> population is non-zero raises an alert to the data platform owner, and the alert is dispositioned before
> the next run. Family `P`. Type **Detective**. Cadence **Daily**. Owner **Devon Ashby**. Policy `POL-07`.
> Criteria **P4.2, P4.3**. Annex A **A.8.10, A.8.16**.

**The policy is `POL-07`, Data Retention and Deletion, and not `POL-06`, Data Classification and Handling.**
`CNB-C-118`, `CNB-C-126`, `CNB-C-127` and `CNB-C-128` all cite POL-07, and `04.08` makes the
one-policy-per-control rule load-bearing precisely so that the question *when POL-07 changes, which controls
must be re-assessed?* has an answer that can be computed rather than remembered. A control filed against the
wrong policy is a control the next policy review does not know it owns.

It is the direct answer to `CNB-C-127`'s design gap: `CNB-C-127` alerts on a record that does not arrive,
`CNB-C-149` alerts on a record that arrives saying nothing happened when something was due. **ADR-0031**
records the decision and the alternative considered.

**What the growth costs has to be said rather than celebrated**, because a new control is the easiest thing
in the world for a programme to be pleased about.

**A control admitted in October cannot evidence October the first.** Its population inside the observation
window is **65 nights, 2026-10-28 to 2026-12-31**, of which **31 have run at this vantage, with 0 alerts
raised**. A stated cadence of **Daily** against a window of **184 days** will be sampled as what it is: a
control that did not exist for the first 119 of them. Nothing about the admission repairs the sixty-eight
nights, and nothing about it makes the earlier part of the window look better.

**The eligible-population half is the hard half, and it is a real engineering cost.** A rule whose eligible
population is genuinely zero on a given night must not alert, or the control becomes noise inside a week and
is muted inside a month. So the control has to compute **what was due** independently of the job that
deletes it — which means **two implementations of one retention rule**, maintained separately, in the same
codebase, and expected to agree. That is a maintenance cost and it is also a source of a different failure:
two implementations that drift apart produce either a false alert every night or a silent agreement that
both are wrong in the same way. The alternative — deriving the expected count from the deletion job
itself — was rejected in ADR-0031 for the obvious reason that it would have reproduced the original defect
exactly: **the predicate was correct and was evaluated against the wrong object**, so a control that derived
its expectation from the same job would have resolved the same object and been told zero.

**And it is a relevant change to the system during the period.** That belongs under **DC9**, which asks the
description to disclose relevant details of changes to the system during the period, and Phase 09 carries
it. It is **not** DC5, which concerns the aspects of the system the criteria are applied against, and it is
**not** DC4, which is engaged by the incident and not by the control admitted in response to it. Naming the
right criterion matters here in the same way it mattered in 04.11: a change disclosed under the wrong
description criterion is a change the reader looks for in the wrong section.

### 5.1 It detects total non-deletion and not partial, and that is on its face

**`CNB-C-149`'s condition is a deletion of zero.** A thirteen-month rolling window over monthly partitions
has thirteen partitions. **A predicate that reaches twelve of them deletes a non-zero number of rows and
under-deletes the rest — and `CNB-C-149` is silent, because the count is not zero.** The control catches
total non-enforcement of a rule. It does not catch partial non-enforcement of one, and a reader who takes
"the detection gap is closed" from §5 would be taking more than the statement gives.

The design that would catch it was considered and rejected: **alert on a material divergence between the
rows deleted and the rows expected**, rather than on zero. ADR-0031 records why it was refused, and the
reason is a real trade rather than a clean win. **On a light night the expected count is small and noisy** —
a rule whose eligible population that night is forty rows will diverge by a large proportion on a rounding
difference, a clock skew or a row inserted between the two computations, and a threshold loose enough to
survive those nights is a threshold that would not have caught anything smaller than the October condition
anyway. Zero is the only divergence that is unambiguous at every volume. The programme took the unambiguous
condition and **recorded the case it therefore cannot see** rather than describing the narrower control in
the wider control's language.

### 5.2 The ownership concentration, named rather than left to be noticed

**Devon Ashby owns `CNB-C-126`, `CNB-C-127`, `CNB-C-128` and now `CNB-C-149`**, owns the corrective action
`CA-07-02`, owns risk **R-38**, and took **DEC-705, DEC-707, DEC-708 and DEC-713**. **A detective control
over a failed control, owned by, alerting, and dispositioned by the person who owns the failed control, is a
concentration worth naming**, and this chapter names it rather than leaving a reader to assemble it from
four documents.

One thing about the design does separate the two. **`CNB-C-149` alerts the *data platform owner*, where
`CNB-C-127` alerts the *on-call engineer*.** That is deliberate: the on-call rota exists to restore a job
that did not run, which is an operational act, while a job that ran and deleted nothing on a rule with a
population raises a question about whether the rule is being enforced at all, which is an accountable one.
Routing the second to a rota would have produced a page somebody acknowledges at 03:00 and a disposition
nobody owns.

What it does not do is separate the reviewer from the owner. The programme **records the acceptance rather
than inventing a second reviewer it has no headcount for**: at 187 people there is one data platform owner,
and a nominated second disposition reviewer drawn from outside the data platform would be reviewing an alert
they could not evaluate. The mitigation that exists is that **every `CNB-C-149` alert and its disposition is
a unit of EC-17 and is read at the `CNB-C-133` quarterly privacy review**, which Tobias Lund chairs and
Devon Ashby does not. That is a quarterly check on a daily control, it is weaker than a second pair of eyes
on the night, and it is what there is.

## 6. What the machinery evidences, and what it does not

| Proposition | Position at 2026-11-27 | What supports it |
|---|---|---|
| Deletion jobs are generated from the retention schedule rather than maintained by hand | **Evidenced** | `CNB-C-126`, the generated job set, EC-17 |
| Every job writes a completion record stating rows deleted per store | **Evidenced, on every night of the period** | `CNB-C-127`, EC-17 |
| A job that fails to report inside its window is alerted | **Evidenced by design; population zero in the period** | No job failed to report. The absence of an alert is not evidence the alert works |
| A job that reports success having deleted nothing on a rule with a non-zero eligible population is alerted | **Not evidenced before 2026-10-28, and the reason is that no control required it** | `CNB-C-149`, 31 of 65 nights run, 0 alerts |
| A job that deletes *some* of what was due and under-deletes the rest is alerted | **No. `CNB-C-149`'s condition is a deletion of zero** | §5.1; a divergence threshold was considered and refused |
| RT-01 and RT-03 to RT-08 were enforced across all three regions | **Evidenced** | `CNB-C-127` completion records for the period |
| RT-02 was enforced in `eu-central-1` | **Not evidenced from 2026-08-17 to 2026-10-23 — 1 of 8 rules, in 1 of 3 regions** | `D-07-01`; 07.03 |
| Backup residue is tracked and its expiry confirmed | **Evidenced monthly** | `CNB-C-128`, EC-18 |
| A restoration re-triggers deletion of what had been deleted | **Evidenced once, under exercise conditions** | TP-31, proven at CAL-10 on 2026-08-19 |
| Five of the twelve categories have an in-life retention rule of their own | **False, and stated as such** | PD-01, PD-02, PD-03, PD-06 and PD-09 have none |

**The third row is the one worth pausing on.** `CNB-C-127`'s alert limb has a population of zero in this
period: no scheduled deletion job failed to report inside its window, in any region, on any night. That is a
clean result and it is not evidence of anything. **A control whose triggering condition never occurred has
not been shown to work**, and Phase 06 made the same point about `CNB-C-067`'s automatic escalation limb.
The uncomfortable version, which this chapter states because 07.03 makes it unavoidable: for sixty-eight of
those nights the alert limb was not merely untriggered but **incapable of triggering** on the condition that
actually existed.

**The last row is the oldest open thing in this chapter.** Phase 02 recorded it, Phase 07 inherited it, and
Phase 07 is closing without having fixed it. What it has done instead is put the question in front of a
control that meets quarterly with a named owner, which is more than it had, and less than a rule.
