## 1. Two columns that decide whether a control can be tested

Of the nine columns in the control library, two determine whether a control is examinable at all. **Owner**
decides whether there is a person who can be asked what happened; **Cadence** decides how many times the
control operated inside the period, which is the population a service auditor samples from. A control with
a perfect statement, a correct mapping and a policy behind it is untestable if nobody owns it and
unsampleable if it operated once, in March, outside the window.

This chapter is about those two columns, and it carries the phase's most uncomfortable finding.

## 2. No control is owned by a team

**DEC-405**, taken by Karim Haddad on 2026-05-15 and recorded in **ADR-0020**, requires every control in
the library to name an individual. Not a team, not a rota, not a function, not a committee.

The argument against is practical and was made properly: individuals leave, go on holiday and change roles,
and a library naming people needs maintaining in a way that one naming "the SRE team" does not. That is why
the rule needed a decision rather than an assumption.

The argument for is that **accountability that is distributed is accountability that is absent**, and it
shows up in three specific places.

**In an audit interview.** An auditor asks who performs the control and what happened on a particular date.
"The infrastructure team" cannot answer. Someone will be produced, and that person either actually does it
— in which case the register was wrong — or is reconstructing what a colleague did.

**When the control does not operate.** A monthly restore test that did not happen has, if it is owned by a
team, no one who failed to do it and no one to explain it. The failure mode is not dishonesty; a missed
occurrence owned by everyone is noticed by no one until the sample is drawn in January.

**When the control needs to change.** A control whose threshold is wrong, whose evidence is not producible
or whose cadence has drifted needs an owner with standing to propose it. Teams do not propose changes;
people do.

The rule has a cost, paid openly: **the library is maintained when people move**, and a transfer of
ownership is a change to documented information under `CNB-C-145`, approved rather than noticed. A register
that never needs updating because it names functions that never leave has traded accuracy for stability.

## 3. Cadence and the population it creates

The observation window is **2026-07-01 to 2026-12-31** — six months, **184 days**, approximately 26 weeks.
Cadence is not how often a control is convenient to run. It is a commitment about a population, and the
population determines what a sample can show.

| Cadence | Occurrences in a six-month window | Controls in the library | Occurrences contributed | What a single miss does to the deviation rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Continuous | A system-generated population; the auditor samples from records rather than from occurrences | 58 | Population-scale | Depends on the sample size drawn |
| Daily | ~184 | 9 | ~1,656 | Under 1% |
| Weekly | ~26 | 6 | ~156 | ~4% of that control's population |
| Monthly | 6 | 21 | 126 | ~17% |
| Quarterly | 2 | 27 | 54 | **50%** |
| Semi-annual | 1 | 5 | 5 | **100%** |
| Annual | 1, or 0 | 22 | 22 at best | **100%, or untestable** |

**Quarterly and semi-annual controls are where a single late occurrence becomes a deviation rate of 50% or
100%.** This is the arithmetic that most control libraries discover in January. A quarterly access review
completed nine days after its due date is, in operational terms, a minor slip. In a six-month window it is
one of two occurrences, and the service auditor records a deviation rate of 50% on that control. The
control did not become worse; the window became shorter than the cadence can absorb.

Three consequences follow, and CloudNimbus has acted on all three.

**First, cadence choices in the library were made against this table rather than against operational
comfort.** Where a control could honestly run monthly rather than quarterly, it was written to run monthly,
because six occurrences absorb a slip that two do not. Where a control genuinely operates quarterly —
CAL-06 risk review, CAL-07 access review, CAL-08 sub-processor review — it stays quarterly, and the
exposure is stated rather than engineered away by claiming a frequency nobody will meet.

**Second, this is why the treatment plan pushed 31 of 34 items before 2026-07-01.** A control implemented
in August has, at best, a partial period of operation, and a quarterly control implemented in August has
one occurrence where the population should be two. The three items that could not be pulled forward — the
disaster recovery exercise, the internal audit programme, and an availability architecture change requiring
a maintenance window — are named in Phase 03 for that reason, and each has a scheduled date inside the
window rather than an intention.

**Third, quarterly and semi-annual controls need their due dates set early in each period, not at the end.**
A control due "in Q3" that operates on 30 September has no margin; the same control scheduled for the first
fortnight of the quarter can slip twice and still be on time. This is a scheduling discipline in the
assurance calendar rather than a control design decision, and it is the cheapest risk reduction available
in this chapter.

## 4. The twenty-two annual controls

**Twenty-two controls in the library operate annually.** In a six-month window each of them has a
population of **at most one**, and a population of **zero** if its annual operation fell outside the window.
A control with a population of zero cannot be tested for operating effectiveness in the period whatever its
design — the auditor has nothing to select. It is not a deviation; it is an absence of evidence about the
period, and it is dealt with by the description of the system stating what the control is and when it last
operated.

**Twelve of the twenty-two fall naturally inside the window. Ten do not.** That split is derived below,
control by control, against the assurance calendar in 01.11 — because a total asserted without its
derivation is exactly the kind of figure that turns out, when somebody checks it, to have been the figure
the programme wanted.

### 4.3 `CNB-C-031`, and why the re-scheduling is load-bearing rather than tidy

One of the ten deserves separating from the other nine. **`CNB-C-031` is the single control serving CC5.3**,
which 04.03 already names as the library's sharpest concentration: one criterion, one control, and an
annual cadence that gives that control a population of at most one.

The procedure inventory was completed with the policy suite on **2026-06-12**, which fixed the control's
anniversary in June. On its natural rhythm the next occurrence would fall in **June 2027**, six months after
the window closed. **CC5.3 would then have had no testable occurrence at all** — not a thin population, not
a sample of one, but nothing: the criterion is served by one control, that control would not have operated
in the period, and the service auditor would have had nothing to select.

That is materially worse than a concentration, and it is worth being exact about the difference. A
concentration means a deviation has nothing to fall back on. An empty population means there is no
occurrence to have a deviation in, and no evidence about the period either way. The first is a disclosed
exposure; the second is a criterion the examination cannot reach.

So the re-scheduling of `CNB-C-031` into September is not tidiness and is not presentation. **It is the
difference between CC5.3 being supported by one occurrence and CC5.3 being supported by none**, and it is
recorded here in those terms rather than buried in a list of ten. If a reader wants to test whether the
disclosure in §4.4 is a genuine disclosure or a formality, this is the row to test it against.

### 4.4 The argument, made without flinching

**Re-performing an annual control inside an observation window so that it has a population is a legitimate
thing to do and a dishonest thing to hide.**

It is legitimate for a plain reason: **the control genuinely operates**. `CNB-C-116` really does rotate the
keys, in October rather than in May; the re-encryption really does complete; the record really is produced.
Nothing is simulated, nothing is back-dated, and the second operation is not a rehearsal for the auditor's
benefit — it is the control, running. An organisation that chose instead to leave the ten outside the
window would be presenting a description of the system containing ten controls the examination cannot say
anything about — one of them the only control serving a criterion — which serves nobody: not the user
entity reading the report, not the auditor, and not CloudNimbus.

It becomes dishonest at exactly one point: **the moment the re-performance is not disclosed.** A reader who
sees a control described as annual, and an occurrence dated inside the window, will infer a natural annual
rhythm that happens to fall there. If it does not — if the occurrence is there because the schedule was
moved to put it there — then the reader has drawn a conclusion the entity knew they would draw and did not
correct. That is not a technical breach of anything. It is a description that is accurate in every
particular and misleading as a whole, which is the failure mode that description criteria exist to prevent.

CloudNimbus's position is therefore three-part and is written down before the window opens rather than
explained afterwards:

1. **The ten are re-scheduled**, with dates set in advance and recorded in ADR-0019.
2. **The re-scheduling is disclosed** in the description of the system, stating the cadence, the previous
   operation, the re-scheduled operation and the reason.
3. **The dates are visible in the evidence itself.** A key rotation record in May and another in October is
   not a fact CloudNimbus could conceal if it wanted to; the service auditor will see two operations in
   under twelve months on a control described as annual, and will ask. **The answer being already written
   down is the difference between a disclosure and an explanation.**

There is one honest limit to state. **Re-performance does not manufacture a population of two.** The window
can carry one occurrence of an annual control, and one occurrence is a sample of one: it can show that the
control operated and produced its artefact, and it cannot show consistency over time. A control with a
population of one is weak evidence about a period, and it is weak whether or not the occurrence was
re-scheduled. What re-scheduling changes is the difference between weak evidence and none.

### 4.5 The pattern worth naming: preventive controls with a periodic re-baseline

Several of the ten share a shape that deserves a name, because it is easy to mistake for a fudge.
`CNB-C-099`, `CNB-C-116` and `CNB-C-137` are recorded as **Preventive** with an annual cadence, and
`CNB-C-140` — which is **Quarterly** and is not one of the ten — has the same shape at a higher frequency.
The enforcement — the module set, the key policy, the build gate, the filtering — runs continuously; the
**operating occurrence that is sampled** is the periodic re-approval that keeps the enforcement correct.

That is a real control shape and not an accounting convenience, but it carries an obligation: **the row must
say which of the two the cadence refers to.** A control described as "web filtering is enforced" with a
cadence of Quarterly invites an auditor to sample four quarters of enforcement and find no such artefact.
A control described as "the category set and standing exceptions are re-approved quarterly" tells the
auditor precisely what to ask for. Every row in this library that carries this shape names the periodic act
in its statement, and that is why the statements are longer than they would otherwise need to be.

## 5. What this chapter commits the programme to

| Commitment | Where it is enforced |
|---|---|
| Every control names an individual | ADR-0020; the build harness rejects a non-individual owner |
| Every control states one of seven cadences | The library schema; no free-text frequencies |
| Quarterly and semi-annual controls are scheduled early in their period | The assurance calendar, CAL-03 and CAL-06 to CAL-09 |
| The ten re-scheduled annual controls operate on the dates in §4.2 | ADR-0019 and the assurance calendar |
| The re-scheduling is disclosed in the description of the system | **DC5**, which this phase carries, and **DC9** relevant changes during the period, which Phase 09 carries |

The last row is the one that matters most and is the easiest to lose. A decision taken in June, recorded in
an architectural decision record, disclosed nowhere the reader of the report will look, is a decision that
was taken honestly and communicated dishonestly. **The disclosure is not the ADR. The ADR is the reason the
disclosure exists.**

The description criteria the disclosure belongs under are worth naming precisely, because the obvious
answer is the wrong one. It is **not DC4**, which concerns disclosure of incidents. A control's stated
cadence and the occurrences that support it are part of **DC5** — the applicable trust services criteria
and the related controls — which is this phase's to answer, and the fact that a schedule was moved during
the period is a relevant change under **DC9**, which Phase 09 carries. Filing a scheduling disclosure under
the incident criterion would put it where no reader of the description looks for it.
