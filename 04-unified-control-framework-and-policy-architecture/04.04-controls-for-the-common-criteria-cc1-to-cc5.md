## 1. What CC1 to CC5 are, and why they read differently from the rest

The Common Criteria are 33 criteria in nine groups. The last four groups — CC6 to CC9 — are recognisably
about information security: access, operations, change, risk mitigation. **The first five are not.** CC1 to
CC5 are the trust services criteria's expression of the **seventeen COSO principles**, and Appendix A of
TSP section 100 carries the mapping between them.

That inheritance is why these criteria feel unlike the rest of the framework, and why control libraries
tend to be weakest here. CC1.1 asks about a commitment to integrity and ethical values. CC1.2 asks whether
the board demonstrates independence and exercises oversight. CC3.3 asks whether the entity considers the
potential for fraud. These are questions about how an organisation is governed, and an engineering-led
programme reaches for them last, writes something plausible, and moves on to the part it understands.

The consequence shows up in a Type II in a specific way. **CC1 to CC5 controls are, on average, the lowest
frequency in the library**, because governance operates quarterly and annually rather than continuously.
Nine of the 31 controls in this chapter operate annually and nine operate quarterly — which means that in a
six-month observation window, more than half of this chapter has a population of one or two. A single late
Audit &amp; Risk Committee pack is not a minor administrative slip in that arithmetic. 04.11 works the
consequence through in full.

**All thirteen SOC 2-only controls in the library's Common Criteria sit in this chapter**, and the reason is
structural rather than accidental. Annex A is a catalogue of **information security controls**. It contains
nothing about board oversight, nothing about a delegation of authority matrix, nothing about the assessment
of fraud risk, and nothing about the operation of a risk process — because ISO/IEC 27001 puts the risk
process in **clauses 6.1.2 and 6.1.3**, which are requirements rather than controls. So when CloudNimbus
records `—` in the Annex A column against `CNB-C-017`, it is not admitting a gap. It is reporting, in the
only way the schema allows, that **Annex A is not the requirements** and that the two frameworks are
organised around different things. The ISMS machinery that discharges clauses 6.1.2 and 6.1.3 exists — it is
in 04.07, where it carries `—` in both mapping columns for the same reason read from the other side.

## 2. CC1 — Control environment

Five criteria: commitment to integrity and ethical values (CC1.1), board independence and oversight
(CC1.2), structures and reporting lines with appropriate authorities (CC1.3), commitment to competence
(CC1.4), and accountability for internal control responsibilities (CC1.5).

Four of these nine controls are owned by Hannah Brill, Head of People, and that concentration is
deliberate: in a 187-person remote-first company the control environment **is** the people process.
Screening, onboarding, training, the Code of Conduct and the disciplinary route are not adjacent to the control
environment; they are the whole of it below the board.

Two controls in this family are worth reading closely. `CNB-C-003` puts the ethics channel outside the
line-management chain, which is what makes it a control rather than an escalation path — a reporting route
that runs through the person a report might concern is not a route. And `CNB-C-005` moves the delegation of
authority matrix from a document to an enforcement point: the approval workflows in the finance,
contracting and access systems refuse a commitment above the approver's recorded limit. A matrix that is
only a matrix is evidence of intent.

## 3. CC2 — Communication and information

Three criteria: obtaining and using relevant quality information (CC2.1), internal communication of
information security objectives and responsibilities (CC2.2), and communication with external parties
(CC2.3).

CC2.3 is the criterion where a service organisation's obligations to its user entities become a control
rather than a marketing page, and CC2.1 is where the *quality of the information* those obligations rest on
becomes one.

**`CNB-C-014` is the obligations-register control, and it is the row A.5.31 belongs on.** A.5.31 requires
the legal, statutory, regulatory and contractual requirements relevant to information security to be
**identified, documented and kept up to date**, and 03.09 recorded the O1 to O12 register as the
implementation. Until this correction the library cited A.5.31 against a control that communicated
commitments and against the insurance cover review, neither of which identifies or maintains anything —
which left 04.09 §2.3's statement that the applicable requirements are "identified and maintained by the
General Counsel" true of a person and false of the control library. `CNB-C-014` now carries the register
itself: the source instrument, the commitment, the accountable individual and the controls that discharge
it, reviewed annually and on change. The security commitments, the availability commitment and the eleven
complementary user entity controls sit inside it as entries, which is where DC6 needs them to be findable
from, because a complementary control the user entity has never been told about is a control nobody
operates.

`CNB-C-013` is the counterpart in the other direction: the trust centre's contents are confirmed quarterly
against the live control set, the vendor register and the status page, so that what CloudNimbus publishes
about itself does not drift from what it does. It is Ana-Sofia Cruz's; `CNB-C-014` moved to Tobias Lund
with the rewrite, because the register is the General Counsel's and a control is owned by the person who
operates it rather than by the person who benefits from it. Neither is a communications activity dressed as
a control: each has a population, a record and a person who signs it.

## 4. CC3 — Risk assessment

Four criteria: objectives specified with sufficient clarity to identify risk (CC3.1), risks identified and
analysed (CC3.2), the potential for fraud considered (CC3.3), and significant change assessed (CC3.4).

**Five of these seven controls are SOC 2-only**, which is the highest proportion in the library and is
explained above. The two that are dual are the two that are about *changes to the world* rather than about
the risk process itself: `CNB-C-021`, the security impact assessment triggered by significant change, which
implements A.5.8 and A.8.27; and `CNB-C-022`, the quarterly review of scope, vendor and personal-data
changes, which implements A.5.9 and A.5.22.

`CNB-C-019` deserves a note because fraud controls are routinely written badly. CC3.3 asks whether the
entity **considers** the potential for fraud in assessing risks — it is a criterion about assessment, not
about anti-fraud mechanisms. A library that answers it with segregation of duties has answered a different
question. `CNB-C-019` is therefore an annual assessment with the Chief Financial Officer, minuted at the
Audit &amp; Risk Committee, covering the three fraud vectors this business actually has: misappropriation,
management override of a control, and fraudulent alteration of calculation-engine output. The third is the
one that is specific to CloudNimbus. In a platform that computes what people are paid, the fraud that
matters is not theft from the company.

## 5. CC4 — Monitoring activities

Two criteria: ongoing and separate evaluations to ascertain whether the components of internal control are
present and functioning (CC4.1), and evaluation and communication of deficiencies (CC4.2).

CC4 is the family a service auditor uses to test whether the rest of the library is real. It is also the
family that will be asked to do the most unglamorous work of the observation window: `CNB-C-023` produces a
weekly exception report of controls overdue against their declared cadence, and `CNB-C-027` requires every
control owner to attest quarterly that the controls they own operated as described.

Those two together are the mechanism by which CloudNimbus expects to find its own problems before anybody
else does. Whether they are enough is not knowable at this vantage, and it is worth saying so: a
self-attestation regime is only as good as the willingness of an owner to record a negative attestation,
and nothing in the design compels that willingness. `CNB-C-024`, the annual independent review commissioned
from a party that neither designed nor operates the controls under review, exists precisely because
self-assessment has a ceiling.

## 6. CC5 — Control activities

Three criteria: selection and development of control activities that mitigate risks to acceptable levels
(CC5.1), selection and development of general controls over technology (CC5.2), and deployment through
policies that establish what is expected and procedures that put those policies into action (CC5.3).

CC5 is the smallest family in the chapter at four controls, and it is the family whose controls are about
the library itself. `CNB-C-028` is the link between Phase 03 and this phase made operational: no risk
treatment item closes until the library control that discharges it exists **with a named owner, a declared
cadence and a declared evidence class**. That is the three-property test from 04.01 enforced as a monthly
reconciliation rather than asserted as a principle.

**`CNB-C-031` is the single control serving CC5.3, and it is the only criterion in this chapter served by
one control.** 04.03 names it as a concentration, sets out the consequence — if that control has a test
exception, CC5.3 has nothing else supporting it — and says what would fix it and why the fix has not been
made. It is repeated here rather than left in the methodology chapter, because a reader consulting the
control table should meet the caveat at the same time as the row.

## 7. Reading this chapter against the COSO principles

One caution to close on. The mapping in Appendix A of TSP section 100 runs between the seventeen COSO
principles and CC1 to CC5, and it is tempting to run the chain one step further: principle → criterion →
control, and declare COSO coverage.

CloudNimbus does not make that claim. **The controls here were designed against the criteria's own text and
against the risks and obligations in Phase 03**, not against the COSO framework, and asserting principle
coverage would be asserting a second crosswalk on top of one that is already CloudNimbus's own assertion.
The COSO relationship is stated because it explains *why these criteria read as they do* — and because a
reader who does not know it will wonder why an information security control library contains a control
about board minutes.
